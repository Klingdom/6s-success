# 2. Company

**Brand:** 6S Success  
**Primary Digital Property:** 6S-success.com  
**Tagline:** Simple systems. Better living.

6S Success applies practical continuous-improvement thinking to ordinary household spaces without requiring customers to understand Lean terminology.

The company helps people make rooms and micro-zones:

- easier to use
- easier to reset
- easier to clean
- easier to maintain
- safer
- calmer
- more aligned with how they actually live

The objective is not merely to make homes look organized.

The objective is to make them **function better**.

---

# 3. Core Customer Problem

Many household organization problems are treated as storage problems.

Often the actual problem is different.

Examples:

- items have no assigned home
- the assigned home is inconvenient
- too much inventory exists
- high-frequency items are hard to access
- low-frequency items consume prime space
- household members disagree on where things belong
- the area has no clear primary function
- the reset process requires too many steps
- supplies are stored away from point of use
- capacity is mismatched to demand
- cleaning is difficult
- replenishment is invisible
- safety hazards are normalized
- the standard is unclear
- nobody owns sustainment

Buying another bin may not solve any of these.

6S Success should diagnose the system before recommending the solution.

# 4. Core Value Proposition

6S Success helps a household answer:

**What should this space do for us?**

Then:

**Why is it not doing that now?**

Then:

**What is the smallest practical change that will make it work better?**

Then:

**How do we keep it that way?**

This produces a customer journey:

**Values**
→ **Desired Function**
→ **Current Friction**
→ **Root Cause**
→ **Micro-Zone**
→ **Quest**
→ **Improvement**
→ **Standard**
→ **Sustainment**

Products and services may support this journey.

They should not replace it.

# 8. Customer Outcome Hierarchy

Prioritize outcomes in this order when possible:

## Level 1: Function

The space performs its intended job.

## Level 2: Friction

The customer can use/reset it with less effort.

## Level 3: Control

# 13. Root Cause Model

Root causes should be standardized enough to support recommendations.

Potential root-cause families:

## Excess

Too many items for available capacity.

## No Home

Item/category has no defined location.

## Wrong Home

Location conflicts with point of use or frequency.

## Poor Access

Required item is difficult to retrieve/return.

## Poor Visibility

Users cannot tell what exists or where it belongs.

## Excess Steps

Reset requires too much effort.

## Unclear Ownership

Nobody knows who maintains the area.

## Capacity Mismatch

Container/storage is too large, small, or poorly shaped.

## No Standard

"Done" is undefined.

## No Replenishment Signal

Consumables run out unexpectedly or are overbought.

## Cleaning Friction

Area cannot be cleaned easily.

## Safety Risk

Placement creates preventable hazard.

This taxonomy should evolve from observed customer behavior.

# 14. 6S Activity Model

The improvement engine uses:

## Sort

Remove what does not support the desired function.

## Straighten

Place what remains where it is easiest to use and return.

## Shine

Clean while identifying conditions that create recurring mess or deterioration.

## Safety

Identify and reduce hazards throughout the process.

## Standardize

Define the simple expected state.

## Sustain

Create signals, routines, ownership, or triggers that maintain the standard.

Do not force a linear six-step experience every time.

A micro-zone may require only one or two targeted activities.

# 49. Product Decision Test

Before creating a product ask:

1. What customer job does it solve?
2. What room/micro-zone does it serve?
3. What desired function does it support?
4. What root cause does it address?
5. Why is the current alternative insufficient?
6. Can the customer understand its value quickly?
7. Is it safe?
8. Can we fulfill/support it?
9. Can we measure whether it works?
10. Does it strengthen the ecosystem?

If answers are weak, do not launch merely to increase SKU count.

---

# 50. Content Decision Test

Before creating content ask:

1. Is there a real customer question?
2. Does existing content already answer it?
3. Can we provide a better answer?
4. Does it map to a room/micro-zone/root cause?
5. Can it produce an actionable outcome?
6. Is there evidence of need?
7. Does it strengthen internal knowledge architecture?

Prefer improving existing content over duplicate publication.

---

# 51. Service Decision Test

Before launching a service ask:

1. Is demand plausible/validated?
2. Can service quality be standardized?
3. Is staffing available?
4. Are supplies/tools defined?
5. Is pricing economically viable?
6. Is scope clear?
7. Are safety/liability issues understood?
8. Can customer outcome be measured?
9. Can service learning improve products?
