Chapter Forty-One

The Primary Bathroom

The primary bathroom is the smallest room you use the most and the first wet room in this book, so cleaning it is not about scrubbing harder, it is about fighting the three problems no other room has all at once, standing moisture, open chemistry, and dates that quietly pass, with a method built for water. This playbook points the whole loop at it one zone at a time, and gives you, for each of the seven zones, the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, so you spray first, dry properly, and read every date.

The primary bathroom is the smallest room in the house and the one you use the most, and it is where this book crosses from the dry rooms into the wet ones. Everything you have cleaned so far in this part fought dust and clutter, problems that sit still and wait for you. This room fights water, which does not wait. It fights standing moisture that turns into mould in the grout and a soft pink film along the bottom of the shower before the week is out. It fights open chemistry, because the cleaners that keep it clean and the medicines that live in it are the most dangerous things in the house to a curious child. And it fights time, because the creams, the cosmetics, and the medicines all carry dates that pass quietly while you sleep, and an expired thing in here is not a spare, it is a hazard.

That is why the bathroom does not clean the way the bedrooms did. In a bedroom you wipe a surface and it is clean. In here, wiping is often the wrong first move, because the enemy is chemistry and water, and both of those work on their own schedule rather than yours. Scrub a dry shower wall hard and you will be there for an hour and it will still film over by Friday. Spray it, walk away, and let the cleaner break the soap film down while you do something else, and it wipes clean in a single pass. The room rewards patience and drying far more than it rewards effort.

So we clean this room by three rules the dry rooms never needed. You spray the wet surfaces first and let the chemistry do the standing about, because chemistry needs time, not scrubbing. You treat drying as the real job rather than wiping, because mould is a water problem and a surface that dries out between uses does not grow it. And you read the date on everything as you go, because in this room a thing that has turned is not stock you are saving, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf. Do that, one zone at a time, and you get back the smallest room you use the most, actually clean and actually safe.

The principleSeven Zones, Not One Room

You do not clean a bathroom. You clear and clean the counter, then the drawers, then the cabinet under the sink, then the medicine cabinet, then the shower, then the toilet, then the linen shelf. Each is a job with a clear finish, and each one you complete makes the smallest room in the house a little safer and a little drier. Face the whole room at once, on your knees with a brush, and you will scrub for an hour and miss the dates and the leak. Start at the counter, because it is the surface you look at twice a day, and clearing it changes what the room looks like from the doorway before you have touched anything else.

The primary bathroom is seven small zones, not one big job: the vanity counter, the vanity drawers, the under-sink cabinet, the medicine cabinet, the shower or tub, the toilet area, and the linen and towel storage. Do them one at a time, and start at the vanity counter, because it is the surface you look at twice a day and clearing it changes what the whole room looks like from the doorway.

There is an order that saves you the most time. Spray the shower and the bowl the moment you walk in, then do the counter, the drawers, and the cabinets while that cleaner sits, and come back to the wet zones once the chemistry has done the hard part for you. Leave the medicine cabinet for a session when you are willing to read the small print on the back of a box.

A finished primary bathroom

A finished primary bathroom is one you would be happy to hand a guest at an hour's notice: a clear counter holding one tray of daily few, a mirror and glass with no film, a shower that dries out between uses with no dark line where the wall meets the tray, a bowl clean under the rim, drawers and cabinets holding only what is in use and in date, the cleaners and medicines locked or high above a child's climbing reach, the plumbing under the sink in plain sight, and every towel on the shelf clean and dry.

The quick passesThe Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

The passes carry their own wet-room flavour here. In Sort, you read the date on everything, because this is the room where Sort is not only about clutter but about expiry, and the separated sunscreen and the crumbling tablet leave with the things you simply do not use. In Straighten, everything in use gets a home that lets it dry and lets you see a leak: one bottle per job, a pale drip tray under the sink, the trap and valves left visible, the daily few on one liftable tray. Safety is sharp in this room, because the cleaners and the medicines are the most dangerous things in the house, and it is woven through every zone below. Standardize is the counts you hold, three towels per person, one product per bowl, four bottles in the caddy, so the room does not silently crowd back up. And Sustain is the drying habit, the squeegee after every shower and the fan left running, which does more to keep this room clean than any amount of weekend scrubbing.

The methodHow to Shine the Primary Bathroom: The Method

Every room in this part of the book keeps the nine rules of Shine you learned at the front door: work top to bottom; dry before wet; work back to front and clean to dirty; mist the cloth, not the surface; use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry; colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener on them; match the cleaner to the surface; give a cleaner its dwell time; and clean to inspect, flagging what you find rather than stopping to fix it. Two of those nine matter more here than anywhere else: dwell time, because chemistry does the work, and colour-coding, because the cloth that cleans the toilet never touches anything else.

On top of them, the bathroom adds its own layer, and it is the layer of the first wet room.

The primary bathroom is the smallest room you use the most, and it is the first wet room in this book, which means it fights three problems no other room faces all at once: standing moisture that breeds mould, open chemistry that has to be kept safe, and dates that quietly pass while you sleep. So the method changes here. You spray the tub, the shower, and the bowl first and let the cleaner do the standing about while you work the dry surfaces, because chemistry needs time, not scrubbing. You treat drying as the real job, not wiping, because mould is a water problem. And you read the date on everything as you go, because in this room an expired thing is not stock, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf.

The first part of that changes the order you work in.

The wet-room surfaces are cleaned with time, not muscle, so the order of work is the opposite of scrubbing. You spray the tub, the shower walls, and the bowl before you touch anything else, and then you leave them: you work the counter, the drawers, and the cabinets while the cleaner does the standing about, breaking down the soap film and the scale and the ring on its own. When you come back, it wipes off in one pass instead of ten. You work top down, you squeegee the glass and the tile last so nothing dries into a film, and you leave the fan running after you walk out.

The second part is the one that actually keeps the room clean between deep cleans.

Mould and the soft pink film are not a dirt problem, they are a water problem, so the winning move in this room is not cleaning, it is drying. A surface that dries out between uses does not grow mould, and a surface that stays wet grows it back faster every week however hard you scrub. So you squeegee the glass, you stand the bath mat on edge to drain rather than leaving it flat and wet, you hang the towels where air actually reaches them, you buff the metal dry so it does not spot, and you run the fan and leave the door open until the room has dried out. The cloth cleans what is there; the drying stops it coming back.

The third part is the one the dry rooms never asked of you.

Every product in this room has a date, and in here a date that has passed is not a supply you are saving, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf: the separated sunscreen, the crumbling tablet, the mascara from two summers ago. So you read the date on every Sort pass, on the creams and the medicines and the cosmetics alike, and what has turned goes, because keeping it is paying storage on a decision that already went wrong. Medicines go to a pharmacy take-back, never down the toilet and never into the bin loose, and the leftover half-course of antibiotics is the single most common thing in the cabinet that should not be there.

Quick Win

Spray the tub and shower walls and the bowl before you touch anything else, then walk away and clean the counter and the toilet outside while the cleaner sits. Chemistry needs time, not scrubbing, so by the time you come back the soap film and the ring wipe off in one pass. Squeegee the glass last and leave the fan running.

The centrepieceZone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the heart of the chapter. For each of the seven zones you get the inputs to gather before you start and the exact order of steps to clean and shine it. Spray the shower and the bowl first, then work the dry zones while they dwell, and come back to the wet ones. Every zone finishes the same way, with a look for the things to flag for the Safety pass.

Zone 1 · The Vanity Counter

This holds the handful of things you touch every morning and every night, and nothing else. Work the whole station top to bottom, mirror and switch plate first, then the tray and its daily few, then the tap and the stone, so every drip lands on something you have not cleaned yet. The base of the faucet and the switch plate are the two spots nobody ever wipes.

Inputs

  • A bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner, and a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for the stone.
  • A streak-free glass and mirror cleaner.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
  • Your portable cleaning caddy.

Steps

  1. Dry-dust the light fixture and bulbs first so grime does not rain down later, then spray glass cleaner onto the cloth, not the mirror, wipe top to bottom in overlapping strokes, and buff dry so no film shows under the bulbs.
  2. Mist a cloth with the neutral pH cleaner and wipe the switch plate and its screw heads where fingertips leave a grey bloom, keeping the cloth damp not wet, then wipe the backsplash down to the counter and give the caulk seam a firm pass.
  3. Lift the whole tray off, wipe the counter under it, then wipe the tray and the base of the soap dish and toothbrush holder where a soft pink film sets, standing each item back only once dry.
  4. Work the soap-film cleaner into a detail brush and scrub the faucet collar where the tap meets the stone, the dried toothpaste, and the scale around the handles, let it dwell a minute, then wipe and buff the metal dry so it does not spot.
  5. Wet the basin, apply the soap-film cleaner, and work it around the drain flange and into the overflow slot with the brush, then rinse the bowl and wipe it dry so no ring hardens at the water line.
  6. Wipe the full run of stone on both sides of the basin with the neutral pH cleaner, which will not etch the finish the way an acidic spray would, follow it into the wall seam and out to the front edge, and dry it off rather than leaving water marks.
  7. Run a damp cloth along the front lip and underside where hands grip, and down to the toe-kick and floor seam where hair and dust gather.

Inspect and flag as you go: a caulk line at the backsplash or basin going black or lifting from the stone, which is water getting in behind; dark spots creeping in from the edges of the mirror silvering; water pooling or a chalky ring under the faucet base that keeps returning; a hairline crack in the basin, or a switch plate cracked, warm, or loose; rust freckling the tap fixings, or a towel ring wobbling in its screws. Flag each.

Zone 2 · The Vanity Drawers

These hold the grooming tools and products you actually reach for, arranged by when in the day you use them. The dirt in these drawers hides underneath, not on top, so lift the dividers right out, tip the drawer over a bin, and clean the parts you never see before you refill by day-order.

Inputs

  • A compact vacuum with the crevice tool.
  • A bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner, and a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
  • A small detail brush set, and a colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
  • A drawer divider set and a drawer safety latch if children are in the house, and your caddy.

Steps

  1. Wipe the outer drawer face and the pull with a neutral pH damp cloth, then run a fingernail behind the cloth along the top edge where a thumb pushes it shut and a dark line builds.
  2. Take every divider and insert tray out and wash them in warm water with the soap-film cleaner to shift the powder and product residue, drying each one fully before it returns.
  3. Tip the loose powder, hair, and grit from the empty drawer bottom into the bin, wipe the bare bottom, and reach into the back corners where the fine stuff settles.
  4. Vacuum the runner tracks and the empty cavity with the crevice tool, wipe both glides with a barely damp cloth, and check the drawer slides flush as you seat it back.
  5. Wipe combs, brush handles, and the outside of the razor box with the soap-film cleaner, and use the detail brush on the teeth of combs and the crust around bottle caps before anything returns.
  6. If heat tools are stored here, unplug first, wipe the barrel and body with a dry cloth only keeping moisture away from the plug and vents, then wind the cord loosely and lay the tool in its own section rather than coiled tight against a divider, capping loose razor blades in a lidded box in one corner.

Inspect and flag as you go: a drawer bottom swelled or lifted at a corner, meaning a product has leaked under the divider; rust on a razor spring, a blade edge, or a hinge inside the box, the mark of damp shut in; a hairline split in a plastic divider that will shed sharp flakes; a runner that grinds or sticks, or a mounting screw backed out; soft black mould in a back corner where wet hair and cap drips collect. Flag each.

Zone 3 · The Under-Sink Cabinet

This stores cleaning supplies and backstock around the plumbing without ever hiding a leak. It is the one cabinet you clean partly to read the plumbing, so empty it, wipe the floor board, and look hard at what the pipes have been doing while the doors were shut.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner.
  • A small detail brush set, and a colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
  • A compact vacuum with the crevice tool.
  • A pale leak tray, a chemical storage bin, and pull-out bins, and your caddy.

Steps

  1. Wipe the P-trap and each pipe joint with a dry cloth, then run a clean dry finger around the underside of every connection, since a dry finger tells you about a seep before a puddle ever will.
  2. Wipe the shut-off valve bodies and stems with a barely damp cloth, then take the detail brush to the threads and handles where grime cakes, so each valve stays easy to read at a glance.
  3. Slide the pull-out bins right out, tip any grit, wash them in the sink with the soap-film cleaner, and dry them before they slide back clear of the trap.
  4. Lift the pale drip tray out and rinse it, and wipe the neck and cap of every bottle before it returns so the tray stays pale and shows a fresh drip.
  5. Wipe the bare floor board and the back and side walls with a neutral pH cloth, working into the corners and along the wall behind the trap where damp gathers first.
  6. Wipe the inside of both doors and the hinges, clear any splash line along the bottom door edge with the detail brush, and set the chemistry back with one open bottle per job and the trap and both shut-off valves in plain sight when the doors swing open.

Inspect and flag as you go: a dark ring or a soft, spongy patch in the floor board, a slow leak showing itself before it shows as water; a green or white crust on a pipe joint or valve stem, the fingerprint of a mineral seep; a drip tray or bin base warped or cupped from pooling liquid; rust bleeding from a can base or a bracket, and mould in the back corner; a valve that will not turn, or a supply line stiff and cracked where it bends. Flag each.

Zone 4 · The Medicine Cabinet

This holds the medicine and first aid you would need tonight, and nothing you stopped needing years ago. Take the shelves out, wash off the sticky rings, and dry everything through before it goes back, because damp is what turns tablets chalky in this room. Clean it in a light bright enough to read every label as you go.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner.
  • A small detail brush set, and a colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
  • Labelled medicine bins, a lockable medicine box, and a catch if children are in the house.
  • Your portable cleaning caddy.

Steps

  1. Wipe the outer mirror with glass cleaner sprayed onto the cloth and buff it dry, then do the inside face where toothpaste mist and steam settle into a haze.
  2. Wipe the hinges and the magnetic catch with a barely damp cloth and lift grime out of the hinge knuckle with the detail brush so the door still shuts cleanly.
  3. Take each shelf out and wash off the sticky rings from cough syrup and old blister foil in warm water, drying each one all the way through before it returns.
  4. Work the detail brush around the shelf clips and into the pin holes, then wipe the back and side walls, reaching the top corner where dust and damp meet.
  5. Empty the pain relief, stomach and cold, and first aid bins, wipe each out, and dry them before the in-date items go back grouped as they were, reading every date as you go.
  6. Wipe the base of every bottle and tin and the outside of the first aid box so nothing carries its own sticky ring back onto a clean shelf, and set the whole cabinet above a child's reach with the catch on, keeping the first aid bin small enough to carry one-handed.

Inspect and flag as you go: rust freckling the metal shelf clips, hinges, or a steel back, the tell that humidity is sitting in here; black mildew in a corner seam or along a shelf underside; condensation or a cloudy bloom trapped behind the mirror; a shelf bowed, or a clip pulling loose under weight; a hairline crack in a glass shelf, or a door catch that no longer holds. Flag each.

Zone 5 · The Shower or Tub

This keeps only the products actually in use within reach, on surfaces that dry out between showers. Spray the walls and tray and let the cleaner do the standing about while you clean the rest of the room, then come back, work top down, squeegee the glass, and leave the door open so it dries out. Start the mould early, because it needs the dwell time.

Inputs

  • A bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner for the tray and mat.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
  • A shower squeegee, a shower caddy, and a non-slip mat or grip strips.
  • Your portable cleaning caddy.

Steps

  1. Spray the whole wall surface and the screen with the soap-film cleaner and the caulk seam and grout lines too, and leave it all to work while you step out and clean the dry zones.
  2. Wipe the showerhead and arm, and where scale has closed the holes work the soap-film cleaner in with the detail brush, let it dwell, then run the water hot to clear the jets.
  3. Return and wipe the walls, tile, and screen from the top down, rinse, then squeegee the glass and tile so nothing dries into a film; a fabric liner goes through the washing machine instead.
  4. Scrub the taps, valve trim, and diverter with the detail brush to lift limescale from the crevices, rinse, and buff the metal dry.
  5. Lift the caddy off, rinse the shelves, and wipe the base of every bottle and the bar dish where a soft pink film and gum collect underneath, keeping only one shampoo, one conditioner, one wash, and one bar per person.
  6. Work the treated caulk seam and grout lines with the detail brush after their dwell and rinse thoroughly, then lift the non-slip mat, wash both faces and the tray floor under it with the floor cleaner, rinse, and stand the mat on edge to drain rather than leaving it flat and wet.
  7. Lift the drain cover, clear the hair caught below it, wipe the cover, then rinse the squeegee blade and hang it back on its hook inside the enclosure to dry.

Inspect and flag as you go: soft black mould in the caulk seam, or a pink film returning along the bottom band faster each time, a sign moisture is not clearing; a grout line gone crumbly or missing, or a tile that sounds hollow when tapped; a grab bar, towel bar, or caddy fixing that gives when you lean on it; a drain running slower each week; rust weeping from a screw or frame, and a screen seal hardened and split. Flag each.

Zone 6 · The Toilet Area

This keeps the smallest and dirtiest patch of the room clean and stocked without letting it contaminate anything else. Treat the bowl and let it sit while you do everything else, then work the outside in strict order from cleanest to dirtiest: handle, lid, seat top, seat underside, rim, pedestal, floor. One cloth, one colour, this job only.

Inputs

  • A bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner, and a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, with one colour kept for this zone only.
  • A small detail brush set.
  • A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.

Steps

  1. Apply the bowl cleaner under the rim and let it dwell while you do everything else in this zone, keeping this the only product in the bowl and never mixing it with another.
  2. Wipe the flush handle or button first, while your cloth is at its cleanest, since it is touched by every hand and rarely wiped.
  3. Wipe the bare cistern lid and tank sides, working around the fixings and down the back where dust cakes against the wall.
  4. Wipe the lid top, then lift it and do the underside where splash dries unseen, then the seat top, the underside, and the ring, flipping open the hinge caps to clear the grime beneath them with the detail brush.
  5. After its dwell, work the bowl cleaner under the rim and around the water line with the brush, scrub down into the trap, and flush, keeping the room ventilated.
  6. Wipe the pedestal from the tank down to the floor, then clean the floor around the base and the strip behind the pedestal, where drips and dust collect and where the smell in a bathroom actually starts.
  7. Rinse the brush under the flush, stand it to drip, tip and wipe out the holder so no liquid pools, and wipe the bin lid and replace the liner.

Inspect and flag as you go: a damp ring or discoloured grout returning at the base of the pedestal, a sign the seal below is failing; a hairline crack in the tank or bowl porcelain, or a weep from the tank fittings; condensation sweating down the tank onto the floor into a slow damp patch; rust on the seat hinge bolts, or a seat that rocks; heavy scale rebuilding under the rim, or a stain trail on the floor behind that points to a slow overflow. Flag each.

Zone 7 · The Linen and Towel Storage

This holds enough clean towels for the way this house actually does laundry, and no more than the shelf can safely carry. Clear the shelves, wipe them, and press the back wall for damp, because a towel put away not quite dry scents the whole cupboard within days, and anything sour goes back through a hot wash before it returns.

Inputs

  • A compact vacuum with the attachments.
  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
  • A moisture absorber or humidity monitor if the cupboard runs damp, and your caddy.

Steps

  1. Dust the very top of the unit where the room's dust settles, then clear and wipe the top shelf before working down.
  2. Take each shelf empty one at a time, vacuum the crumbs and lint from the corners and front lip, wipe with a neutral pH cloth, and dry the board fully before anything goes back.
  3. Wipe the back wall and press the flat of your hand along it while the shelves are bare, since damp there is what the towels have been drinking.
  4. Wipe the shelf brackets and clear dust from the fixings with the detail brush, noticing whether each still carries its load without movement.
  5. Wipe the inside of the door and its edges, and run the detail brush through any louvre or vent slots so air keeps moving through the cupboard.
  6. Vacuum the cupboard floor and inner skirting and wipe it, where fibre dust and stray damp gather low down.
  7. As you restack, smell each folded set, and a musty note means it went away damp, so send it back through a hot wash with no softener before it returns, keeping three bath towels per person and the heavy ones on the low shelves.

Inspect and flag as you go: a damp or discoloured patch on the back wall, meaning moisture is coming through from behind; a bracket pulling away, or a fixing plug backing out under a deep linen stack; a shelf board bowed in the middle from more load than it was fixed for; mildew on a shelf underside, and a musty smell that lingers after the towels come out; a soft or crumbling board edge low down, the quiet damp that draws silverfish. Flag each.

The hard callsThe Calls That Matter Here

This room's hard calls are all about the gap between what you keep and what you actually use, sharpened by water and by dates. The tool that lives out is the first: the straightener or dryer sits on the counter because the outlet is right there, but the counter holds only what you touch every single day, so a three-times-a-week tool becomes a drawer tool with a heat-safe pouch. The expensive shade you never wear is the same call with makeup: if it is open and past its window, or you have worn it twice in a year, keeping it is paying storage on a decision that already went wrong, and the money is gone either way. And the bottle you keep out of guilt in the shower is the same again: a product is in use only if you touched it this week, the caddy holds four not eight, and the rest are why it never dries out.

The other calls are about safety and the slow damage water does. The pipe you cannot see is the one you buried behind a tower of paper towels, and the arithmetic is simple: a joint that weeps unnoticed for months turns a cheap washer into a floor that has to come up, so the trap and both shut-off valves stay visible. The leftover prescription, the half-course of antibiotics or the strong painkillers from a procedure two years ago, is the single most common thing in the cabinet that should not be there, because a medicine belongs to a condition someone in this house has now, and the rest goes to a pharmacy take-back. What you keep within splash range settles with one line, that nothing which touches your face, your mouth, or your food goes within arm's reach of the flush. And the good towels you never use are a shelf running backwards, so you rank every towel, use the top of the ranking daily, and let the worn grey ones go.

The Expired-Is-Not-Stock Challenge

Read the date on every product on every Sort pass, the creams, the medicines, and the cosmetics alike. An expired thing is not a supply you are saving, it is a failure already on your shelf, so let it go, and take medicines to a pharmacy take-back rather than the toilet or the loose bin. The leftover half-course of antibiotics goes first.

The hazardsWatch for in the Primary Bathroom

The Shine pass through this room will have flagged most of what needs fixing. Here is the safety pass, framed as the five questions you ask in every room, tuned to the one room where chemistry, water, and electricity share a few square feet.

Ask, first, about poison, choke, and strangle, because this room and the nursery are where those hazards concentrate. Bleach, drain opener, and bowl cleaner sitting at a child's eye level behind an unlatched door go up above the sink line or behind a catch, and you never let bowl cleaner meet bleach in the same bowl, because together they make a gas. Loose tablets and pushed-through blister packs go up high, and you measure the height a child can reach by what they can climb, the closed toilet lid included, not by the floor. Ask about water and electricity, because a plugged-in heat tool within cord reach of a full basin, a charger run up to the cistern top, and a trap drip creeping toward a socket are each a shock waiting to happen, and nothing stays plugged in while there is water standing in the bowl. Ask where someone could fall: on soap film on wet glaze at the moment they step over a high tub wall, which is why the mat truly grips and a grab bar goes in if anyone is unsteady, and off a step stool on a wet floor reaching a top shelf, which is why the everyday linens come down to shelves you reach flat-footed. Ask where something could fall, cut, or crush: a razor on the tub edge at a child's grab height, bare blade refills loose among the brushes, and a glass bottle or heavy tin on a top shelf above a hard basin. And ask about burn or fire, giving any hot iron or heat tool a metal or ceramic rest it always returns to, in the same spot every time.

This is the room where cleaning products, medicines, water, and electricity all share a few square feet, so the safety rules are firm. You keep one product per bowl and you never mix them, because bowl cleaner and bleach together make a gas, and one cleaner does the job alone. You store the bleach, the drain opener, and every medicine locked or high above a child's climbing reach, measured from the closed toilet lid they can stand on rather than from the floor. Nothing stays plugged in while there is water standing in the basin, and no charger runs up to the cistern top. And you keep the plumbing under the sink in plain sight, because a joint that weeps unseen for months turns a cheap washer into a floor that has to come up.

Common Mistake

Scrubbing a dry surface hard instead of spraying it and giving the cleaner time, wiping the mould off again and again instead of drying the surface so it cannot return, keeping expired creams and medicines as if they were stock, and storing the bleach and the pills at a height a child reaches by standing on the closed toilet lid.

The ritualBefore and After

The friction meter that carried you through the first eight parts of this book reached its goal a while ago, at the end of the main arc. In its place, every room in this part gets a quieter record: a photograph of the room before you start and a photograph of it finished, the pair taped inside a cabinet as the standard the room resets to.

Stand in the doorway. In one frame, capture the counter, the mirror, and the shower. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, so you keep an honest record of what the room really held. Then clean it, zone by zone, and take the after from the same spot in the same light.

This is the bathroom the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, the counter clear, the glass and mirror filmless, the shower drying out with no dark seam, the bowl clean under the rim, and the chemistry and medicines locked safely up out of reach. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard, because a bathroom you could hand a guest at an hour's notice is the one that stays good, and the photo is what it has to match after every reset.

One Idea to Keep

A bathroom is not clean because you scrubbed it hard. It is clean because you sprayed first and let the chemistry work, dried the surfaces so the mould cannot come back, read the date on everything and let the failures go, and locked the chemistry safely away. Do one zone this weekend, the counter or the shower, and give yourself the smallest room you use the most, actually clean and actually safe.
