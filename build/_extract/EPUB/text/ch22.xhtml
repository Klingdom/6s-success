Chapter Twenty-Two

Improve Overall Safety

Finding and removing hazards makes a home safer today. But a home does not hold still, and some harm cannot be prevented at all, only prepared for. This chapter completes the fourth S by adding what removing cannot: the moves that keep new dangers from taking hold, and the protections that stand ready for the day something goes wrong anyway. See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it, and the home is safe.

You can now do two things you could not do when this part began. You can look at your home and see the hazards in it, the ones hiding in plain sight, because you have the lens. And you can remove them, thoroughly, room by room, because you have the hunt. Between them, those two skills have made your home meaningfully safer than it was, and if you stopped here you would have done real good.

But there is a reason this part has one more chapter, and it is a pair of honest facts about homes. The first is that a home does not hold still. The hunt you ran last chapter made the home safe as of that day, and then the day ended, and things began, quietly, to flow back. A new appliance arrives with a new cord. A child grows two inches and reaches a shelf that was safe last month. Batteries die. The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks. The second fact is harder and gentler: some harm cannot be removed in advance at all. You can clear every hazard you can find and a fire can still start, a person can still fall, and no amount of hunting will have prevented it. Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach.

So this chapter completes the fourth S by adding the two moves that a hazard hunt cannot make. The first is to keep new dangers from taking hold, so the home does not quietly undo the work you did. The second is to be ready for the harm you cannot prevent, so that on the worst day the home is on your side. See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it. With the last two, Safety is whole, and the home is not just cleared of today's dangers but genuinely, durably safe.

The reframeFrom Removing to Improving

Everything so far in this part has been about subtraction: find a danger, take it out. That is the right place to start, and it is not the place to stop, because subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever.

Improving overall safety

Improving overall safety is the work beyond removing today's hazards: preventing new ones from taking hold, and preparing for the harm you cannot prevent. Removing hazards makes a home safe now. Prevention and preparation are what keep it safe.

You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet.

Think of the difference between bailing a boat and fixing the leak. Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming. Improving overall safety is the other kind of work, the kind that changes the boat itself so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the home's baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less, because you are no longer depending on catching every single hazard by hand, one at a time, forever. That is a good thing, because catching every hazard by hand, forever, is exactly the kind of vigilance the last chapter warned does not scale.

Raising the baseline has two halves, and the rest of this chapter is those two halves. One looks forward at the hazards that have not arrived yet and keeps them from taking hold. The other looks at the harm that no prevention can stop and gets the home ready to meet it. Prevention and preparation. Together they are what turns a home that has been made safe into a home that stays safe.

The first movePrevent: Keep New Hazards From Taking Hold

Start with the leaks. A hunted home drifts back toward hazard not because anyone is careless but because ordinary life brings new things in, and some of them are dangerous, and the dangerous ones do not announce themselves any more than the last batch did.

A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way.

That last phrase is the whole of prevention, and it is worth taking seriously, because it is the opposite of willpower. You will not keep your home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater; you proved that to yourself back in Straighten. What works is design. You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing.

Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in.

In practice that is three small habits. The first is to choose the safer default when something new comes into the house, at the one moment it is easiest to do, which is the moment it arrives. The heater you buy is the one that switches itself off if it tips; the cleaner you replace runs out into a cupboard that already has a latch. You are not adding a chore; you are just steering the ordinary decision, once, toward safe. The second is to give the dangerous things a home, the same move you learned in Straighten, now aimed at safety: the medicines live in one latched box and go back into it every time, the knives live in the block, the button batteries live up high, so that "away" and "safe" are the same place and putting a thing away is what makes it safe. The third is to run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, not as a project but as a habit, a slow walk with the five questions every so often, so the drift is caught while it is small. Nothing here is a schedule to be written down and enforced; that kind of documented standard is a later S. This is just the safe way, made easy enough that it happens on its own.

The second movePrepare: Ready for What You Cannot Prevent

Now the harder, gentler half, the one that is not about hazards at all. However well you prevent, some harm will always sit outside your reach. A fire can start in a wall you cannot see. Carbon monoxide has no smell and gives no warning your body can catch. A person can fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor. You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps. What you can do is prepare, so that on the day one of them comes, the home meets it ready.

Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes.

Preparation, like prevention, is a short and concrete list, not a state of dread.

Put the protections in place for the harm you cannot prevent: working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows.

The center of it is the alarm, because it is the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious. A working smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where there is anything that burns fuel, on every floor and near the bedrooms, is the single highest-value thing in this whole chapter, and its whole value depends on one unglamorous fact: it has to actually work, which means it has to be tested and its battery kept alive, or it is a plastic disc that does nothing. Beyond the alarm, the list is calm and finite. A fire extinguisher within reach of the kitchen, where most home fires begin, and a rough sense of how to use it. A way out of every room, ideally two, and an exit plan the household has actually walked, from the bedrooms to a single meeting spot outside, so that in the dark and the noise nobody has to invent a plan. And the few plain numbers and steps everyone should know, including the children, so that the knowledge does not live only in one head. That is the whole of it. Not a bunker. A prepared home.

Quick Win

Test every smoke and carbon-monoxide alarm in the home right now, one press each, and note any floor that has none. Working alarms are the highest-value protection in the house, and the only one that has to keep working while you are asleep.

6S Tip

Keep the tools of preparation in one known place, a single shelf or box: the spare alarm batteries, the fire extinguisher, a flashlight, the first-aid kit. Preparation you cannot find in the dark is not preparation.

All four movesThe Whole of Safety

Step back now and look at the fourth S whole, because you have just finished it, and it turns out to have been four moves all along, each the answer to a question the one before it could not ask.

You learned to see the hazard, by changing the question you asked the home from "is it tidy?" to "could this hurt someone?". You learned to remove it, thoroughly, room by room, fixing each danger the moment you found it. You have now learned to prevent the next one, by making the safe way the easy way so the home stops drifting back. And you have learned to prepare for the harm that none of that can stop, so the home is ready on its worst day. See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it. That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down.

And notice what it is not. It is not the exhausting vigilance the last chapter warned against, the impossible job of watching everything forever. Each of the four moves is the opposite of vigilance: it is a piece of work you do deliberately, once or now and then, precisely so that you can stop watching. The professionals give the goal a stark name, zero accidents and injuries, and it can sound cold, but read it as what it is, a direction rather than a destination you will ever tick off. You will not achieve a mathematically perfect home. You are simply always turned that way, and being turned that way, with these four moves in hand, is what safety consciousness actually is: not fear held at a high pitch, but a steady, low, permanent habit of care that mostly runs quietly in the background of an ordinary, happy home.

Family Challenge

Walk the exit plan together, calmly, once. From each bedroom, how do you get out, and where does everyone meet outside? Children who have walked it once remember it, and walking it turns a frightening idea into a known, ordinary thing.

Finding hazards makes a home safer once. Preventing them and preparing for them keep it safe for good.

The step onto this mark was small, and that is honest, and it does not diminish the milestone. It was small because the big daily friction was cleared long ago, in Sort and Straighten and Shine, and what these three safety chapters added was a different kind of gain, the removal not of everyday friction but of the friction of the worst day, the harm kept from happening and the emergency the home is now ready to meet. In closingSafety, Complete

That is Part Six, and it is the fourth S, done. It is worth marking, because it was a turn in the book: for the first nineteen chapters you worked on the state of the room, and in these three you turned to the people in it, which was always the more important thing.

You did it in three moves that added up to four. In Chapter 20 you learned to see, trading the question "is it tidy?" for "could this hurt someone?", and you did a first pass on the obvious dangers. In Chapter 21 you learned to remove, carrying the five questions through the whole home and fixing each hazard the moment you found it. And in this chapter you learned to keep the home safe, preventing new hazards from taking hold and preparing for the harm you cannot prevent. A home that is cleared, arranged, clean, and now safe is a genuinely good home to live in, and you have built one.

What is left is not more building; it is holding. Everything you have made across these twenty-two chapters lives, for now, largely in your head and your hands: it works because you know how it works and you keep it going. The last two S's are about changing that, so the home holds its shape without leaning on any one person, and keeps holding it over years. That work begins in the next chapter. For now, the fourth S is complete, and your home is safe. Take the win.

Common Mistake

Fitting a smoke alarm, feeling safe, and never testing it again, so the one protection you were counting on is silent the night it matters. A protection you do not keep alive is only decoration. Give the button a press whenever the clocks change.

One Idea to Keep

Safety is four moves, and you now have them all: see the hazard, remove it, prevent the next one, and prepare for the harm you cannot stop. A safe home is not one where nothing can ever go wrong. It is one that is ready when it does.
