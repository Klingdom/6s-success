Floor first, then one zone at a time

How to organize a garage full of stuff, step by step

Organize a full garage in two moves: first clear the floor, so you can walk and park again, then work through the garage one zone at a time, the overhead bins, the sports gear, the tools, the garden things and the car supplies. The whole garage is about seven to ten hours of hands-on work, which is a weekend or several evenings, not an afternoon. The floor alone changes how it feels.

The short answer. Pick a dry day and mark out three areas on the driveway: keep, leaves the house, and trash. Clear the floor first, then empty and sort one zone at a time, never the whole garage at once. Put things back by how often they are used: the everyday things nearest the door, the rarely used things high up, and heavy things low. Get everything off the floor and onto the walls. Old chemicals, fuel, oil-based paint and batteries usually go to a hazardous waste drop-off, not the household trash.

Before you start: a dry day and three areas

A garage is the one room you can empty outdoors, which makes it easier than it looks. Pick a dry day, or a run of evenings with a dry forecast, and set up three areas on the driveway with a tarp or chalk:

  • Keep. Things that go back into the garage. This area should end up the smallest.
  • Leaves the house. Things to donate, sell or pass on. Decide where it goes before you start, and book the drop-off, or it will move back in.
  • Trash and recycling. Broken things, empty boxes, things nobody will fix.

Add one more box, clearly labeled, for hazardous things: old paint, solvents, garden chemicals, motor oil, fuel and old batteries. Most of these go to a household hazardous waste drop-off rather than the trash bin, and some places accept fully dried latex paint with the trash, so check your city or county rules for what goes where. Keep that box away from children and pets, in the shade, and do not combine products from different containers.

Wear work gloves and closed shoes, and get a second person for anything heavy or anything coming down from overhead.

Step 1: Clear the floor

Start with whatever is on the floor, because it is the biggest visible change and it gives you room to work. Carry each thing out and put it in exactly one area: keep, leaves or trash. Boxes you have not opened since you moved in are usually the easiest calls in the garage; open one, and if nothing in it has been missed, the rest of the box can go.

Aim for the floor of the parking bay, or a clear walkway to the door, before anything else. Sweep it once it is empty. If you only have an afternoon, stop here: a garage with a clear floor already works better, and every later step is easier with room to stand.

Do not put anything back yet. Things only return once their zone has been sorted, or the floor fills up again by the evening.

Step 2: The overhead and bulk storage

Bring the bins down to the floor before you judge them, because nobody sorts honestly from a ladder with one arm through a lid. Start with the box marked "misc" and the holiday bin that has not come down in three years. What you keep goes back labeled in letters you can read from the floor, with the heaviest bins on the lowest rack and nothing hanging over where the car parks.

Write the month and year on the end of each bin when you close it. A bin you next open in two years with nothing missed is a bin you can let go of, and the overhead storage zone covers the whole method. It is about an hour to an hour and a half.

Step 3: The sports equipment and outdoor gear

Carry every ball, helmet, racket and pair of cleats out to the driveway and match each one to a person who still plays. That is the slow part, and it is the whole decision: gear for a sport nobody plays this year leaves, and so does a helmet for a head that has outgrown it. Helmets are also the one item worth replacing rather than keeping if they have taken a hard knock.

Bikes go on wall hooks nearest the door, with bare floor underneath, and each sport gets one labeled bin, with this season's at the front. The sports and recreation zone is about an hour to an hour and a half.

Step 4: The tools, the batteries and the workbench

Tools are where garages collect duplicates. Pull every hammer, screwdriver, wrench and pair of pliers onto one sheet on the floor, and keep one good one of each that you actually use; the spares can go to someone setting up their first toolbox. Hang what you reach for weekly at chest height nearest the door, and sheath saw blades before they go back. The hand tool wall has the full standard.

For power tools, test every battery pack in its own tool before you decide, because a pack that no longer holds a charge is the most common thing in the drawer. Keep packs and chargers together by brand, one shelf per battery system, and do not leave packs sitting on chargers. A swollen, cracked or damaged lithium battery should not go in the household trash; take it to a battery recycling point. The power tool and battery zone covers it.

Then clear the workbench down to the bare top. It is usually buried under everything else in the garage that has no home, so most of what is on it belongs to another step. What stays is the vise, the light and one tray for the job in progress. The workbench zone is about an hour to an hour and a half. If the bench, the power tools, the fasteners and a material rack take up a real share of the garage, how to organize a workshop walks through that half of the room on its own, safety station first.

Step 5: The garden tools, chemicals and outdoor shed

Out go the rake with three tines left, the hose with the split you have meant to patch for two summers, and the cracked pots kept for seedlings you never started. Most garages hold four spades and dig with one. Long-handled tools hang head-up on a rail rather than leaning in a corner, and the hose goes on a reel by the door it goes out of.

Garden chemicals are the step where tidy and safe part ways. Keep them in their original labeled containers, never decanted into drink bottles, on one shelf behind a latch and above the reach of children, away from food and the cooler. Anything without a readable label goes in the hazardous box. The lawn and garden tool zone takes about an hour to an hour and a half, much of it on the chemical shelf.

Step 6: The car maintenance supplies and cleaning kit

Line every bottle up and read the label. Keep only the fluids for cars you still own, standing upright on a tray with the opened date written on them; a bottle for a car you sold three years ago is a decision that has been waiting since then. Jumper cables and the tire gauge go together in one bag by the door, and used rags go in a closed metal can rather than a pile. Rags with oil-based stain or linseed oil on them can heat up and catch fire on their own, so those get laid out flat outdoors to dry before they go anywhere. The automotive care zone is about forty-five to seventy-five minutes.

Step 7: Put it back by how often it is used

Now the keep area goes back in, zone by zone, using three rules. The things you use every week go nearest the door you use. The things you use once or twice a year go highest. Heavy things go low. Get as much as possible off the floor and onto the walls, hooks and shelves, because a garage floor with things on it is a floor that collects more.

Resist buying storage until this step, and then only for what you are actually keeping. A garage that was full before you started is almost always too much stuff rather than too little shelving, which is the whole argument of why more storage won't fix clutter. Measure what is left, then buy the racks to fit it.

Finish by taking the leave pile to its drop-off the same day, or the next, and the hazardous box to its collection point. A garage that is clear because its contents moved to the driveway has not been organized.

Your garage checklist

  • A dry day, three areas on the driveway, gloves, a second person for heavy things
  • A labeled box for paint, chemicals, fuel, oil and old batteries, and your local drop-off rules checked
  • The floor cleared and swept, or at least the parking bay and a path to the door
  • Overhead bins brought down, sorted, labeled, dated, heavy ones lowest
  • Sports gear matched to someone who still plays, bikes on hooks
  • One of each hand tool you use, battery packs tested, the bench top bare
  • Long-handled tools on a rail, chemicals latched and labeled
  • Car fluids only for cars you own, rags in a closed metal can
  • Everything back by frequency: daily near the door, yearly up high, heavy low
  • The leave pile and the hazardous box gone the same week

A very messy garage, or a garage you cannot get into

If the garage is so full you cannot walk in, start at the door and work inward in timed rounds of about twenty minutes, with a break between, and stop when you are tired, because decisions made exhausted are the ones you undo. Clear a path first, then a patch of floor to sort on, then keep going. Doing it in several evenings is fine; doing it in the same order is what matters.

If the hard part is not the space but the deciding, especially the "might need it someday" things, give each one a decide-by date instead of deciding today, as in how long to keep a maybe. And if most of what fills the garage is spares of things you already own, is that really a backup? is the question to ask of each one.

Keeping it that way

A garage gets full again for one reason more than any other: it is where things go when nobody has decided where they belong. Give the garage a rule that things only come in with a place to go, and do a ten-minute floor check once a week, putting back anything that has landed. Twice a year, at the change of seasons, swap the front-of-shelf gear and open the bins that should have come down. If a spot keeps filling up anyway, the reason is usually covered in why is my house always messy.

Common questions

How do I organize a garage full of stuff?

Pick a dry day and set up three areas on the driveway: keep, leaves the house, and trash. Clear the floor first, then empty and sort one zone at a time, the overhead bins, the sports gear, the tools, the garden things and the car supplies. Put back only what you keep, by how often it is used: everyday things by the door, rarely used things up high, heavy things low.

Where do I start organizing a messy garage?

Start with the floor. Carry out whatever is on it and sort each thing into keep, leaves or trash, aiming for a clear parking bay or a path to the door. It is the biggest visible change, and every later step is easier once you have room to stand and sort.

How long does it take to organize a garage?

The whole garage is about seven to ten hours of hands-on work across its seven zones, so plan a weekend or several evenings. Clearing the floor can be done in an afternoon on its own, and each zone after it is roughly an hour to an hour and a half.

How do I declutter a garage quickly?

If you only have a few hours, clear the floor and stop. Sort everything on it into keep, leaves and trash, sweep, and take the leave pile and trash away the same day. A garage with a clear floor works better immediately, and the zones can follow one at a time.

What do I do with old paint and chemicals from the garage?

Never pour them down a drain. Keep them in their original containers in one labeled box, out of reach of children and pets, and take them to a household hazardous waste drop-off. Some places accept fully dried latex paint with the trash; your city or county will say what goes where and when.

How do I organize tools in a garage?

Gather every hand tool onto one sheet, keep one good one of each that you use, and hang the weekly ones at chest height nearest the door. Test each power tool battery in its tool before keeping it, and keep packs and chargers together by brand, one shelf per battery system.

How do I keep my garage organized?

Only let things into the garage when they have a place to go, and do a ten-minute floor check once a week. Twice a year, at the change of seasons, swap the gear at the front of the shelves and open the bins that should have come down.
