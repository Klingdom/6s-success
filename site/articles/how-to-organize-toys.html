Living room, bedroom and the bins in between

How to organize toys so a child can put them away

Organize toys so the child, not an adult, can put them away. Keep fewer out at once, sort them into families, give each family one open bin with no lid on a low shelf, and tape a photo of what goes inside to the front. Put the rest in a dated rotation box out of reach, and swap a few toys every couple of weeks.

The short answer. The problem is rarely the storage. It is usually too many toys out at once, in containers a child cannot open or read. Tip everything out, keep what gets played with, rotate the rest, and use open bins at the child's height with picture labels. If a three-year-old can put it away without asking, it is organized.

Why toys take over

Toys arrive faster than any other thing in a house. Birthdays, holidays, party bags, grandparents and hand-me-downs all add, and almost nothing leaves on its own, because a toy that is no longer played with looks exactly like one that is. So the count climbs, and with it the clutter, and the tidy-up gets longer every week.

Then the containers make it worse. A deep toy box with a lid, a bin on a high shelf, a basket labeled in words a child cannot read: each one means the child has to ask for help to put a toy away, so they do not. That is not a discipline problem. It is a home that is hard to use for the person who uses it most.

And toys travel. They are played with in the living room, the kitchen and the hallway, and stored in the bedroom, so the honest place to drop them is wherever play stopped. That is the root cause behind toys on the living room floor every evening, and it is the one covered in why it lives in one room but gets used in another.

First, tip it all out and sort into families

Pick one zone, not the whole house: the bedroom toys or the living room toys. Tip every bin onto the floor or a play mat. Then make four piles:

  • Played with this month. These stay out.
  • Loved, but not lately. These go into the rotation box.
  • Outgrown or never chosen. These leave: donate, pass on, or sell.
  • Broken or missing pieces. Fix it this week or let it go. A toy waiting for a repair that never happens is just taking a bin.

Now group what stays into families a child would recognize: blocks with blocks, vehicles with vehicles, small figures together, art supplies together. Families matter more than any container, because a child can remember "the cars go in the car bin" long before they can follow a system an adult invented.

Involving a child in this works better than doing it while they are out, as long as you keep it small. Ask about one family at a time, and keep the question concrete: "Which three cars do you play with most?" is a question a child can answer. "Do you still want this?" almost never gets a no. The sentimental toys, the ones kept for the giver rather than the child, are a separate decision; how to decide about sentimental items is written for exactly that.

The storage that works: open, low, labeled

Once the toys are sorted into families, the storage rules are short:

  • One family per bin. Five or six bins is plenty for most rooms. A bin with two families in it becomes a bin with everything in it.
  • Open bins, no lids. A lid is one more step between a child and a tidy room, and a child will skip it. Deep toy chests with heavy hinged lids also carry a real risk of the lid falling on a child; if you keep one, choose a lid support that holds the lid open in any position, or take the lid off.
  • Low. Every bin a child uses sits no higher than their shoulder. The lowest shelf is the best shelf in the room.
  • Photo labels on the front. Take a picture of what goes inside and tape it to the face of the bin, where a child looking down sees it. Words are for adults. A picture works for a two-year-old.
  • Anchor the furniture. A low shelf with bins in it is something a toddler will pull up on. Anchor any shelf or dresser to the wall following the furniture's own instructions.

Cube storage

Cube shelving is the most common toy storage there is, and it works well if you follow the same rules: one family per cube bin, photo labels on the front of each bin, and the toys used most in the bottom row. Leave the top row for things a child should not reach on their own, like art supplies that need supervision or the rotation box. Keep the heavy bins low, so the unit cannot tip, and anchor it.

Large toys

Ride-ons, play kitchens, dollhouses and big building sets do not fit a bin, so give each a parking spot instead: a marked place on the floor or one shelf of its own. If a large toy has no spot, it is either the next thing to rotate out or the next thing to leave.

Toy rotation: fewer out, more played with

Rotation is the single most useful habit for toys. Keep only part of the collection out, and store the rest in a rotation box somewhere a child cannot reach, such as the top shelf of a closet. Write the date on the box. Every couple of weeks, swap a few toys: some go into the box, some come out.

Three things happen. Tidy-up gets shorter, because there is less out. Toys that come back out feel new. And the box answers the hardest question for you: a toy that has sat in the rotation box for months, and that nobody asked for, is ready to leave.

A living room with toys

Most families want toys in the living room, because that is where the adults are. The trick is to let the toys visit without letting them move in.

  • One container, a fixed size. One basket or two bins in the living room, holding a small selection. When it is full, something goes back to the bedroom before something new comes down.
  • A clear edge. A play mat or one corner of the room is the play area. Toys stay on it, so the rest of the room stays a living room.
  • An evening reset. Before the last part of the evening, whatever is on the floor goes back in the basket, and anything that belongs upstairs goes in a carry basket at the foot of the stairs.

The living room floor and circulation path guide covers the walking routes that toys tend to block, which matter most at night.

Small spaces

In a small home, rotation does most of the work, because the space you need is set by how many toys are out, not by how many you own. Use vertical space for the rotation box and anything adult-only, keep the child's bins on the lowest shelf, and give large toys a spot or a deadline. Under-bed bins work for a rotation set, as long as they slide out easily and the floor beside the bed stays clear for walking at night.

Safety, before anything else

Toy storage is the one zone in the house where a young child is the main user, so check these before you think about how it looks:

  • Small parts go up high if there is a child under three in the house. A quick check: anything that fits through a toilet paper tube is small enough to choke on, and it does not belong in a floor-level bin.
  • Button batteries. Keep toys with button or coin batteries in good repair, with the battery compartment closed, and store loose batteries out of reach. If a child may have swallowed one, get emergency medical help immediately.
  • Cracked hard plastic can have sharp edges. Toys with cracks, loose seams or corroded battery compartments leave the bins.
  • Tip-over. Shelves and dressers that hold toys are anchored to the wall, with the heaviest bins on the lowest shelf.

Keeping it that way

Toys drift back for the same reasons they took over: they keep arriving, and tidy-up is hard. Three habits keep it working:

  • Pickup before the next good thing. Clearing the floor is what comes before the story, the bath or the screen, every time. It works better than asking, because it is a routine, not a request.
  • Rotation on a rhythm. Swap a few toys every couple of weeks, and check the rotation box's date while you are there.
  • A quick sort after birthdays and holidays. When a batch of new toys arrives, it is the natural time to sort the family it joins and let one or two old ones go.

Common questions

What is the best way to organize toys?

Sort them into families a child recognizes, such as blocks, vehicles and small figures, and give each family one open bin with no lid on a low shelf. Tape a photo of what goes inside to the front of each bin. Keep only part of the collection out and put the rest in a dated rotation box out of reach.

How do you declutter kids toys?

Tip one zone's toys onto the floor and make four piles: played with this month, loved but not lately, outgrown or never chosen, and broken. The first stays out, the second goes into a rotation box, the third leaves, and the fourth gets fixed this week or goes. Ask a child about one family at a time with a concrete question, like which three cars they play with most.

How do I organize a living room with toys?

Give toys one fixed-size basket or two bins in the living room and one clear play area, such as a play mat. When the basket is full, something goes back to the bedroom before something new comes down, and an evening reset puts whatever is on the floor back in the basket.

How do you organize toys in cube storage?

One family of toys per cube bin, a photo label on the front of each bin, and the toys used most in the bottom row where a child can reach them. Use the top row for things that need an adult, like art supplies or the rotation box, keep heavy bins low, and anchor the unit to the wall.

What is toy rotation?

Keeping only part of the toy collection out and storing the rest in a dated box a child cannot reach, then swapping a few toys every couple of weeks. It makes tidy-up shorter, makes returning toys feel new, and shows you which toys nobody misses.

Should a toy box have a lid?

Open bins work better, because a lid is one more step a child will skip. A toy chest with a heavy hinged lid can also fall on a child; if you keep one, use a lid support that holds the lid open in any position, or remove the lid.

How do you organize toys in a small space?

Rotate. The space you need is set by how many toys are out, not how many you own. Keep a small selection in low open bins, store the rotation box up high or in an under-bed bin, and give each large toy one spot or a deadline to leave.
