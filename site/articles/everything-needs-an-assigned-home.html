Straighten, and the difference between a spot and a home

Why everything needs an assigned home, not just somewhere it could go

The counter has room on it, so the mail lands there. The chair has room on it, so the coat lands there. Neither one is a home, and that is the whole problem: a surface with space left is not the same thing as a place an item is actually supposed to be, and only one of those two stops the pile from reforming.

The short answer. No assigned home is the single most common root cause behind a cluttered zone. An item without one specific place to return to gets set down wherever there is room, which is a different spot every time, which is what a pile actually is. Naming one home per category, and writing it down if more than one person uses the space, fixes the pile at its cause instead of tidying its symptom.

A spot is not a home

Almost anywhere in a house can technically hold an item for a moment. A countertop can hold mail. A chair can hold a coat. A stair step can hold whatever is headed to the second floor. All of that space is real and all of it is available, which is exactly why it gets used, and exactly why it does not work. Availability answers "could this go here." A home answers a different question: "is this where it is supposed to be," and a spot that was merely open when you needed it has no answer to that second question at all.

The difference matters because only a home gets returned to on purpose. A spot that happened to have room gets used again by accident, the next time something else needs a place in a hurry, and a second item lands on top of the first. Nobody decided the counter is the mail's home. The counter just had room, twice in a row, and now it looks like a decision that nobody actually made.

How to tell a zone has this problem

Ask, out loud, where one specific kind of item lives. Not where it usually ends up: where it lives. If the honest answer is instant and the same every time, that category has a home. If the answer is some version of wherever there was room, or it depends, or normally over there but sometimes here, the category has a spot, not a home, and that is the gap doing the damage.

A reliable tell is the item that reappears in a different place each time you look for it. An item with a real home is boring to search for, because there is only one place to check. An item without one is genuinely hard to find, not because the house is large, but because its location was never decided, only guessed at, over and over, by whoever put it down last.

Assigning a home in three steps

  1. Name the category, not the item. "Chargers" is a home. "The white cable I use for my old phone" is not, because the next cable that shows up has nowhere defined to go. A home has to be broad enough to hold every future member of its category, not just the one sitting in your hand right now.
  2. Pick one location and say it out loud. A hook, a labeled shelf, a marked spot on a tray, or a specific drawer all count as a home. It does not need a container bought for the purpose. It needs to be one place, decided before the next item shows up, not chosen in the moment the way a merely available spot is.
  3. Write it down if more than one person uses the zone. A home that lives only in your head is a home for you and a mystery for everyone else in the house, and they will do exactly what you would do without it: set the item down wherever looked open. A short label or a line on the household's written standard turns a private decision into a shared one.

A worked example: three zones built entirely around this

The entryway landing zone exists to solve exactly this problem for keys, and its standard says so directly: the surface holds the tray and the folder and nothing else, and keys never ride out of the house in a coat pocket that lives on a hanger. The tray is not a spot keys could go. It is the one place they are supposed to be, which is why a missing set of keys in a house with this zone working means checking one tray instead of searching four rooms.

The stair landing handles the harder version of the same problem: items that are not homeless, only in transit to a home on another floor. Its standard is a stair basket that sleeps empty, a deliberate holding zone for things that already have a home somewhere else and are only passing through this one. That is different from a pile with nowhere to go, and naming the difference is what keeps the landing from becoming a permanent dumping ground for things that were only ever supposed to stop there for a day.

The charging and device zone shows the same idea at the level of a single item rather than a whole category: one named slot per device, so a phone, a tablet, and a game controller each has its own answer to "where does this go," instead of three devices competing for one open outlet wherever it happens to be free that night.

Where this fits in the six steps

Sort decides what stays. Straighten decides where it lives, and assigning a home is the actual work of that step, not a side effect of it. A zone can pass Sort completely, keeping only what genuinely belongs there, and still refill within a week if Straighten never gave each category one specific place to return to. The pile is not a Sort failure in that case. It is a Straighten failure wearing a Sort problem's clothes, and the fix is naming homes, not removing more things.

Common questions

What does no assigned home actually mean?

It means an item has a place it could go, or several, but no single place it is supposed to go. A surface with room on it is not the same as a home. A home is one named spot that the item returns to every time, decided in advance rather than picked in the moment.

How do I know if a zone has no assigned home problem?

Ask where one specific item lives and see if the answer is instant. If the honest answer is wherever there was room that day, or it depends, the zone has surfaces but not homes, and that is why the same pile keeps forming in the same spot.

Is a junk drawer the same as no assigned home?

A junk drawer is what happens when a whole category of small items, batteries, tape, spare keys, is never split into its own homes. The drawer itself can be a legitimate home for loose odds and ends. The problem is when everything with nowhere else to go ends up there by default, which turns one drawer into a home for every category at once.

Do I need a container for every item to give it a home?

No. A home is a location, not a purchase. A hook, a labeled shelf, a marked spot on a tray, or a specific drawer all count. Buying a bin before deciding the location just gives the clutter a nicer container to sit in without a home.

What if two people disagree about where something should live?

Pick one home and write it down where both people can see it, rather than letting the item drift between two informal spots depending on who put it away last. A home that only one person knows about is not a shared home, and the item will keep landing wherever whoever is holding it decides.
