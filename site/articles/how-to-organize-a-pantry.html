Start at the dry goods shelves, not the cans

How to organize a pantry, step by step

A pantry goes wrong in exactly two ways: food you cannot see, and food you already owned when you bought it again. Start with the dry goods shelves, because that is where the oldest opened bags are hiding, and emptying them gives every other zone somewhere to put things. Then work outward: the cans and jars, the baking zone, the snack and lunch bin, and the backstock and bulk reserve at the back. The whole pantry is about three to four and three quarter hours of hands-on work across five zones, which is a weekend or several evenings, not an afternoon.

The short answer. Take one dry goods shelf off and onto the counter, open every bag, and bin anything stale, webbed or bought for a recipe you cooked once. Check every can and jar for a dented seam, a weeping lid or a hiss, which means spoiled rather than old. Build one lift-out bin for baking basics, set a child-height snack bin to what the youngest person in the house may take without asking, and keep the backstock reserve at or below a written maximum, dated the month it arrived.

Before you start: why the dry goods shelves come first

A pantry is five small zones, and the dry goods shelves are where the oldest, least visible problem lives: the opened bag pushed behind the cereal, the flour nobody remembers buying. Emptying that shelf first does two things at once. It gives you somewhere to put things while you work the rest of the room, and it tells you honestly what you actually cook from in a normal week, which is the fact every other zone gets built around.

Once the shelves are clear, this room needs very little equipment: a few lidded containers for staples, a tray for the baking basics, one open bin for snacks, and a marker for writing dates on lids and shelf edges. Resist buying bins before you have emptied what is already here.

Step 1: The dry goods shelves

Bring down every bag and box, open the ones already opened, and smell the flour and the oats. Stale, webbed, or bought for one recipe you cooked two summers ago means it leaves today, not back on the shelf.

Rice, pasta and oats go at chest height, in the arc your arm makes when you are already holding a pan. Specialty flours and the lentils you reach for once a month go on the shelf above, where standing on your toes is an honest signal about how often you use them. Keep heavy grain containers low and light boxes high, nothing stacked more than two deep, since a cereal box or a heavy jar coming down from above shoulder height lands on whoever is underneath it. Once a container is open, write the date on the lid: a standard is only useful once it is written down. The dry goods shelves zone covers the full standard, including the moth and webbing check that is most of your pest plan. It is forty-five to seventy-five minutes, most of it opening bags and reading dates.

Step 2: The canned and jarred goods

Take every can and jar out and find the date on each one. A dented seam, a rusted rim, a domed lid or a hiss as it opens means spoiled rather than merely old, and it goes straight into the bin, never tasted, decanted or given to the dog. Then count duplicates, because four jars of the same pasta sauce is telling you exactly what you keep buying blind.

What is left goes back in rows by meal rather than by size: tomatoes, coconut milk and stock together because they cook together, beans and tuna together because they are lunch, tall jars behind short cans so nothing disappears. Label each row and put the back row up on a riser so it sits above the front row, and keep the densest rows below shoulder height, since a can landing on a bare foot is a broken toe and a glass jar bursting on tile leaves splinters you find for weeks. The canned and jarred goods zone is thirty to forty-five minutes.

Step 3: The baking zone

Test the leaveners rather than guessing: a spoon of baking powder into hot water should fizz hard, and yeast in warm sugared water should foam. The dead ones go, along with the brick of hardened brown sugar and the cocoa that has turned gray.

Build one lift-out bin holding flour, sugar, bicarbonate of soda, vanilla and a scoop, so starting a bake is one trip to the shelf instead of six, with cutters and piping tips in a second bin behind it. Give nut flours, almond meal and sesame their own sealed container and their own scoop at the end of the row, since one shared scoop carries allergen traces straight into a cake, and keep small decorating pieces like silver balls off any shelf a toddler can reach. Write the opening date on every leavener's lid the day you open it, since the printed date stops meaning much once air is in the tin. The baking zone is thirty to forty-five minutes.

Step 4: The snack and lunch zone

Match every lid to a container and bin the orphans in both directions: lidless tubs, and lids for tubs that already died. Pull the snack boxes with two bars rattling in the bottom and combine them.

Pre-approved snacks go into one open bin at child height, set by the youngest person in the house who can reach it, since whole nuts, hard sweets and grapes going straight from an open bin into a small mouth is a real choking risk. The snacks that start an argument go on a shelf above eye line, where reaching for them means climbing, and a shelving unit a child climbs either moves out of the pantry or gets fixed to the wall. Lunch bags and ice packs stand next to the containers rather than in a drawer in another room. The snack and lunch zone is thirty to forty-five minutes.

Step 5: The backstock and bulk zone

Open up the back of this zone and meet what you forgot you owned: the second sack of rice, the twelve-pack of a sauce nobody liked, the case of sparkling water from a party in another season. Anything you hold more than one spare of moves forward into the working shelves this month or it leaves.

Weight decides height: sacks and cases go on the floor or the bottom shelf, paper towels go up top because they are light, and everything sits one row deep so you never move a case to see a case. Stack no higher than chest height, since a stacked column is stable right up until somebody pulls the second case from the bottom, and bend at the knees for a rice sack rather than lifting it with a twist. Write a maximum for each line on the shelf edge and mark every pack with its arrival month, since undated bulk is where the saving quietly expires out of sight. The backstock and bulk zone is forty-five to seventy-five minutes.

Your pantry checklist

  • Every dry goods bag opened, smelled, and dated on the lid if it stays
  • Every can and jar checked for a dented seam, rust, a domed lid or a hiss, and binned if found
  • Cans and jars labeled by row, back row on a riser, dense rows below shoulder height
  • One lift-out bin for flour, sugar, bicarb and a scoop, allergen flours sealed and set apart
  • One open snack bin at child height, set by the youngest child who can reach it
  • Backstock at or under a written maximum per line, each pack dated with its arrival month
  • Stacked cases no higher than chest height, heavy sacks lifted with bent knees
  • Nothing stacked more than two deep on the dry goods shelves

Deep shelves, a small pantry, or a pantry cabinet

A deep shelf is still only as useful as the forearm's reach of it: treat the back as backstock, never as an everyday zone, and bring the whole shelf forward when you restock rather than sliding one new can behind the row you already have. Pull-out shelves and wire racks hold the same five zones, they just make the depth easier to see without emptying it first.

A small pantry or a single cabinet runs the same five zones at a smaller scale rather than skipping any of them: a shelf for dry goods, a shelf for cans and jars, a small bin for baking basics, a tub for snacks, and the thinnest possible backstock, since there is the least room to spare for a spare you are not using.

Keeping it that way

Read the can rows and the shelf gaps before the shopping list gets written, not after. A missing tin of tomatoes is far easier to see on a shelf than to remember at a table, and cans never force the issue by spoiling, so this room fails silently and by depth if nobody looks. Refill the snack bin to its marked line every evening, by whoever emptied the lunchboxes, and let a working container running empty be the trigger that pulls its replacement from backstock in the same movement, written on the list as it happens. If snacks keep turning into an argument at four o'clock, why your family won't put things back is the same question this pantry's bin already answers: the decision belongs on the shelf, not in the moment. If staples keep running out without warning, why you keep running out of things is the fix: one visible maximum, written where you restock.

Common questions

How do I organize a pantry?

Start with the dry goods shelves, because that is where the oldest opened bags are hiding, and emptying them gives every other zone somewhere to put things. Then work the cans and jars, the baking zone, the snack and lunch bin, and finish with the backstock and bulk reserve at the back, one zone at a time rather than the whole pantry at once.

Where do I start organizing a pantry?

Take one dry goods shelf completely off and onto the counter before you decide anything. A shelf sorted in place keeps its own history, and the bag pushed behind the cereal two years ago stays behind the cereal unless you empty the whole shelf first.

How long does it take to organize a pantry?

The whole pantry is about three to four and three quarter hours of hands-on work across its five zones, so plan a weekend or a few evenings. The dry goods shelves and the backstock zone are the longest at forty-five to seventy-five minutes each, and the lightest zones are thirty to forty-five minutes.

How do I organize a pantry with deep shelves?

Treat the back of a deep shelf as backstock, never as an everyday zone: nothing you reach for weekly belongs further back than your forearm. Bring the whole shelf forward when you restock rather than sliding one new can behind the row, and write a maximum quantity on the shelf edge so depth stops hiding a third jar you forgot you owned.

How do I organize a small pantry or a pantry cabinet?

Run the same five zones at a smaller scale rather than skipping any of them: a shelf for dry goods, a shelf for cans and jars, a small bin for baking basics, a child-height tub for snacks, and the thinnest possible backstock, since a small cabinet has the least room to spare for a spare you are not using yet.

How do I keep a pantry organized?

Read the gaps before you write the shopping list: a missing can or an empty spot on the shelf is easier to see than to remember at a table. Refill the snack bin to its marked line every evening, and let a working container running empty be the trigger that pulls its replacement from backstock, in the same movement, written on the list as it happens.
