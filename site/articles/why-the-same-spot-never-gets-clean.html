Shine, and the spot that always loses

Why does the same spot never actually get clean?

The blinds. The top of the fridge. The range hood filter. The inside of the microwave. Every house has one or two spots like this, cleaned rarely enough that you can remember the last time. It is not the spot you forgot. It is the spot that keeps losing to every other five minute task on the list, because it was never a five minute task to begin with.

The short answer. A spot gets skipped when cleaning it costs more than everything else nearby, a stool, a moved appliance, a product kept in another room, or parts that have to come off first. That is not a motivation problem, it is a cost problem, and the fix is the same one 6S uses everywhere else: lower the actual cost of doing the right thing instead of trying to want it more.

Why it is never the spot you forgot

Ask someone which part of their kitchen they clean least and the answer comes fast, with no hesitation. That is the tell. A genuinely forgotten spot would not be so easy to name. What people are describing is not a memory failure, it is a spot they think about and then do not do, over and over, which is a completely different thing. Every time there is a free stretch of time to clean something, the mind runs a quick, mostly unconscious cost comparison across everything that needs doing, and the spot that needs a stool and a screwdriver loses to the counter that needs a wipe, every single time, for the same reason a longer line always loses to a shorter one.

This is not a willpower problem and it is not a standards problem. Somebody who keeps a spotless counter every day and has not touched the range hood filter in a year is not inconsistent. They are running the same rational cost comparison you would, and the filter keeps losing on cost, not on care.

The root cause, named

Call it what it is: difficult cleaning. Distinct from a missing standard, because the person usually knows exactly what "clean" would look like there. Distinct from Safety, because the spot is not usually hazardous, just unpleasant to get to. And distinct from simple neglect, because the same person who skips this one spot is often meticulous everywhere else in the same room. The zone fails not because nobody cares about it, but because the physical cost of cleaning it, in tools, reach, and disassembly, is genuinely higher than the cost of cleaning the rest of the room combined, and nothing about the task shrinks that gap on its own.

Four things that make a spot expensive to clean

Almost every chronically avoided spot is expensive for one or more of these four reasons. Naming which one applies is most of the fix, because each has a different, specific answer.

  • The tool lives somewhere else. The glass cleaner is under the bathroom sink and the window that needs it is in the bedroom. Every cleaning session now has a first step that is not cleaning: a trip to fetch something. That small detour is enough friction to turn "I'll do it now" into "I'll do it later" more often than not.
  • It needs a stool, a ladder, or moving something heavy. Ceiling fan blades, the top of cabinets, behind the refrigerator. The task itself might take ninety seconds. Getting into position to do it is what actually costs the time and the risk, and that setup cost has to be paid in full whether you clean the spot for ninety seconds or five minutes, which quietly pushes people toward doing it as rarely as possible rather than as often as it needs.
  • It has to be taken apart first. A range hood filter, an oven rack, blinds that have to come down slat by slat. Disassembly and reassembly can take longer than the actual cleaning, and unlike the cleaning itself, taking something apart offers no visible progress while you are doing it, which makes it feel like pure overhead.
  • Nobody sees it up close. The top of a tall cabinet, behind furniture, inside a vent. A dirty visible counter creates its own pressure to fix it. A dirty spot nobody looks at directly creates none, so it is competing for attention against spots that are actively bothering someone, and it keeps losing.

Lower the cost, do not raise the discipline

The instinct is usually to solve this with a schedule, a reminder, a resolution to finally deal with it. Schedules and reminders address the wrong side of the equation. They try to make the expensive task feel more urgent instead of making it less expensive, and urgency fades within a week while the actual cost of the task, the stool, the disassembly, the fetched tool, has not changed at all. The task will still lose the next comparison it faces, reminder or not.

The 6S answer is the same one used for a missing reorder line or a home in the wrong location: change what the task costs, not how much you want to do it. Keep the tool inside the zone instead of across the house, so there is no fetch step. Cut a difficult reach down with a grabber, a lower shelf, or a permanent step stool stored right there rather than in a closet. Where disassembly is unavoidable, cut the interval instead of the difficulty, a filter wiped monthly stays far easier than one scrubbed once a year, because grime that has not had a year to bake on comes off in a fraction of the time.

A worked example

Take the range hood filter, a near universal version of this problem. It is mounted overhead, coated in a film that only gets worse with time, held on by two clips that are not obvious on first look, and the degreaser that actually cuts through it is usually stored under a different sink entirely. Every one of the four costs above is present at once, which is exactly why it is one of the most commonly neglected spots in an otherwise clean kitchen. The fix is not a bigger resolution to finally deal with it. It is moving a small bottle of degreaser into the kitchen itself, learning the two clips once and writing that down, and putting the whole task on a fixed interval short enough that the grease never gets the chance to bake on and turn a five minute wipe into a twenty minute scrub.

What this is not

This is not about lowering your standards or deciding some spots do not matter. A range hood filter caked in old grease is a fire and airflow issue, not a cosmetic one, and looking clean is not the same as being safe. The point is not to accept the spot staying dirty. It is to stop treating the fact that it keeps getting skipped as a discipline failure, when it is a design failure in how much the task costs to start.

The habit that makes it stick

Once the cost is actually lowered, tool nearby, reach shortened, interval matched to how fast the grime returns, the spot needs one more thing to stay clean: a trigger that is not "whenever I remember." Attach it to something that already happens on a fixed schedule nearby, wipe the range hood filter the same day you take out the recycling, dust the ceiling fan the same weekend you change the bedding. The spot stops depending on memory the moment it is riding on a habit that was already going to happen anyway.

Before you start. This is guidance for organising and cleaning a home. It is not instruction for electrical, gas, structural, or any other licensed work. Do not lift or move anything unsafe, do not mix cleaning products, and follow the label on anything you use. If a job needs a professional, that is the right answer. See the full safety notice.

Common questions

Why do I keep avoiding cleaning certain spots in my home?

Most of the time it is not avoidance in the sense of not caring. It is that the spot costs more to clean than everything else nearby, a step stool, a removed panel, a product you do not have on hand, and a task that costs more than the others quietly loses to the ones that do not, every time you are deciding what to do with a free twenty minutes. The spot is not forgotten. It is correctly, if unconsciously, priced as the most expensive item on the list.

What makes something difficult to clean, specifically?

Four things, usually in combination: it needs a tool or product kept somewhere else, it needs to be reached with a stool or by moving something heavy, it needs to be taken apart before you can get at the actual dirt, or the payoff is invisible because nobody sees it up close. Any one of these adds friction. Two or three together are enough to make a spot get skipped for months without anyone deciding that on purpose.

Do I need special products to clean the spots I keep avoiding?

Usually not a different product, a closer one. The active ingredient rarely changes between a spot that gets cleaned weekly and one that gets cleaned once a year. What changes is whether the right bottle and cloth are already in the room, or in a different room, a different floor, or a cabinet you have to unload to reach. Move the tool to the zone and most of the difficulty goes with it.

How do I know if a spot is genuinely hard to clean or if I am just avoiding it?

Time the actual task once, honestly, from gathering what you need to putting it away. Most spots people describe as impossible take two to five minutes once the tool is in hand; what took longer was finding the tool, working up to it, or both. If the task itself, done, is genuinely over fifteen minutes or needs taking something apart, that is a real difficulty worth redesigning around, not a discipline problem.

What is the Shine pass in the 6S method?

Shine is the third of the six steps, Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, Sustain. It is the only point in the method where you clean a zone while it is empty, which is also the only time you can actually see and reach every surface in it, so a zone that gets skipped at Shine carries hidden dirt and hidden damage into every step that follows.
