Sort, and the collection nobody ever tested

How to organize recipes, on paper and on your phone

A recipe pile is rarely a storage problem. It is a collection that grows every time you browse and shrinks only when you cook, which is why it outruns you. Keep what you have actually made, give it one home instead of four, and attach the removal step to the weekly shop so the collection stops rebuilding itself.

The recipes are in a drawer, on the side of the fridge, inside three cookbooks with the corners turned down, in a folder of printouts, and in several hundred screenshots on a phone. Nobody can find the one they want, so the household cooks the same six meals it already knows by heart. Buying a nicer binder does not change that, and neither does an afternoon of filing. Here is the part that does.

Why a recipe collection grows faster than you cook

Saving a recipe takes a second. Cooking one takes an evening, a shopping trip and an appetite for the result. Those two numbers are so far apart that any collection with no removal step in it will grow steadily while the meals on your table stay exactly the same. That is not a discipline failure. It is arithmetic, and it is the reason the pile feels like it appeared overnight.

So the root cause here is almost never inadequate capacity. It is a collection with no test and no exit: nothing ever asks whether a saved recipe earned its place, and nothing ever removes one that did not. A box, a binder or an app added before that question is answered just gives an untested collection a tidier address. The same thing happens to a drawer that nobody has given a job, which is why more storage will not fix a messy zone on its own.

Decide the one home first, because recipes live in three places at once

Before sorting anything, decide where the collection is going to live. This matters more for recipes than for most things, because a recipe collection is the rare zone that is genuinely split across paper, a shelf of books and a phone, and a household searching three places will reliably check the wrong one first.

Pick one place as the keeper. A binder or a card box is a reasonable choice: it survives a flat battery, it props open on a counter, and anybody in the house can use it without an account. A single named folder or album on a phone is equally reasonable if that is genuinely where you cook from. What does not work is both at once, with no rule about which holds what. If you keep paper as the keeper, the phone becomes a capture tool only, and it gets emptied into the binder on the same day each week.

Cookbooks stay on the shelf as their own thing. They are not the problem as long as the recipes you cook from them have been copied into the keeper, which is the step most collections skip.

The test that sorts the whole pile in one pass

Bring every loose recipe into one place: the drawer, the fridge door, the folder, the pages torn from magazines, the handwritten cards. Having it all in one pile is uncomfortable and it is the point, because the size of the collection is the fact that has been hidden across four locations.

Then deal it into three piles, holding each recipe only once.

  • Cooked, and would cook again. You have made it at least twice. This is the real collection, and in most households it is far smaller than the pile it came out of.
  • Never cooked. Not a verdict on the recipe, only on the evidence. These go into one dated envelope at the back of the binder, not back into circulation.
  • Cannot use. Faded, torn mid-method, missing the oven temperature, or a photograph of a page where the ingredient list is cut off. An incomplete recipe is not a recipe, and this pile leaves today.

Date the envelope. If a season passes and you have not pulled a single recipe out of it, it leaves unopened, without being read through again first. That turns a question you cannot answer honestly in the moment into a fair, time limited test, which is the same approach that works for anything you cannot decide about while sorting.

File by the question you ask at six o'clock

Only now do the dividers go in, and they should match how the decision is actually made. Almost nobody stands in a kitchen at the end of a working day and thinks of a dish by name. They think about what is in the fridge, or how long they have, or who is eating. Dividers like chicken, pasta, slow weekend, thirty minutes and feeds a crowd will find a meal faster than an alphabetical list of titles ever will.

Write the divider names on paper before you file anything, and keep the set small enough that no recipe could plausibly sit under two of them. If one keeps belonging in two places, the divider names are wrong, not the recipe. A collection small enough to file in an afternoon rarely needs more than six or seven.

The six passes, start to finish

For a typical household collection this is one session of about an hour, and the slow part is the sorting, not the filing.

  • Sort. Every loose recipe into one pile, then dealt into cooked, never cooked and cannot use. The cannot use pile leaves the house today. The never cooked pile goes into one dated envelope.
  • Straighten. One keeper, chosen before filing starts. Dividers named for the question you would actually ask, written down first, then the keepers filed behind them. Cookbooks you cook from get their used pages copied into the keeper so you never have to remember which book held what.
  • Shine. Wipe the cards and sleeves that have been splashed, and throw out any page too greasy to read. If the keeper is a binder that lives on a counter, give the cover a wipe, because it sits in a room where everything gets a film on it eventually.
  • Safety. Paper belongs away from the cooktop. A recipe propped against a pan, a clipping taped to the backsplash, or a binder left open beside a lit burner is a genuine fire risk, and so is a dish towel laid over the top of one. If you cook from a phone or tablet, give it a stand away from the burners and away from the sink rather than balancing it on the edge of a wet counter.
  • Standardize. Write the divider names on the inside cover, so the categories are visible rather than remembered. Anything that does not match a divider does not get filed in the keeper, which stops the binder quietly turning back into a pile with a cover on it.
  • Sustain. Attach both halves to the weekly shopping list. New recipes captured during the week get filed or discarded while you write the list, and anything in the dated envelope that has not been cooked by season's end leaves. One habit, attached to something that already happens, is what keeps this from needing doing again next year.

Screenshots, bookmarks and the ones you can never find again

A phone collection fails differently from a paper one. Nothing is lost, everything is saved, and none of it is findable, because a screenshot of a recipe looks exactly like a screenshot of anything else in a roll of several thousand photographs.

The fix is the same two decisions in a different medium. Give the collection one named album or folder, so recipes are not sitting among holiday photographs. Then, once a week while the list is being written, go through what you saved: the ones you would genuinely cook get moved into the keeper, and the rest get deleted. Deleting is the step that feels wrong and is the one doing the work, because a saved recipe you will never cook costs you nothing to keep and costs you every search.

If the keeper is paper and the recipe came from a screen, write it out or print it. The copying is not wasted effort: it is the moment you find out whether you actually want it enough to spend four minutes on it.

What to do with cookbooks you use two pages of

A cookbook earns its shelf space by being cooked from. If a book has two recipes you make and nothing else you have ever tried, copy those two pages into the keeper and let the book go. Keep the ones you genuinely read for pleasure, and keep the ones with several recipes in rotation. The point is not a smaller shelf for its own sake, it is that a book you have to remember to look inside is a worse filing system than a binder that opens at the right divider.

One check before anything leaves: look inside the front cover. An inscription from somebody who is gone is the one thing a photograph of a page does not replace, and that is a different decision from an organizing one. If a book is hard to part with for that reason, it is not clutter, and the honest move is to keep it and copy the page anyway. The same distinction applies to anything you are keeping for who gave it to you rather than what it does.

When the collection keeps rebuilding itself

If the binder was sorted last year and the pile is back, the sorting was never the problem. A collection that grows every time you browse and shrinks only when you cook will always return to the same size unless something removes from it on a schedule. Doing the sort again, harder, buys another few months.

What holds is the removal step and where it is attached. Tied to the weekly list, it happens because the list happens. Left as a standalone intention, it waits for a free afternoon that does not arrive, and the pile grows back at exactly the rate it grew the first time. That is the same reason a zone you reset drifts back within a week: the arrangement changed, but nothing attached the standard to a routine that was already running.

Common questions

How do you organize recipes?

Sort by what you have actually cooked, not by category. Go through the collection once and keep only the recipes you have made at least twice and would make again, plus a small, honestly labeled shortlist of ones you genuinely intend to try. Then give the keepers one home rather than four: a single binder or box for paper, a single named folder or album for anything on a phone. Category dividers come last, after the collection is small enough that the categories are obvious.

How do you organize printed recipes and clippings?

Put every printout, clipping and handwritten card into one pile, then deal them into three: cooked and would cook again, never cooked, and cannot read or is missing a step. The third pile leaves straight away, because an incomplete recipe is not a recipe. The never cooked pile goes into a dated envelope at the back of the binder, and if you have not pulled anything from it by the end of a season it leaves unopened. Only the first pile earns a sleeve or a divider.

How do you organize paper recipes so you can actually find one?

File them by the question you would ask when you are standing in the kitchen at six o'clock, which is almost never the name of the dish. Most households reach for a meal by what it is made of or how long it takes, so dividers like chicken, pasta, slow weekend and thirty minutes find a recipe faster than an alphabetical list of titles. Write the divider names down before you file anything, and keep the set small enough that no recipe could plausibly belong under two of them.

Should you keep recipes on paper or on your phone?

Either works, but splitting the same collection across both is what makes recipes hard to find. Pick one as the place the collection lives and let the other be temporary. A common arrangement is paper as the keeper, because a binder survives a dead battery and sits open on a counter, with the phone used only for capture, then emptied into the binder on the same day each week. The rule that matters is that there is exactly one place you would look first.

What do you do with cookbooks you only use one or two recipes from?

Copy or photograph the pages you actually cook, add them to the binder, and let the book go if nothing else in it has ever been made. A cookbook earns its shelf space by being cooked from, not by being owned. Keep the ones you genuinely read for pleasure and the ones with many recipes in rotation, and stop treating a book with two good pages as a storage obligation. Check any gift inscription before a book leaves, because that is the one thing a photograph does not replace.

Why does my recipe collection keep growing even though I cook the same meals?

Because saving a recipe costs almost nothing and cooking one costs an evening, so the collection grows at the speed of browsing rather than the speed of your kitchen. Nothing in the loop ever removes anything, which is why it keeps growing while the meals stay the same. The fix is a removal step attached to something you already do: when you write the shopping list, anything you saved and have not cooked within a season goes to the dated envelope, and the collection stops outrunning you.
