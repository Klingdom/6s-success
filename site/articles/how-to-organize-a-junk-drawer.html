Sort, and the one drawer every kitchen is allowed

How to organize a junk drawer, and keep it that way

Every kitchen ends up with one: the drawer that sticks a little, holds a torch, three pens that may or may not write, a roll of tape, and a screw that belongs to something you can no longer name. The usual advice is to empty it and buy a divider. That fixes it for a week. Here is what actually keeps it fixed.

The short answer. A kitchen is allowed one drawer of small useful things, but it needs a name and a written list, not an amnesty. Decide what belongs, tape, string, a torch, batteries, a working pen, the takeaway menus you actually order from, then hold everything in it to that list. Anything you cannot name a use for while you are holding it goes into a dated box on a shelf instead, and if nothing has been pulled out of the box by the end of a season it leaves unopened.

Why every kitchen gets one, and why that is not a personality flaw

A junk drawer is not evidence that you are disorganized. It is what happens to the drawer nearest the door, the counter, or wherever you are standing when your hands are full and a decision needs to be made in half a second. Setting something down there is not laziness, it is the nearest available surface winning against every other option in the moment you needed one. It happens every time a drawer has no job, because the real decision being avoided is never what to throw away. It is admitting the drawer needs a job at all.

That is a root cause worth naming plainly: an unnamed drawer has no rule for what belongs in it, so anything with nowhere else to go defaults into the nearest one that opens easily. Buying a divider before that question is answered gives the same clutter a tidier place to live. It does not stop the drawer from filling, because the divider was never the problem.

The rule that keeps it from becoming a second junk drawer

One drawer of small useful things is a reasonable thing for a kitchen to have. What keeps it from sliding back into chaos is treating it like any other named zone rather than a catch-all. Write its list before you fill it: tape, string, a torch, batteries, a working pen, the takeaway menus you actually order from. Everything that goes back in has to be something you would genuinely come to the kitchen looking for. That is the whole test, and it takes less time to apply than it takes to read this paragraph.

Then take an empty box for the rest. Anything you cannot name a use for while you are actually holding it goes in the box, not back in the drawer. Date the box, set it on a shelf, and leave it alone. If nothing has been pulled out of it by the end of the season, it leaves unopened. You never have to feel certain about any one object to make this work, because time makes the final call instead of you. The same box-and-date approach is the right move for anything you cannot decide about while sorting, not only what lands in a kitchen drawer.

What actually belongs, and what is only hiding there

The honest list is short. A torch, spare batteries that are still tested and dated, string, tape, a working pen, and the two or three takeaway menus for places you genuinely order from. Everything on that list earns its place because you would come looking for it in that exact drawer.

What does not belong is almost always a decision that got postponed rather than made. The screws in an unlabelled envelope, the manual for an appliance that left the house months ago, a second and third can opener, loose button batteries rolling free, an unlabelled key that opens nothing you can currently name. None of those are kitchen items, and they are the reason the drawer no longer closes flush. They are not clutter because they are worthless. They are clutter because nobody has decided where they actually live, and this drawer was simply the easiest place to stop deciding.

The five minute reset

The whole job is smaller than it feels from the outside, usually fifteen to thirty minutes, and the slow part is never the wiping.

  • Sort. Tip the drawer onto a tea towel and put back only what matches the list you just wrote. Two can openers means one leaves. Rubber bands, the unlabelled key, and loose batteries are not utility items and do not go back in.
  • Straighten. A divider goes in before anything else does, and you name the drawer before you fill it, not after. The category is the plan, not an afterthought once the drawer is already full again.
  • Shine. Lift the whole insert out and tip the grit, because the crumbs that jam a drawer are never in the visible tray, they are underneath the divider and packed into the runner track. Wipe both runners before you refill it, and check that it closes with one push.
  • Safety. A loose blade lying among the spatulas cuts the hand that reaches in without looking, and loose batteries at a child's drawer-opening height are a real swallowing hazard. Give any blade a sheath or its own shallow tray at the front, and keep spare batteries in a closed container rather than rolling free.
  • Standardize. Write the category inside the drawer front. The name is the rule, and anything that does not match the name does not go in, no matter how convenient the drawer is in the moment.
  • Sustain. When you put the shopping away, the new roll of tape goes in on end and anything that has migrated into the wrong drawer travels back to its own. That single habit, attached to something you already do weekly, is what stops the reset from being needed again in a month.

When it will not stay organized no matter how many times you redo it

If a junk drawer keeps refilling within weeks of being emptied, the emptying was never the problem. A drawer with no name and no written list is still the easiest place in the kitchen to set something down, so it will keep collecting exactly what it collected before, on the same schedule. The fix that actually holds is not doing the sort again, harder. It is writing the category where you will see it and treating anything that breaks the category as something that needs a real home somewhere else in the house, today, rather than an excuse to reopen the drawer's amnesty.

This is the same pattern behind why a reset zone drifts back to its old shape: the arrangement held for a while, but nothing made the standard visible or gave it a place to attach to a routine, so it quietly slid back the moment attention moved elsewhere. A name on the drawer front is a small, cheap version of exactly that fix.

Is it actually fine to have one

Yes. The goal was never a kitchen with zero junk drawers, and aiming for that is a good way to make the whole project feel impossible before it starts. One drawer of small useful things, named and listed, is a legitimate category with a real job: catching the torch, the tape, the spare pen, the two menus you actually call. What turns it from a system into a problem is only the absence of a rule for what belongs, which is a five minute fix, not a character flaw.

Common questions

How do you organize a junk drawer?

Give it a name and a written list of what belongs, then hold everything in the drawer to that list. A kitchen is allowed one drawer of small useful things, tape, string, a torch, batteries, a working pen, the takeaway menus you actually order from, but it has to be a named category with a divider, not an amnesty for anything with nowhere else to go. Anything you cannot name a use for while you are holding it does not go back in.

What should not go in a junk drawer?

Anything that is not actually a small useful thing you would come to that room looking for. Screws in an envelope, the manual for an appliance you no longer own, loose batteries, an unlabelled key, and duplicate tools are the classic residents, and none of them are kitchen or utility items. They are unmade decisions that landed in the nearest open drawer because it was easier than deciding where they actually belong.

How often should you clean out a junk drawer?

Twice a year is enough for the drawer itself, timed to when the season changes, because that is a natural prompt to ask whether you have used something since the weather last turned. The overflow box that catches what does not fit the drawer's list works on a slower clock: date it once, and if nothing has been pulled out of it by the end of a season, it leaves unopened without being reopened first.

Is it normal to have a junk drawer?

Yes, and one is a reasonable thing for a kitchen to have. The problem was never that the drawer exists. It is that most junk drawers have no name and no list, so anything with nowhere else to go defaults into it, and a drawer with no rule for what belongs will fill with everything. One named, listed drawer is a system. An unnamed one is where decisions go to be avoided.

Why does my junk drawer keep filling back up after I clean it out?

Because cleaning it out sorted the objects but never fixed the drawer's job. If the drawer still has no name and no written list, it is still the easiest place in the kitchen to set something down, and it will refill on the same schedule as before. The fix that holds is writing the category inside the front edge, so the name becomes the rule and anything that does not match the name has to find its home somewhere else instead of defaulting back in.

What do I do with things I cannot decide about while sorting a junk drawer?

Do not force a decision on the spot. Put them in a dated overflow box on a shelf instead: date it today, and if a season passes without you going back into the box for anything, it leaves unopened. That turns an unanswerable question into a fair, time-limited test, which is the same rule that works for any object you cannot decide about, not only the ones that end up in a kitchen drawer.
