Straighten, and the shelf that fights back

Why is the thing you use every day the hardest one to reach?

The everyday pan lives under two others. The daily mug is on the shelf you need the step stool for. The good scissors are behind the sewing kit nobody has opened this year. None of this is random. It is what happens when a zone gets filled by whatever order things arrived in, rather than by how often each one actually gets used.

The short answer. A zone can be fully sorted, fully homed, and still fail every day because the item you reach for most is not the item in the easiest spot. That is poor accessibility, a fixable mismatch between frequency and placement, not a discipline problem and not a storage shortage.

It looks like a shelf problem

Open the cabinet that annoys you most. Somewhere in it is a thing you touch almost every day, sitting behind, above, or under something you touch a few times a year. The everyday coffee mug on the top shelf next to the punch bowl that comes out once. The pan you cook with tonight nested under two you have not used since spring. None of it is disorganized in the usual sense. Everything has a spot. The spots are just not sorted by the one thing that actually matters day to day: how often your hand goes there.

The usual read on this is that the cabinet is "just how it is," or that whoever put it away was careless. Neither is quite right. Storage almost never fills up in frequency order. It fills in whatever order things happened to be unpacked, gifted, or put back after the last time the cabinet was cleared out, and that order has nothing to do with how often anything inside it gets used.

The root cause, named

Call it what it is: poor accessibility. Distinct from too many steps, which is about how many separate actions a task takes once you have your hands on the thing. Distinct from inadequate capacity, which is about whether there is room at all. Poor accessibility is a placement problem specifically: the right item exists, there may be plenty of room for it, and it is still costing more than it should to get to, because nobody weighed reach and height against how often it gets used.

It hides well because it rarely announces itself the way a jammed drawer does. Nobody has an argument about the top shelf. They just quietly stop using what is on it, or they keep using it and pay a small physical cost every single time, a stretch, a stool, a stack moved and restacked, until the cost adds up to a habit of reaching for whatever is easiest instead of whatever is best.

The rule underneath "eye level" and "arm's reach"

Every zone in this method that talks about placement is applying the same rule, whether it says so directly or not: match height to frequency, not to category or to whatever fits. The band between your shoulder and your waist is the true no stretch, no stool, no bending zone, and it is the smallest, most valuable real estate in any cabinet, drawer, or closet. What goes there should be decided by one question only, how often does this get used, not by what category it belongs to or what shape it happens to be.

  • Daily, several times a day. The reach band. Shoulder to waist, front of the shelf, nothing stacked on top of it.
  • Weekly. Just above or just below the reach band. A small stretch or a slight bend, not a stool.
  • A few times a year. The top shelf, the back corner, the high cupboard. This is the only honest reason a stool belongs in the plan at all.

Weight adds one more rule on top of frequency: heavy items sit low regardless of how often they are used, lifted with a squat rather than pulled down from above a shoulder. A cast iron pan used weekly still belongs at or below waist height. Frequency decides how central a spot is. Weight decides how high off the ground that spot is allowed to be, and the two are answered separately, not traded off against each other.

Four zones built around exactly this

The rule sounds simple written out. Seeing it applied is what makes it usable, so here it is in four real zones from the method, each one a small, specific answer rather than a general principle.

  • The Upper Cabinets. Everyday plates and bowls sit at eye level on the side nearest the dishwasher, glasses beside them. The serving platters and the punch bowl, used a handful of times a year, go to the top shelf, where a step stool is an honest once a year cost rather than a daily one.
  • The Lower Cabinets and Cookware. Cast iron and the full stockpot sit on the cabinet floor, never above waist height, lifted from a squat rather than a bend over an open door. Weight, not frequency, is what puts them there.
  • The Dry Goods Shelves. Rice, pasta, and oats sit at chest height, inside the arc your arm already makes while holding a pan. The specialty flours reached for once a month go on the shelf above, where standing on your toes is the honest cost of something used that rarely.
  • The Snack and Lunch Zone. Approved snacks sit in an open bin a child can reach without a stool. The ones that start an argument sit on the shelf above their eye line. The height difference is the whole rule, chosen on purpose rather than left to whatever shelf happened to be empty.

Notice what none of these examples do. None of them buy new storage, and none of them ask anyone to use less. They move what was already there into a different spot, based on one question asked honestly: how often does this actually get used.

What this is not

This is not the same finding as too many steps, which is about how many separate actions a task takes once an item is in hand, opening a lid, unlatching a bin, carrying something to another room. A thing can be perfectly placed at eye level and still take four steps to put away if the container itself is fussy. It is also not the same as inadequate capacity, where the honest problem is that nothing that size will ever fit no matter how it is arranged. A zone can have empty shelf space and still fail this test, because the empty space is in the wrong place relative to what is used most.

A note on aging in place and changing bodies

The reach band described above assumes an adult standing at an average counter height, and no single number is right for every household. A wheelchair user's reach band sits lower and narrower. A young child's sits lower still and needs a step for anything above it. A shoulder or a back that does not move the way it used to shortens the band from the top down. The rule does not change, match height to frequency, only the measurements do. If mobility in the home is changing, that is worth planning around directly rather than working around indefinitely, and a home is allowed to be re-measured for the people actually living in it today rather than the people who set it up years ago.

The one question before anything goes back on a shelf

The fix does not require new bins, new labels, or a weekend project. It requires one honest question asked about each item as it goes back after a reset: how often does this actually leave this spot. Not how important it feels, not what category it belongs to, just the honest frequency. Put the daily answers in the reach band, the weekly answers just outside it, and let everything answered "a few times a year" earn its place on the high shelf without guilt, because that is exactly what the high shelf is for.

Before you start. This is guidance for organising and cleaning a home. It is not instruction for electrical, gas, structural, or any other licensed work. Do not lift or move anything unsafe, do not mix cleaning products, and follow the label on anything you use. If a job needs a professional, that is the right answer. See the full safety notice.

Common questions

Why is the thing I use most often always the hardest to get to?

Because storage usually gets filled by what fits, not by what gets used. The first things put away claim the easy spots, eye level, arm's reach, the front of the shelf, and whatever is left over goes wherever there is still room. Nobody plans it that way. It is just the order things happened to land in, and the order things land in has nothing to do with how often you reach for them.

Is poor accessibility the same thing as not having enough space?

No. A zone can have plenty of room and still fail this test, because the problem is not how much space exists, it is which spot within that space a given item occupies. A cabinet with empty room on the bottom shelf and the everyday mugs stacked on the top one is not short on capacity. It is arranged by whatever order things went in rather than by how often each one comes back out.

How do I decide what goes at eye level and arm's reach?

Rank by frequency, not by category or by size. The three or four things you touch daily get the zone between your shoulder and your waist, the true no stretch, no stool, no bending band. Weekly items go just above or below that band. Anything used a few times a year, the punch bowl, the fondue set, the good tablecloth, is the only honest reason to use a top shelf or a step stool at all.

What about items that are heavy as well as frequently used?

Weight overrides frequency for where something sits vertically, even when it does not change how central the spot is. A heavy stockpot used weekly still belongs low, at or below waist height, lifted from a squat rather than reached down from above with a straight back. Frequency decides how central a spot is. Weight decides how high off the floor that spot is allowed to be.

Which of the six S's actually fixes this?

Straighten, the second step. Sort decides what belongs in a zone at all. Straighten decides exactly where within that zone each thing lives, and that is the step where reach, height, and how often something is used are supposed to be weighed against each other. A zone that has been sorted but not straightened this way often looks organized and still fails a reader on the fourth or fifth day.
