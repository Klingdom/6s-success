The Bulk and Overhead Storage

The bulk and overhead storage, in the Garage. Holds the things you genuinely need once or twice a year, high up and out of the way, without becoming the place decisions go to hide. Every overhead bin labelled in letters you can read standing on the slab, a last-opened month and year on the end of each one, the heaviest bins on the lowest rack, nothing overhanging the parking bay, and a small map taped beside the light switch.

One session: 60-90 min, most of it in Sort, and every bin has to come down to the floor to be judged.

The short version

  1. Sort. Bring the bins down to the floor before you judge them
  2. Straighten. Heavy bins go on the lowest rack and light bulky things, folding chairs
  3. Shine. Wipe the lids, the depth of dust on an overhead lid tells you exactly how
  4. Safety. Nothing goes overhead that you cannot lift down with both hands on a stable ladder
  5. Standardize. Every bin gets a large label you can read from the slab plus the month
  6. Sustain. The moment a bin is on the floor with its lid off

Each step in full, with what to have on hand and what done looks like, below.

What done looks like

Every overhead bin labelled in letters you can read standing on the slab, a last-opened month and year on the end of each one, the heaviest bins on the lowest rack, nothing overhanging the parking bay, and a small map taped beside the light switch.

Check these before you start

  • FallWorking from a ladder rocking on a sloped slab, or coming down with a bin in both arms and no free hand for the stile.
  • Fall, cut, or crushAn overloaded rack, or bins stacked so they overhang the rails directly above where people get in and out of a car.

Which of these is true here?

Match what you are actually seeing in the bulk and overhead storage to why it keeps happening, then start at the pass that fixes that, not the top of the list.

I have to get a ladder and open three bins to find one thing

  • Labels are too small to read standing on the slab. Confirm in 30 seconds: Open the door and, without moving anything, name what is at the back. If you cannot, that stock is invisible. If that checks out, start at Straighten.
  • Bins are grouped by what things are, not by when they come down. Confirm in 30 seconds: Ask two people what this surface should look like at bedtime. Two answers means there is no standard to keep. If that checks out, start at Standardize.
  • There is no map by the light switch. Confirm in 30 seconds: Fetch it once and count the movements. More than two and the storage is charging you rent every time. If that checks out, start at Straighten.

There are bins up there nobody has opened in years

  • Nothing records when each bin was last opened. Confirm in 30 seconds: Name the moment it happens. If the answer is "when I get round to it", there is no trigger and it will not survive a bad week. If that checks out, start at Sustain.
  • They hold things kept for what they represent. Confirm in 30 seconds: Ask if you would buy this again today for what it does. If the answer is no but you still cannot decide, the hold is sentimental. If that checks out, start at Sort.
  • They hold a decision that has been postponed by putting it up high. Confirm in 30 seconds: Pick up one piece. If your first thought is a verb (pay, sign, call, decide) rather than a place, it needs a decision, not a home. If that checks out, start at Sort.

Getting a bin down is a two-person job, or a risky one

  • The heaviest bins are on the highest rack. Confirm in 30 seconds: Look for the four: a blade your hand would meet, weight above shoulder height, heat beside something that burns, and an unsafe placement near a child's reach. If that checks out, start at Safety.
  • Bins are packed to a weight nobody can lower at arm's length. Confirm in 30 seconds: Count what you used in the past month. If the honest number is a third of what is stored here, the space is not too small. If that checks out, start at Sort.
  • There is no stable ladder, so a chair or a bumper gets used. Confirm in 30 seconds: Have the shortest person who uses this zone fetch what they need daily. If they climb, reach overhead, or ask for help, the height is wrong. If that checks out, start at Safety.

Something up there overhangs where the car parks

  • The rack was loaded past its own footprint. Confirm in 30 seconds: Put back only what belongs here. If it still will not close, or still stacks four deep, the capacity is the problem, not the habit. If that checks out, start at Sort.
  • Nothing marks how far the bins may come forward. Confirm in 30 seconds: Ask two people what this surface should look like at bedtime. Two answers means there is no standard to keep. If that checks out, start at Standardize.
  • It has been like that so long nobody sees it when parking. Confirm in 30 seconds: Ask someone who does not live here to name what looks out of place. If they see it instantly and you had stopped noticing, this is the cause. If that checks out, start at Sort.

Only have 15 minutes? Relabel every overhead bin in letters you can read standing on the slab, write the month and year on the end of each one you open, move the heaviest bins to the lowest rack, and tape a map of what is where beside the light switch. Victory: Labels readable from the floor, a date on every bin, the heaviest ones lowest, and a map by the light switch.

The six passes, in order

Work them in this order. Sorting after you have arranged things means arranging things you were about to remove.

This zone's full kit list is below, after the method. Skim it first if you would rather gather everything before you start.

Sort

Decide what stays. Everything else leaves the zone now.

Bring the bins down to the floor before you judge them, because nobody sorts honestly from the third step of a ladder with one arm through a lid. Start with the holiday bin that has not come down in three Decembers and the box marked "misc".

Straighten

Give what stays a home, placed where the hand actually reaches.

Heavy bins go on the lowest rack and light bulky things, folding chairs and the artificial tree, go highest. Group by the time of year they come down, so one December trip up the ladder brings the lights, the tree, and the wrapping bin down together.

Shine

Clean it, and use the cleaning to inspect what you cannot see when it is full.

Wipe the lids. The depth of dust on an overhead lid tells you exactly how many years it has been since anything came out of that bin. While you are up there, look at the rack underside and the ceiling above it for the brown stain that means the roof is letting water in.

Surface by surface, all 7 of them, with what to use on each.

Safety

Make the zone safe for everybody who uses it, including the shortest person.

Nothing goes overhead that you cannot lift down with both hands on a stable ladder, and nothing you might need in the dark or in a hurry. Check that the rack bolts land in joists, not just in the plasterboard, and that loaded bins sit fully inside the rails rather than overhanging the roof of a parked car.

Standardize

Write down the best way you know today, so it survives you forgetting.

Every bin gets a large label you can read from the slab plus the month and year it was last opened. A map taped beside the light switch says which bay holds what, so nobody climbs a ladder simply to find out.

Sustain

Attach the reset to something that already happens, so it keeps itself.

The moment a bin is on the floor with its lid off, write the new month and year on the end before it goes back up. Ten seconds with the marker that lives by the switch. The dates read from the slab, so slip is a bin whose newest date is two Decembers old, and that bin comes down at the next clear-out rather than being lifted straight back. Height protects the drift up here. Checking costs a ladder, so a bin can hold an unmade decision for years without anybody feeling it.

The box you have never opened

There is at least one box up there that moved in with you and has not been opened since. You keep it because opening it costs an afternoon and might cost more than that. Here is what settles it. Bring it down, open it on the floor with three things beside you: a rubbish sack, a donation box, and one empty container. Whatever you truly want to keep has to fit inside that single container and has to have a living person's name attached to it. Anything you cannot name an owner for goes today. Do not tape it up and send it back overhead, because a box that goes back unopened has just bought itself another five years and taught the rack that this is allowed.

Cleaning it properly, surface by surface

The depth of dust on a lid tells you how long a bin has sat untouched. While you are up there, read the roof deck above the rack for the brown stain that means water is getting in.

Work down, not across. Whatever comes off a high surface lands on the one below it, so cleaning the floor first means cleaning it twice.

Carry these in with you. Compact vacuum with attachments; Color-coded microfiber cloth set; Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; Portable cleaning caddy; Extendable duster; Stable step ladder.

  1. The roof deck and ceiling above the rack
    From a stable step, wipe the underside of the roof deck and the ceiling above the rack and clear the cobwebs, so a new stain would show against clean board.
  2. The rack frame and its underside
    Wipe the rails and the underside of the rack with a damp cloth, working the settled dust off the steel.
  3. The rack shelf decks under the bins
    Once a level is cleared, wipe the plywood or wire deck the bins sit on with a damp cloth, since grit ground under a base scratches the bin and hides damp.
  4. The bin lids
    Dust each lid with an extendable duster or a cloth, noting the depth of dust as you go, since it tells you how many years since anything came out of that bin.
  5. The bin exteriors and labels
    Wipe the bin sides and the label face so the letters read from the slab, and update the last-opened month if you have opened one.
  6. The rack uprights and bolt plates
    Wipe down the uprights and clear the grit from the bolt plates where the rack meets the wall and the ceiling.
  7. The slab under the parking bay edge
    Sweep and vacuum the floor under the rack, especially the strip right at the edge of the parking bay.

How to clean anything else in the house, surface by surface.

Inspect as you clean. Cleaning is the only time you see the zone empty, so use it.

  • A brown water stain spreading on the roof deck, the rack underside, or the ceiling, the sign the roof is letting water in.
  • A bin gone soft, stained, or damp at its base, and any musty smell when you lift a lid.
  • Rust, or a bend or sag, in a rack upright or a rail carrying the load.
  • A rack bolt that has drawn part-way out of its hole, or that lands in plasterboard rather than a joist, and rodent droppings sitting on a lid.

The standard that keeps it fixed

Every overhead bin is labelled to be read from the slab and carries the date it was last opened.

Reset trigger. When a bin comes down, the new date goes on the end of it before it goes back up.

How much this bulk and overhead storage can actually hold

Every overhead bin labelled in letters you can read standing on the slab, a last-opened month and year on the end of each one, the heaviest bins on the lowest rack, nothing overhanging the parking bay, and a small map taped beside the light switch.

The last-opened date is the capacity rule, and it is the most honest one in the house. A bin that has not been opened in two years is not storage, it is a decision nobody made, and adding another rack simply buys that decision more time. Nothing overhangs the parking bay, ever: a bin that clears the roof of the car today does not clear the next car, and the cost of being wrong is measured in windscreens or heads. Run the honest count.

If your bulk and overhead storage is not like this

The passes above assume a fairly ordinary bulk and overhead storage. A few real households are not, and the fix is not to skip the method, it is to run it against a different starting shape.

No overhead racks, only shelving or a loft hatch
The labels and the dates do the same work on a shelf, and the map matters more, because a loft swallows things far more completely than a rack does. Keep the heaviest boxes at waist height, not overhead and not on the floor, because both ends of that range are where backs get hurt.
Genuinely seasonal storage, such as decorations or camping
Group by the occasion it comes down for rather than by what the things are, because that is how you will go looking for it. One bin that holds everything for one weekend beats four bins sorted by material, and the last-opened date will tell you honestly whether that weekend still happens.
