Primary Bathroom micro zone

The Medicine Cabinet

To hold the medicine and first aid you would need tonight, and nothing you stopped needing years ago.

One session: 45-75 min. most of it in Sort, reading labels and dates one box at a time

What done looks like

Three labeled bins, pain relief, stomach and cold, first aid, each holding only in-date items. Prescriptions grouped by the name on the label, the first aid bin small enough to carry one-handed to whoever is bleeding, and the whole cabinet above a child's reach with a catch on it if children are in the house.

The six passes, in order

Work them in this order. Sorting after you have arranged things means arranging things you were about to remove.

1. Sort

Decide what stays. Everything else leaves the zone now.

Read every label and every date, including the small print on the boxes you kept because the box was still good. Anything expired, anything unlabeled, and anything prescribed for a condition nobody here has now goes straight into a bag for the pharmacy take-back.

2. Straighten

Give what stays a home, placed where the hand actually reaches.

Group by the reason you come to this cabinet: headache, stomach, cut, cold, eyes. Put the two things you reach for at two in the morning on the shelf you can find without turning the light on.

3. Shine

Clean it, and use the cleaning to inspect what you cannot see when it is full.

Take the shelves out and wash off the sticky rings left by cough syrup and the dust of old blister foil. Dry them all the way through before they go back, because a damp shelf is what turns tablets chalky in here.

4. Safety

Make the zone safe for everybody who uses it, including the shortest person.

Bathroom humidity breaks tablets and capsules down faster than a bedroom shelf does, so move anything you take daily out of this room entirely. Whatever stays gets a child-secure catch, and nothing ever goes down the toilet.

5. Standardize

Write down the best way you know today, so it survives you forgetting.

Bins are labeled by symptom rather than brand, so a guest or a sitter can find what they need without asking which box the cold tablets are in. The first aid bin holds plasters, tape, gauze, and one pair of scissors, and it never becomes a home for spare vitamins.

6. Sustain

Attach the reset to something that already happens, so it keeps itself.

Every time you come home from the pharmacy, the new box goes into its bin and you read the dates of everything already in that bin before you shut the door. The take-back bag then rides back with you on the next trip.

The leftover prescription

There is a half-course of antibiotics in here, or a bottle of strong painkillers from a procedure two years ago, and you keep it because it might come in useful and because handing it back feels like waste. It is the single most common thing in this cabinet that should not be in it. The rule: a medicine belongs to a condition someone in this house has now, prescribed to that person, in date, with the label intact. Everything else goes in the take-back bag today, including the one you are quietly telling yourself is still fine. Leftover painkillers sitting behind a mirror are the most likely thing in this room to seriously hurt a visitor, and nothing about not wasting them outweighs that.

Check these before you start

  • Poison, choke, or strangle Loose tablets, gummy vitamins, and pushed-through blister packs at a height a child reaches by standing on a closed toilet lid. Measure the reach from what they can climb, not from the floor.
  • Fall, cut, or crush A glass peroxide bottle or a heavy first aid tin balanced on the top shelf above a hard basin. Heavy and breakable belongs at eye level, not overhead.

Cleaning it properly

Take the shelves out, wash off the sticky rings, and dry everything through before it goes back, because damp is what turns tablets chalky in this room. Clean it in a light bright enough to read every label as you go.

Inspect as you clean. Cleaning is the only time you see the zone empty, so use it.

  • Rust freckling on the metal shelf clips, the hinges, or a steel cabinet back, the tell that humidity is sitting in here.
  • Black mildew spotting in a corner seam or along the underside of a shelf.
  • Condensation or a cloudy bloom trapped behind the mirror, a sign of moisture getting behind the glass.
  • A shelf that has bowed, or a shelf clip pulling loose from its pin hole under the weight.
  • A hairline crack in a glass shelf, or a door catch that no longer holds the door shut.

The standard that keeps it fixed

Only in-date, correctly labeled, currently needed medicine lives here, grouped by symptom and kept above child reach.

Reset trigger. Every trip home from the pharmacy. The new box goes in, the dates in that bin get read, and the take-back bag goes back with you next time.

Before you start. This is guidance for organising and cleaning a home. It is not instruction for electrical, gas, structural, or any other licensed work. Do not lift or move anything unsafe, do not mix cleaning products, and follow the label on anything you use. If a job needs a professional, that is the right answer. See the full safety notice.
