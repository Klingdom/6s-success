The Automotive Care Zone

The car care supplies, in the Garage. Keeps the fluids, cables, and cleaning kit for the cars you actually own upright, labelled, and separate from everything the household touches. Every fluid bottle standing upright on a clean drip tray with its opened date written on the shoulder, jumper cables and tyre gauge together in one bag by the door, rags in a closed metal can, and not a single bottle for a car nobody here drives.

One session: 45-75 min, most of it in Sort, reading labels bottle by bottle.

The short version

  1. Sort. Line every bottle up and read the label out loud
  2. Straighten. Fluids stand upright on a shallow drip tray at waist height
  3. Shine. Wipe every bottle shoulder and check the tray for the ring
  4. Safety. Petrol and any can that has ever held it stays out of the house envelope
  5. Standardize. Label the tray shelf with exactly what belongs on it, and mark every bottle
  6. Sustain. You already lift the bonnet to check the oil

Each step in full, with what to have on hand and what done looks like, below.

Every fluid bottle standing upright on a clean drip tray with its opened date written on the shoulder, jumper cables and tyre gauge together in one bag by the door, rags in a closed metal can, and not a single bottle for a car nobody here drives.

The six passes, in order

Work them in this order. Sorting after you have arranged things means arranging things you were about to remove.

Sort

Decide what stays. Everything else leaves the zone now.

Line every bottle up and read the label out loud. Oil in a weight your current engine does not take, coolant for the van you sold, and the half bottle whose label has soaked away into unreadable grey all come out of the row.

What to store it in, now that you know what you are keeping

Bins and shelves are for what Sort left behind, not a stand-in for it. Buying storage before sorting means buying a container for things you have not yet decided to keep.

Straighten

Give what stays a home, placed where the hand actually reaches.

Fluids stand upright on a shallow drip tray at waist height, where a weeping cap shows as a ring rather than soaking into a shelf. Jumper cables, tyre gauge, and lug wrench live together in one bag right by the door, because you go for those with a dead engine, in the dark, in a hurry.

Shine

Clean it, and use the cleaning to inspect what you cannot see when it is full.

Wipe every bottle shoulder and check the tray for the ring that says something is seeping. Wash the wash mitts and microfibre towels on their own, never with house laundry, so grit and old wax stay out of the good towels.

Surface by surface, all 7 of them, with what to use on each.

Safety

Make the zone safe for everybody who uses it, including the shortest person.

Petrol and any can that has ever held it stays out of the house envelope, capped tight, and never on a shelf near the water heater's pilot flame. Polish and oil rags go into a metal can with a lid, not the plastic bin.

Standardize

Write down the best way you know today, so it survives you forgetting.

Label the tray shelf with exactly what belongs on it, and mark every bottle with the month you opened it. Brake fluid pulls water out of the air and coolant goes off once the seal is broken, and the date is the only way you will ever know which is which.

Sustain

Attach the reset to something that already happens, so it keeps itself.

You already lift the bonnet to check the oil. While it is up, look along the tray for a fresh ring, and an empty bottle goes into the bin instead of back into the row. Twenty seconds, by whoever does the oil check. A ring on the tray is the leak signal, which is why the tray is pale, and the second check is ownership, because a bottle for a car nobody here drives has no reason to stay. Dates matter more here than anywhere, since brake fluid and coolant both change once opened.

The fluids for the car you no longer own

The hardest bottles here are the two thirds full ones. They cost real money, they have not gone off, and they fit nothing on your driveway. The rule is that every bottle must match a vehicle parked at this address, and no match means no keep. Offer them this week to the neighbour who drives that model, and take whatever is unclaimed to your council's household hazardous waste collection, never down a drain. Then write the opened date on every bottle from today onward, because the only reason this shelf grew this deep is that nobody could tell which ones were still good, so nothing was ever thrown out.

Cleaning it properly, surface by surface

Wipe every bottle shoulder and read the drip tray as you go, because the tray tells you what is seeping before the floor does. Car cloths and mitts wash on their own, never with the house laundry.

Work down, not across. Whatever comes off a high surface lands on the one below it, so cleaning the floor first means cleaning it twice.

Carry these in with you. Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; Color-coded microfiber cloth set; Compact vacuum with attachments; Portable cleaning caddy; Oil-absorbent granules.

  1. The shelf of fluid bottles
    Lift each bottle, wipe its shoulder and cap where oil and coolant weep down, and stand it back squarely on the tray with its dated shoulder facing out.
  2. The shelf board under the bottles and tray
    With the bottles and tray lifted off, wipe the shelf board itself with the neutral pH cleaner, lifting the greasy film and any fluid that has crept under the tray unseen.
  3. The drip tray under the bottles
    Slide the tray out, wipe it clean, and read what is on it as you go, since a fresh ring shows exactly which bottle has started to seep.
  4. The jumper cables and tyre gauge bag
    Wipe the cable clamps and coil the leads back into their bag, then wipe the gauge before it goes in beside them.
  5. The metal rag can
    Wipe the outside of the can and check the lid closes flat, leaving the oily rags sealed inside it rather than loose on the shelf.
  6. The wash mitts and microfibre towels
    Wash the mitts and drying towels on their own load, never with house laundry, so old wax and grit stay out of clean fabric, and dry them fully before they go back.
  7. The slab where the car parks and drips
    Sweep the bay, then work oil-absorbent granules into any fresh spots on the concrete, leave them to draw the oil up, and sweep them off before wiping the area.

How to clean anything else in the house, surface by surface.

Inspect as you clean. Cleaning is the only time you see the zone empty, so use it.

  • A fresh ring on the drip tray under one bottle, the sign it has started to seep.
  • A sweet-smelling puddle anywhere on the slab, which is leaking antifreeze and deadly to a pet or a child.
  • An oil stain spreading on the concrete under where the car sits, telling you the car itself is dropping fluid.
  • A bottle whose printed label has washed or faded illegible, and any rust weeping from the base of the rag can.

The standard that keeps it fixed

Every fluid stands upright on the tray, wears an opened date, and belongs to a car on this driveway.

Reset trigger. When the bonnet is already up for the oil check, that is when the tray gets its look.
