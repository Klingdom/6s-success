## 4. Posting cadence & format

- **3 posts/week** — Monday, Wednesday, Friday — between 7:30–9:00 AM Mountain Time
- **One pillar per post.** Don't mix.
- **Format:** Hook (one sentence) → setup (2–3 sentences) → insight (1–2 sentences) → implied CTA (1 sentence). Total under 80 words for short posts; under 200 for longer.
- **No emojis.** No hashtag stuffing — 1 or 2 max if any.
- **No "thoughts?" at the end.** Make a claim. Let the comments come.
- **Native LinkedIn — no link posts.** Drop links in the first comment if needed.

### When to break the format
- **Long form (300–500 words)** once every two weeks — a worked example or a teardown of a real process. These convert highest to DMs.
- **Carousel** (6–8 slides) once a month — a framework breakdown. Reuse a visual from the book. Cover slide should be the strongest single statement on a clean background.

## 5. Starter post bank — 15 posts across the 5 pillars

Copy-paste-ready. Edit the specifics, but the structure works.

### Pillar 1 — The Gap (between documented and actual)

**Post 1**
> Your SOP says 5 steps. Your team takes 17.
>
> The other 12? Not in the documentation. They're workarounds your senior person developed two years ago and never wrote down.
>
> That gap is where rework lives. It's where new hires get stuck. It's where audits fail.
>
> You can't close it from a Notion doc. You close it by watching what actually happens.

**Post 2**
> New hire joins. Reads the SOP. Follows it. Gets stuck on step 3.
>
> A teammate walks over and says "oh yeah, you also have to do this other thing first."
>
> Welcome to the gap between documented and actual.
>
> The fix isn't a longer SOP. It's an honest one — built from what the team does, not what someone wrote down two years ago.

**Post 3**
> The person who actually knows how the process works is leaving in two weeks.
>
> You're going to interview them, take notes, draft an SOP. They'll review it (sort of). You'll publish it. Three months later it's already wrong.
>
> Or: have them walk through the work one time, recorded. Done. The next person follows what actually happens.

### Pillar 2 — The Hidden Math of Time

**Post 4**
> A typical office process: 35 minutes of actual work hidden inside 3 business days of cycle time.
>
> That's 1.5% value-added. The other 98.5% is queues, approvals, handoffs, outboxes.
>
> Lean's first insight is that the waste isn't in the work. It's in the gaps between the work.
>
> Measure your cycle time. Then measure your work time. The ratio will surprise you.

**Post 5**
> Cycle time is what the customer experiences.
>
> Process time is what your team is actually doing.
>
> They're rarely close. In most processes I see, the ratio is 30:1 or worse.
>
> If you only measure one, measure cycle time. That's the one the customer feels.

**Post 6**
> TAKT time = available production time ÷ customer demand.
>
> If your customer needs 280 units per day and you have 420 working minutes, TAKT is 90 seconds.
>
> If your actual cycle is 120 seconds, you can't keep up. Adding overtime won't fix it. Adding people might, but probably not. Eliminating waste from the 120 always works.
>
> Math beats opinion.

### Pillar 3 — Tools, Used Correctly

**Post 7**
> The Cause and Effect Matrix is the most under-used tool in Lean.
>
> It does one thing: takes your list of 30 process inputs and tells you which 5 actually matter. Score correlation 0/1/4/9, multiply by output importance 1–10, sort by total. Done in 90 minutes.
>
> Then those 5 inputs are all you take into FMEA. The rest gets parked.
>
> Most teams skip this step and FMEA everything. Don't.

**Post 8**
> FMEA is not a spreadsheet exercise.
>
> Severity × Occurrence × Detection = RPN. Sort descending. Anything over 200 gets action this Kaizen Event.
>
> Severity ≥ 9 always gets action regardless of RPN.
>
> Re-score after the action. The drop in RPN is your improvement, measurable in one number per failure mode. That's the whole game.

**Post 9**
> SIPOC takes 45 minutes. Most teams skip it because it looks too simple.
>
> Then halfway through Day 2 they realize they've been mapping the wrong process. Or they disagree on where the process starts. Or they forgot a major customer.
>
> Suppliers · Inputs · Process · Outputs · Customers. Five columns. Do it first. Every time.

### Pillar 4 — Sustaining the Gains

**Post 10**
> Most Kaizen Events deliver real improvements. Most of those improvements are gone in six months.
>
> The reason isn't the team or the work. It's that nobody wrote the Control Plan.
>
> The Control Plan is the post-event contract: which inputs we measure, what spec they need to hit, who measures, how often, what we do when they drift.
>
> No Control Plan = no sustained gain. Plan accordingly.

**Post 11**
> A consultant runs your Kaizen Event, leaves, and your process drifts back in six months. You blame the team.
>
> The real failure is that the Process Owner never got a Control Plan with reaction rules, audit schedules, and named accountability.
>
> If your consultant didn't hand you that document at close-out, the engagement wasn't actually done.

**Post 12**
> Standard Work has a half-life of about six weeks.
>
> That's how long it takes for a procedure to drift from what someone wrote to what people actually do.
>
> The fix isn't to write better procedures. It's to make the act of updating Standard Work part of the daily standup. When the work changes, the doc changes. Same day.
>
> If your SOPs need a "review cycle," they're already wrong.

### Pillar 5 — The Event Format

**Post 13**
> The 5-day Kaizen Event format works because it forces three things:
>
> 1. The right people in the room — process owner, operators, the customer-facing person
> 2. A timebox that prevents endless analysis
> 3. Implemented improvements by Friday, not a slide deck
>
> Skip any of those three and it's not a Kaizen Event. It's a workshop. Workshops produce reports. Events produce change.

**Post 14**
> The most important meeting in a Kaizen Event is the 15-minute retrospective on Friday.
>
> Three columns: what went well, what didn't, what we'll change.
>
> The third column is the only one that matters. Every "what didn't" needs a named owner and a specific change for next time.
>
> Without it, you do the same Kaizen the same way next month with the same problems.

**Post 15**
> A 5-day Kaizen Event with the wrong people is worse than no event at all.
>
> The right people: the Process Owner who has authority, the Sponsor who has budget, and the operators who actually do the work. Not their managers. The operators.
>
> If you can't get those three in a room for five days, fix the calendar before you fix the process.

## 6. Engagement plays

The math: 70% of consulting leads from LinkedIn come from comments and DMs, not from the post itself.

### Comment strategy
- **20 minutes per posting day:** comment on posts from 10–15 people in your ICP (operations leaders, COOs, CI heads). Substantive comments — add a Lean insight, share a counter-example, ask a precise question.
- **Avoid the "Great post!" loop.** It signals low value. Make it look like you read it.
- **Comment on your own post 1–2 hours after publishing.** Add a follow-up thought or a contrarian take. This boosts reach and continues the conversation.

### Following strategy
- Follow 5 new ICP people per posting day. After 90 days you'll have ~200 new connections in your ideal audience.
- Don't connect cold. Like, comment, then connect — in that order.

### DM strategy
When someone in your ICP comments on a post or reacts to multiple posts in a row:

> Hey [name] — appreciated your comment on [topic]. Curious — is [pain point from their context] something you're actively working on, or just thinking about? Happy to share what I've seen work in similar setups, no pitch.

Three things this does: references them specifically, names a pain, gives them an exit. The "no pitch" tag does heavy lifting.

### Discovery call funnel
- DM → 20-min discovery call (Calendly link)
- Discovery call → scoped proposal (PDF, sent within 48 hours)
- Proposal → engagement

Aim for 1 discovery call per 15 DMs sent. 1 engagement per 4 discovery calls. So 60 well-targeted DMs over a quarter should yield one solid engagement at the start, climbing from there as content compounds.

## 8. The lead magnet

You have a book. Use it.

- Offer a free chapter (Chapter 4 — Define Process is the most actionable standalone chapter) in exchange for an email signup
- Build a 1-page Kaizen Event Readiness Checklist (pulled from the inputs lists in the book) and offer it as a PDF download
- These get gated behind a simple email capture and feed a quarterly newsletter

The newsletter is where DM conversations get warmed up. Most of your eventual engagements will have been on the list for 4–6 months before they reach out.

## 9. What to measure (and what to ignore)

### Track weekly
- DMs received from people in your ICP
- Discovery calls booked
- New connections in your target roles

### Track monthly
- Profile views from ICP companies
- Engagement-to-DM conversion rate
- Newsletter signups
- Pipeline value of active conversations

### Ignore
- Total followers (vanity, not aligned to revenue)
- Likes (no signal)
- Impressions on individual posts (high variance, low signal)
- Post performance in the first 24 hours (the algorithm is not your customer)

## 10. The 90-day plan

### Days 1–14: Foundation
- Refresh profile (headline, About, Featured)
- Pin the strongest post from the starter bank
- Build the email-capture lead magnet
- Identify 50 ICP accounts to follow and engage with

### Days 15–60: Build the rhythm
- 3 posts/week from the starter bank, customized
- 20 minutes of comments per posting day
- 5 DMs per week to engaged commenters
- First 5–10 discovery calls

### Days 60–90: Tune and convert
- Identify your highest-converting pillar; weight content toward it
- First engagement closed (target: 1 by Day 90)
- Newsletter launched off the email list
- Begin writing the second piece of long-form content (a teardown of a real process, anonymized)

### After Day 90
This compounds. Consulting funnels on LinkedIn take 4–6 months to start producing reliable inbound. Don't quit at month 3. The work you publish in month 2 will close engagements in month 8.
