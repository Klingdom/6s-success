# Chapter 41 Quotes: The Primary Bathroom

The chapter's frozen strings, byte-verbatim. Every quote card and pull quote must match these exactly.

## The rule (wet room; three problems; chemistry needs time)
> The primary bathroom is the smallest room you use the most, and it is the first wet room in this book, which means it fights three problems no other room faces all at once: standing moisture that breeds mould, open chemistry that has to be kept safe, and dates that quietly pass while you sleep. So the method changes here. You spray the tub, the shower, and the bowl first and let the cleaner do the standing about while you work the dry surfaces, because chemistry needs time, not scrubbing. You treat drying as the real job, not wiping, because mould is a water problem. And you read the date on everything as you go, because in this room an expired thing is not stock, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf.

## The seven zones
> The primary bathroom is seven small zones, not one big job: the vanity counter, the vanity drawers, the under-sink cabinet, the medicine cabinet, the shower or tub, the toilet area, and the linen and towel storage. Do them one at a time, and start at the vanity counter, because it is the surface you look at twice a day and clearing it changes what the whole room looks like from the doorway.

## Spray first, let the chemistry work
> The wet-room surfaces are cleaned with time, not muscle, so the order of work is the opposite of scrubbing. You spray the tub, the shower walls, and the bowl before you touch anything else, and then you leave them: you work the counter, the drawers, and the cabinets while the cleaner does the standing about, breaking down the soap film and the scale and the ring on its own. When you come back, it wipes off in one pass instead of ten. You work top down, you squeegee the glass and the tile last so nothing dries into a film, and you leave the fan running after you walk out.

## Dry beats mould
> Mould and the soft pink film are not a dirt problem, they are a water problem, so the winning move in this room is not cleaning, it is drying. A surface that dries out between uses does not grow mould, and a surface that stays wet grows it back faster every week however hard you scrub. So you squeegee the glass, you stand the bath mat on edge to drain rather than leaving it flat and wet, you hang the towels where air actually reaches them, you buff the metal dry so it does not spot, and you run the fan and leave the door open until the room has dried out. The cloth cleans what is there; the drying stops it coming back.

## Read the date, expired is not stock
> Every product in this room has a date, and in here a date that has passed is not a supply you are saving, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf: the separated sunscreen, the crumbling tablet, the mascara from two summers ago. So you read the date on every Sort pass, on the creams and the medicines and the cosmetics alike, and what has turned goes, because keeping it is paying storage on a decision that already went wrong. Medicines go to a pharmacy take-back, never down the toilet and never into the bin loose, and the leftover half-course of antibiotics is the single most common thing in the cabinet that should not be there.

## Chemistry, water, and electricity
> This is the room where cleaning products, medicines, water, and electricity all share a few square feet, so the safety rules are firm. You keep one product per bowl and you never mix them, because bowl cleaner and bleach together make a gas, and one cleaner does the job alone. You store the bleach, the drain opener, and every medicine locked or high above a child's climbing reach, measured from the closed toilet lid they can stand on rather than from the floor. Nothing stays plugged in while there is water standing in the basin, and no charger runs up to the cistern top. And you keep the plumbing under the sink in plain sight, because a joint that weeps unseen for months turns a cheap washer into a floor that has to come up.

## A finished primary bathroom
> A finished primary bathroom is one you would be happy to hand a guest at an hour's notice: a clear counter holding one tray of daily few, a mirror and glass with no film, a shower that dries out between uses with no dark line where the wall meets the tray, a bowl clean under the rim, drawers and cabinets holding only what is in use and in date, the cleaners and medicines locked or high above a child's climbing reach, the plumbing under the sink in plain sight, and every towel on the shelf clean and dry.

## One Idea to Keep
> A bathroom is not clean because you scrubbed it hard. It is clean because you sprayed first and let the chemistry work, dried the surfaces so the mould cannot come back, read the date on everything and let the failures go, and locked the chemistry safely away. Do one zone this weekend, the counter or the shower, and give yourself the smallest room you use the most, actually clean and actually safe.

## Before and after
> This is the bathroom the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, the counter clear, the glass and mirror filmless, the shower drying out with no dark seam, the bowl clean under the rim, and the chemistry and medicines locked safely up out of reach. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard, because a bathroom you could hand a guest at an hour's notice is the one that stays good, and the photo is what it has to match after every reset.
