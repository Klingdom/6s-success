## The bathroom Shine layer (nine rules from Ch 31 PLUS)
- **Spray first, dwell, do not scrub**: spray the tub, shower, and bowl before anything else and let the cleaner work while you do the dry surfaces; chemistry needs time, not muscle; squeegee last, fan on.
- **Dry beats mould**: mould and the pink film are a water problem, so the winning move is drying, not wiping; a surface that dries between uses does not grow mould.
- **Read the date, expired is not stock**: a date that has passed is a failure on your shelf, not a supply; medicines to a pharmacy take-back, never the toilet.
- **Open chemistry, kept safe**: one product per bowl and never mixed; cleaners and medicines locked or high above a child's climbing reach; keep the plumbing visible.

## Seven zones (start at the vanity counter, the surface you look at twice a day)
1. **Vanity Counter** - the daily few on one tray; mirror and switch plate first, then tray, tap, and stone; the faucet collar and switch plate nobody wipes.
2. **Vanity Drawers** - divided, day-ordered; the dirt hides underneath, so lift the dividers and clean the bottom and runners; razor blades capped.
3. **Under-Sink Cabinet** - store around the plumbing without hiding a leak; a pale drip tray, one open bottle per job, the trap and shut-off valves in plain sight.
4. **Medicine Cabinet** - only what you would need tonight and in date; shelves out, sticky rings off, dry it through, above a child's reach with a catch.
5. **Shower or Tub** - spray and dwell first; work top down, squeegee the glass, mat on edge to drain, the caulk seam and grout given real dwell time.
6. **Toilet Area** - treat the bowl and let it sit; work the outside cleanest to dirtiest, one cloth one colour this job only; the strip behind the pedestal.
7. **Linen and Towel Storage** - clean-and-dry towels only; clear the shelves, press the back wall for damp, anything sour back through a hot wash.

## FROZEN STRINGS (byte-verbatim in manuscript AND final.html)

### Seven zones
The primary bathroom is seven small zones, not one big job: the vanity counter, the vanity drawers, the under-sink cabinet, the medicine cabinet, the shower or tub, the toilet area, and the linen and towel storage. Do them one at a time, and start at the vanity counter, because it is the surface you look at twice a day and clearing it changes what the whole room looks like from the doorway.

### The rule (wet room; three problems; chemistry needs time)
The primary bathroom is the smallest room you use the most, and it is the first wet room in this book, which means it fights three problems no other room faces all at once: standing moisture that breeds mould, open chemistry that has to be kept safe, and dates that quietly pass while you sleep. So the method changes here. You spray the tub, the shower, and the bowl first and let the cleaner do the standing about while you work the dry surfaces, because chemistry needs time, not scrubbing. You treat drying as the real job, not wiping, because mould is a water problem. And you read the date on everything as you go, because in this room an expired thing is not stock, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf.

### The definition (defbox)
A finished primary bathroom is one you would be happy to hand a guest at an hour's notice: a clear counter holding one tray of daily few, a mirror and glass with no film, a shower that dries out between uses with no dark line where the wall meets the tray, a bowl clean under the rim, drawers and cabinets holding only what is in use and in date, the cleaners and medicines locked or high above a child's climbing reach, the plumbing under the sink in plain sight, and every towel on the shelf clean and dry.

### Spray-first reframe (dwell, do not scrub)
The wet-room surfaces are cleaned with time, not muscle, so the order of work is the opposite of scrubbing. You spray the tub, the shower walls, and the bowl before you touch anything else, and then you leave them: you work the counter, the drawers, and the cabinets while the cleaner does the standing about, breaking down the soap film and the scale and the ring on its own. When you come back, it wipes off in one pass instead of ten. You work top down, you squeegee the glass and the tile last so nothing dries into a film, and you leave the fan running after you walk out.

### Dry-beats-mould reframe
Mould and the soft pink film are not a dirt problem, they are a water problem, so the winning move in this room is not cleaning, it is drying. A surface that dries out between uses does not grow mould, and a surface that stays wet grows it back faster every week however hard you scrub. So you squeegee the glass, you stand the bath mat on edge to drain rather than leaving it flat and wet, you hang the towels where air actually reaches them, you buff the metal dry so it does not spot, and you run the fan and leave the door open until the room has dried out. The cloth cleans what is there; the drying stops it coming back.

### Read-the-date reframe (expired is not stock)
Every product in this room has a date, and in here a date that has passed is not a supply you are saving, it is a failure already sitting on your shelf: the separated sunscreen, the crumbling tablet, the mascara from two summers ago. So you read the date on every Sort pass, on the creams and the medicines and the cosmetics alike, and what has turned goes, because keeping it is paying storage on a decision that already went wrong. Medicines go to a pharmacy take-back, never down the toilet and never into the bin loose, and the leftover half-course of antibiotics is the single most common thing in the cabinet that should not be there.

### Safety reframe (open chemistry, water, electricity)
This is the room where cleaning products, medicines, water, and electricity all share a few square feet, so the safety rules are firm. You keep one product per bowl and you never mix them, because bowl cleaner and bleach together make a gas, and one cleaner does the job alone. You store the bleach, the drain opener, and every medicine locked or high above a child's climbing reach, measured from the closed toilet lid they can stand on rather than from the floor. Nothing stays plugged in while there is water standing in the basin, and no charger runs up to the cistern top. And you keep the plumbing under the sink in plain sight, because a joint that weeps unseen for months turns a cheap washer into a floor that has to come up.

### Before-and-after caption
This is the bathroom the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, the counter clear, the glass and mirror filmless, the shower drying out with no dark seam, the bowl clean under the rim, and the chemistry and medicines locked safely up out of reach. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard, because a bathroom you could hand a guest at an hour's notice is the one that stays good, and the photo is what it has to match after every reset.

### One Idea to Keep
A bathroom is not clean because you scrubbed it hard. It is clean because you sprayed first and let the chemistry work, dried the surfaces so the mould cannot come back, read the date on everything and let the failures go, and locked the chemistry safely away. Do one zone this weekend, the counter or the shower, and give yourself the smallest room you use the most, actually clean and actually safe.

## Four callouts (frozen)
- **Quick Win (win)**: Spray the tub and shower walls and the bowl before you touch anything else, then walk away and clean the counter and the toilet outside while the cleaner sits. Chemistry needs time, not scrubbing, so by the time you come back the soap film and the ring wipe off in one pass. Squeegee the glass last and leave the fan running.
- **6S Tip (tip)**: Treat drying as the real job. Squeegee the glass, stand the bath mat on edge to drain, hang the towels where air reaches them, buff the metal dry, and run the fan until the room has dried out. A surface that dries between uses does not grow mould, however little you scrub it.
- **The Expired-Is-Not-Stock Challenge (family)**: Read the date on every product on every Sort pass, the creams, the medicines, and the cosmetics alike. An expired thing is not a supply you are saving, it is a failure already on your shelf, so let it go, and take medicines to a pharmacy take-back rather than the toilet or the loose bin. The leftover half-course of antibiotics goes first.
- **Common Mistake (mistake)**: Scrubbing a dry surface hard instead of spraying it and giving the cleaner time, wiping the mould off again and again instead of drying the surface so it cannot return, keeping expired creams and medicines as if they were stock, and storing the bleach and the pills at a height a child reaches by standing on the closed toilet lid.

## The calls that matter here
The tool that lives out (the counter holds only what you touch every single day; a three-times-a-week tool is a drawer tool with a heat-safe pouch); the expensive shade you never wear (open and past its window, or unused, goes); the pipe you cannot see (the trap joint and both shut-off valves stay visible); the leftover prescription (a medicine belongs to a condition someone has now, prescribed to that person; the half-course goes to take-back); the bottle you keep out of guilt (in use means you touched it this week; the caddy holds four, not eight); what you keep within splash range (nothing that touches your face, mouth, or food goes within arm's reach of the flush); the good towels you never use (rank every towel, use the top of the ranking daily, let the worn ones go).

## Watch for in the primary bathroom (five safety questions)
- **Poison, choke, or strangle**: bleach, drain opener, and bowl cleaner at a child's eye level behind an unlatched door; the chance of bowl cleaner meeting bleach in one bowl; loose tablets and pushed-through blister packs at a height reached by standing on the closed toilet lid; polish remover and loose vitamins in a low drawer.
- **Water and electricity**: a plugged-in heat tool within cord reach of a full basin; a charger run up to the cistern top; a trap drip creeping toward a socket or a wall nightlight.
- **Fall**: soap film on wet glaze at the moment you step over a high tub wall (grip strips or a mat that truly grips, a grab bar if anyone is unsteady); a step stool on a wet floor to reach a top shelf.
- **Fall, cut, or crush**: a razor on the tub edge at a child's grab height; bare blade refills loose among the brushes; a glass bottle or heavy tin on a top shelf above a hard basin; an overloaded linen shelf on light brackets.
- **Burn or fire**: a hot iron or heat tool set down on a towel or a cosmetic bag to cool (give it a metal or ceramic rest, the same spot every time).

## Kit (product TYPES only)
Key input, new to the wet rooms: a bathroom grime and soap-film cleaner (the soap film, the scale, the ring, the caulk seam). Plus a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner (for stone, since it will not etch the finish the way an acidic spray would), a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner, a floor-material-compatible cleaner for the tub floor and mat, a colour-coded microfiber cloth set (colour-code matters most here, the toilet gets its own colour, this job only), a small detail brush set (scale in crevices, hinge caps, valve threads), a compact vacuum with attachments, and a portable cleaning caddy. Storage and safety: a shower squeegee and a shower caddy, a lockable medicine box and labelled medicine bins, a leak tray and a chemical storage bin for under the sink, two-tier slide-out organizers, a drawer safety latch, a moisture absorber or humidity monitor, water-resistant labels, and a FIFO date marker. No acidic bathroom spray on stone.
