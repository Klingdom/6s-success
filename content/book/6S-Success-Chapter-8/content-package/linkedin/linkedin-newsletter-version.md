# Take Out What Does Not Belong, and Half the Mess Leaves

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

For four chapters we prepared, and not one object moved. We chose a space, scored it, photographed it, and wrote it a job. I asked a lot of your patience. This week it pays off, because this week we finally take something out.

This is Sort, the first of the six S's, and it is the simplest one to describe and the most satisfying one to feel. Its aim is small and blunt: separate the things that belong in a space from the things that do not, and carry the ones that do not somewhere else. You will buy nothing. You will clean nothing. You will just decide, object by object, what actually lives here.

## Clutter is an occupancy problem

Before we start, one reframe, because it changes what the whole job is. The clutter on your console is not a filing problem. It is an occupancy problem. Most of what makes a space feel chaotic is not disorder, it is the sheer presence of things that were never supposed to be there. The charger, the read magazines, the loose homework, the bowl that quietly collects coins. None of that needs a better system. It needs to leave.

This is why buying storage first backfires. Bins bought first just organize the clutter and lock it in place. Remove what does not belong, then buy for what is left, if you even still need to.

## The One Question

To move fast, you need one decision rule you can point at every object. Here it is. Does this help the space do its job?

That is the entire test. You hold up an object, you look at the job you wrote for the space, and you ask whether this thing helps that job happen. Yes means it belongs here, and it stays. No means it belongs somewhere else, and today its job is to go to a better home.

Notice how much lighter this is than the usual method, where you interrogate each object about your whole life. Do I still like this. Will I use it someday. The One Question refuses all of that. It asks one small, answerable thing. As the chapter says, "You are not judging the charger. You are just checking its address." A charger is useful, and for the entryway the answer is still no, because charging phones is not what that space is for.

And then do the second half. Sort is separate, then remove, and people love the separating and skip the removing, which is why rooms end up merely rearranged. Separating is thinking. Removing is the part that changes the room. For most items the carry-out is a ten-second walk, because the thing already has a home and just got lazy. You are not inventing homes. You are returning things to the ones they wandered from. The few honest maybes go in one spot, and you keep moving.

## Removing is not throwing away

I want to slow down on the thing that actually stalls people, because it is not the objects. It is guilt. You pick up a thing that does not belong, and a simple address change starts to feel like a verdict on the object's worth or on yours. The bowl was a gift. The hand hesitates, the thing goes back down, and the pass dies.

So keep this sentence close. You are not deciding what it is worth. You are only deciding it does not live here. Moving a thing out says nothing about whether it is good or whether you were foolish to own it. It is ordinary, kind, and completely reversible. And to be clear about the fence around this week's work: sorting an item out of the space is not the same as getting rid of it. When the charger goes to the desk, it has not been donated, sold, or thrown out. It has moved. Where things go to be let go is a later question with its own chapter.

## The needle moves for the first time

Through all of the preparing, the friction meter for your space held perfectly still, and it was right to, because nothing had physically changed. This week it moves. You carried the magazines and the charger and the bowl out to their real homes, and the space is measurably emptier than it was this morning. That is a real change in the world, the first one, done with your hands in an afternoon, without spending a cent.

Read the gauge honestly, though. It steps toward calm but does not arrive, and it should not. Sort is only the first of six S's. So the needle stops partway, pointed at the goal but short of a finish you have not yet earned.

That is the whole assignment this week. Stand in your space, say its one job out loud, ask the One Question of everything on it, and carry out the noes. Even one object is a legal, complete Sort. You will feel the thing this method keeps promising: most of what overwhelms us was never disorder to be organized, only surplus to be removed.
