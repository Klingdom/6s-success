# Sort First, and Half the Mess Leaves Before You Buy a Single Bin

The usual advice for a messy space is to go buy storage. Bins, baskets, a nice tray, a set of hooks. It feels like progress because you spent money and came home with a plan. But storage bought first just organizes the clutter and locks it in place. You end up with a neatly contained version of a room that was already holding too much.

There is a step before that, and it costs nothing. It is called Sort, and it is the first of the six S's in this method. Sort has exactly one aim: separate the things that belong in a space from the things that do not, and carry the ones that do not somewhere else. That is the whole move. You buy nothing. You clean nothing. You just decide, object by object, what actually lives here.

## Clutter is an occupancy problem

Here is the reframe that changes everything. The clutter on your entryway console is not a filing problem. It is an occupancy problem. Most of what makes a space feel chaotic is not disorder, it is the sheer presence of things that were never supposed to be there in the first place. The charger, the read magazines, the loose homework, the decorative bowl that quietly collects coins. None of that needs a better system. It needs to leave.

So the fix is not to organize the things. It is to notice which things do not belong and show them the way out. Once they go, what remains is not a tidier version of the old space. It is a smaller, truer one, holding only what the space needs to do its job.

## The One Question

To do this fast, you need one decision rule you can point at every object. Here it is. Does this help the space do its job?

That is the entire test. You hold up an object, you look at the job the space is for, and you ask whether this thing helps that job happen. If yes, it belongs here, and it stays. If no, it belongs somewhere else, and today its job is to go to a better home.

Notice how much lighter this is than the usual method, where you pick up each object and interrogate its whole relationship to your life. Do I still like this. Will I use it someday. Who gave it to me. That is an exhausting way to spend a Saturday. The One Question refuses all of it. As the chapter puts it, "You are not judging the charger. You are just checking its address." A charger is genuinely useful, and the answer for the entryway is still no, because charging phones is not what that space is for.

## Do the second half

Sort has two halves, and you do them in order. First you separate: you split everything into what belongs here and what belongs somewhere else. Then you remove: you actually carry the second group out to where it lives. People love the first half and skip the second, which is why so many organizing afternoons end with the room exactly as full as it started, only rearranged. "Separating is thinking. Removing is the part that changes the room."

For most items, the removing is a ten-second walk, because the thing already has a home and just got lazy. The charger goes to the desk. The screwdriver goes to the toolbox. The sweater goes to the closet. You are not inventing homes. You are returning things to the ones they wandered from. A few honest maybes will stump you. Do not solve them on the spot, or the whole pass grinds to a halt. Set those aside in one place and keep moving.

## Removing is not throwing away

One thing stalls people more than any other, and it is not the objects. It is guilt. Somewhere in the pass you pick up a thing that does not belong, and a simple address change suddenly feels like a verdict on the object's worth, or on yours. The bowl was a gift. The magazines cost money. The hand hesitates, the object goes back down, and the pass dies right there.

So keep this sentence close. You are not deciding what it is worth. You are only deciding it does not live here. Moving a thing out of a space says nothing about whether it is good or whether you were foolish to own it. It says one modest thing: this is not where it goes. And it is completely reversible. When removing stops feeling like throwing away, you stop flinching, and when you stop flinching, you finish.

To be clear about the fence around today's work: sorting an item out of this space is not the same as deciding to get rid of it. When the charger goes to the desk, it has not been donated, sold, or thrown out. It has moved. Where things go to be let go is a later question with its own chapter. Right now you are only changing addresses inside your own home.

## The needle moves for the first time

If you have been following this method, you have a friction meter for your chosen space, and through four chapters of preparing it held perfectly still, because nothing had physically moved. Today it moves. You carried the magazines and the charger and the bowl out to their real homes, and the space is measurably emptier than it was this morning. That is a real change in the world, done with your hands, in an afternoon, without spending a cent.

Read the gauge honestly, though. It steps toward calm, but it does not arrive, and it should not. Sort is only the first of six S's. You have removed what does not belong, which is the biggest single step, but the things that stay are not yet arranged or cleaned or set to a standard. So the needle stops partway, pointed at the goal but not yet there.

That is a full and legal Sort. Do it for even one space, one console, one drawer, and you feel the thing this whole method has been promising: most of what overwhelms us was never disorder to be organized, only surplus to be removed.
