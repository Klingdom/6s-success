# Chapter 8 Outline: Sort: Remove What Does Not Belong

A section-by-section map of the chapter as written in the final HTML and manuscript. This chapter physically sorts a space for the first time; it removes what does not belong here, and does not yet decide what you need to own.

## 1. Chapter Opening: Here at Last
You chose the space in Chapter 4, scored it 14 out of 30 in Chapter 5, photographed it in Chapter 6, and wrote it a purpose in Chapter 7. Four chapters of preparing and not one object moved. Today the patience pays off: you take something out. Sort is the first of the six S's, the simplest to describe and the most satisfying to feel, with one aim, to separate what belongs here from what does not and carry the noes somewhere else. Most of the mess was never supposed to be there.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- Say what Sort actually is, and what it is not
- Use one question to test every object in a space
- Run a first Sort pass on your own space, judged by its purpose
- Send the non-belonging things to a better home without agonizing
- See why removing is not the same as throwing away

## 3. What Sort Actually Is
Sort is not tidying (which leaves the pile in place) and not cleaning (which is Shine, later). It is the structural step before both: does this object live here, yes or no? The idea is Seiri, the first of the factory five S's, where clutter was slow and unsafe. The reframe: clutter is an occupancy problem, not a filing problem. Sort has two halves, separate then remove, done in order; people love the first and skip the second, which is why rooms end up merely rearranged.
- Definition box (Sort, the first S): separate what belongs from what does not, remove what does not, judge by the space's job. Sort is not tidying and not cleaning. It is deciding what lives here.

## 4. The One Question
The purpose from Chapter 7 becomes a single gate every item passes through: Does this help the space do its job? Yes means it belongs here and stays (a small green dot). No means it belongs somewhere else and goes to a better home. This is far easier than interrogating each object's whole relationship to your life. A phone charger is useful and still fails, because charging phones is not what the entryway is for. You are not judging the charger, just checking its address.
- Hero infographic: The One Question (objects flow toward the gate and split into "belongs here" green and "a better home" arrow out)

## 5. A Pass Through the Drop Zone
Walk the console with the purpose held up like a ruler. The easy yeses: keys, outgoing mail, sunglasses, the dog's leash, a folded reusable bag, the umbrella, a small tray for keys and wallet, each earning its dot the same way. The noes: already-read magazines, junk mail, a charger (lives better at a desk), finished homework and school papers, a stray screwdriver, a shed sweater (closet), the coin-collecting decorative bowl. Nothing was bought; the console now holds only its door-job things.
- Hero infographic: The First Pass (two-column belongs here / belongs somewhere else, each no with its real home)
- Callout (Quick Win): Stand in your space, say its one job out loud, pick up the first thing your eye lands on, ask the One Question, and if it does not help, carry it where it belongs. One object is a legal, complete Sort.

## 6. Where "Belongs Somewhere Else" Goes
Most items already have an obvious home; the discipline is to make the ten-second walk instead of setting the thing down two feet away. A better home means the place the object actually lives. A few will stump you or you will not be sure; do not solve them now, set them in one spot and keep moving. Those honest maybes get their own system in a couple of chapters. And sorting an item out is not the same as getting rid of it: you are only changing addresses inside your home, not deciding an object's fate.
- Callout (6S Tip): Sort by address, not by fate. Move the clear ones, park the maybes in one spot, and do not let a single hard object stall the pass.

## 7. It Is Not About Throwing Away
The reason most people never finish a Sort is not the objects but the guilt: a simple address change starts to feel like a verdict, the hand hesitates, and the pass dies. The sentence to keep close: you are not deciding what it is worth, you are only deciding it does not live here. The bowl can be lovely and still not belong on the launch pad. Deciding where your belongings live is ordinary, kind, and reversible. When removing stops feeling like throwing away, you stop flinching, and you finish.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Everyone grabs three things that do not belong and walks each to its real home. Nine things gone in five minutes, no arguing about anyone's stuff but your own.

## 8. The Needle Moves
Through all of Part 2 the friction meter held still, because nothing had physically changed and a meter that swings on nothing is flattery. But today objects moved, so the needle steps off the friction side for the first time and travels toward calm, done by hand in an afternoon with no money spent. Read it honestly, though: it stops in the middle, pointed at the goal but not arrived, because Sort is only the first of six S's. Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain are still ahead.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Buying bins and baskets before you Sort just organizes the clutter and locks it in place. Remove first, then buy for what is left, if you even still need to.

## 9. Chapter Close: One Pass Done
You took a space failing because it held too much, asked one question of everything, and carried out every no. What is left is a launch pad that actually launches. That is a full and legal Sort, and it proves that most of what overwhelms us was never disorder to be organized, only surplus to be removed. But look at what remains: everything now belongs here, which does not yet mean you need to own all of it. You sorted by belonging; next you sort by need.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): You do not organize a space by adding storage. You organize it by removing what was never supposed to be there. Sort first, and most of the mess walks out the door before you buy a single bin.
