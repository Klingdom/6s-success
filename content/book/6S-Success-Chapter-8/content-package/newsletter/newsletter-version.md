# Newsletter Edition: Sort: Remove What Does Not Belong

**Subject line (placeholder):** Today you finally pick something up

**Preview text (placeholder):** Four chapters of preparing are done. Now one object leaves.

---

Hi friend,

Here we are at last, standing in front of the drop zone with our sleeves rolled up. For four issues we prepared and did not touch a thing. You chose this space, gave it an honest score, took its picture, and wrote it one plain job. Today, for the first time, you pick something up and it leaves.

This is Sort, the first of the six S's, and it is the simplest one to describe and the most satisfying one to feel. It has one aim: separate the things that belong in a space from the things that do not, then carry the ones that do not somewhere else. You will not buy anything. You will not wipe a single surface. You will just look at each object and decide whether it earns its place, with the purpose you wrote as the judge.

A quick and useful distinction first. Sort is not tidying, because a tidied pile is still a pile, sitting exactly where it was. Sort is not cleaning either, because there is no sense polishing a surface that is about to lose half of what sits on it. Sort is the step before both. It asks one blunt question of every object: does this thing live here, yes or no? The idea is old, borrowed from Toyota's factory floors, where the insight was almost embarrassingly simple. Before you organize anything, remove what does not belong in the area at all. The clutter is not a filing problem. It is an occupancy problem, and step one is simply to notice the squatters and show them the way out.

Here is where the purpose you wrote earns its keep, because it turns into a tool you can point at things. The gate is one question, and it is the same for the keys and the coupons and the stray screwdriver: **Does this help the space do its job?** That is the entire test. If yes, it belongs here, and it stays. If no, it belongs somewhere else, and its job today is to go to a better home. Notice how much lighter this is than the usual method, where you pick up each object and interrogate its whole relationship to your life. A phone charger is genuinely useful, and the answer is still no, because charging phones is not what the entryway is for. You are not judging the charger. You are just checking its address.

So walk the console with the purpose held up like a ruler. This entryway is for getting everyone out the door fast and landing softly on the way back in, so that mornings start calm instead of frantic. Point the question at each thing. The keys stay, obviously, because this is the leaving place. The umbrella stays, for the rainy game day. The dog's leash, the folded reusable bag, the small tray, tomorrow's outgoing mail, all yes, because each one helps you get out the door or land softly coming back. Now the other group. The stack of already-read magazines does nothing for getting anyone out the door, so it goes. The junk mail, same answer. The charger lives a better life at a desk. The finished homework, the stray screwdriver, the shed sweater, the coin-collecting bowl, all belong somewhere else. You carried the noes out to their real addresses, bought no baskets, wiped no wood, and the console that was drowning now holds only the door-job things. Remember, separating is thinking. Removing is the part that changes the room.

One honest note about where the noes go. For most items the answer is easy, because the thing already has a home and just got lazy. The charger goes to the desk, the screwdriver to the toolbox, a ten-second walk each. A few will stump you, and that is fine. Set the honest maybes in one spot and keep moving. Do not let a single hard object stall the whole pass.

And here is the sentence to keep close, because it is the real reason people stall. Somewhere in the pass you will feel a small tug of guilt, and it will try to turn a simple address change into a verdict about your worth or the object's. So remember this. You are not deciding what it is worth. You are only deciding it does not live here. The bowl can be lovely and still not belong on the launch pad. Moving a thing out of a space says nothing about whether the thing is good, only where it goes, and that is ordinary, kind, and completely reversible. When removing stops feeling like throwing away, you stop flinching, and when you stop flinching, you finish.

Now go look at the friction meter, because for the first time in this whole book, it has something to report. Through all the preparing, the needle held perfectly still, and it was right to, because nothing had physically changed. But today objects moved, so the needle finally steps off the friction side and travels toward calm. Read it honestly, though. It stops in the middle, pointed at the goal but not arrived, because Sort is only the first of six S's. Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain are all still ahead. That is exactly where an honest first step should stop.

**To try this week:** Stand in your space, say its one job out loud, then pick up the first thing your eye lands on and ask the One Question. If it does not help the space do its job, carry it to where it actually belongs. One object is a legal, complete Sort. Do more if it feels good, park the maybes in one spot, and stop before it turns into a chore.
