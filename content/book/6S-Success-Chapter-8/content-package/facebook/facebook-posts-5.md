## Post 1 · One question sorts any space

Before you buy a single bin, try this. It costs nothing and takes about ten seconds per object.

Stand in your messiest little space and ask one thing about everything sitting there: Does this help the space do its job?

That is the whole test. Not "do I love it," not "will I use it someday," not "who gave it to me." Just: does this thing help this space do its one job? If yes, it belongs here and it stays. If no, it belongs somewhere else, and today its job is to go to a better home.

Take the entryway table. Its job is getting everyone out the door fast and landing softly on the way back in. So the keys stay, easy. The phone charger? Useful, sure, but charging phones is not what the entryway is for. You are not judging the charger. You are just checking its address.

One question, and half the surface sorts itself.

## Post 2 · A quick walk through the drop zone

Let me pick up each object on the entryway console and point one question at it: does this help the space do its job? Watch the surface change.

The easy yeses first, because it feels good. Keys, yes, you cannot leave without them. Sunglasses, yes. The dog's leash, yes, because half your departures involve the dog. A folded reusable bag by the door, the umbrella, tomorrow's outgoing mail in your hand, a small tray to catch keys and a wallet. All yes. Every one of them helps you get out the door or land softly coming back.

Now the other group. The stack of magazines you already read does nothing for getting anyone out the door, so it belongs somewhere else. The junk mail, same. The phone charger lives a better life at a desk. The finished homework and loose school papers belong wherever paper gets dealt with in your house. The stray screwdriver belongs with the other tools. The shed sweater belongs in the closet. And the decorative bowl that just quietly collects coins and receipts? It belongs somewhere else too.

You bought nothing. You wiped nothing. You just carried the noes back to where they live, and the console that was drowning now holds a handful of things that actually help.

## Post 3 · Removing is not throwing away

Here is the reason most people stall halfway through decluttering, and it has nothing to do with the stuff.

You pick up something that does not belong here, and you feel a little tug of guilt. The bowl was a gift. The magazines cost money. Suddenly a simple move feels like a verdict, and verdicts are heavy, so the hand hesitates and the thing goes back down. The whole afternoon dies right there, on the guilt.

So keep this close: You are not deciding what it is worth. You are only deciding it does not live here.

That is the whole truth of it. The bowl can be lovely and still not belong by the front door. The charger can be valuable and needed and still live better at the desk. Moving a thing out of a space says nothing about whether it is good or whether you were silly to own it. It says one small thing: this is not where it goes.

Notice too that nothing here is permanent. You are only changing an address inside your own home, which is an ordinary, kind, completely reversible thing to do.

Hold that, and the weight lifts off every decision. This belongs, that goes to the desk, this belongs, that goes to the closet.

## Post 4 · Sort by address, not by fate

When something does not belong in a space, the very next worry is usually: okay, but then where does it go?

Good news. For most of what you pull off that surface, the answer is already obvious, because the thing has a real home and just got lazy. The charger goes to the desk. The screwdriver goes to the toolbox. The sweater goes to the closet. A better home just means the place the object actually lives. You are not inventing homes. You are returning things to the ones they wandered from.

The discipline is simply to make the walk, instead of setting the thing down two feet away and calling it progress.

A few things will stump you, and that is fine. Set those honest maybes in one spot, a box or a corner, and keep moving. Do not let one hard object stall the whole pass. (The maybes get a system of their own a couple of chapters later.)

And to be clear, sorting something out of a space is not deciding to get rid of it. Today you are only changing addresses inside your own home. Sort by address, not by fate.

## Post 5 · Put the bins down first

The most common decluttering mistake is buying storage before you sort.

It feels productive. New bins, matching baskets, a label maker. But storage bought first just organizes the clutter and locks it in place. You end up with a tidier version of too much stuff.

Try it the other way. First remove what does not belong in the space at all, then look at what is left, and buy for that, if you even still need to. Most of the time you need far less than you thought, because most of what overwhelms us was never disorder to be organized, only surplus to be removed.

Here is the part I love. You can do this with no money and no cleaning supplies, in an afternoon. And when you finish, something quietly shifts. The space is doing its one job and nothing else, and it looks it. You did that with your hands, not your wallet.

One question, a few short walks to where things actually live, and the surface breathes again.
