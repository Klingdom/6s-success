# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 12, Straighten: A Place for Everything

The console is clear, and yet the keys are on it. So is the leash, and the umbrella, and the bag, and tomorrow's mail. Every one of them earned its place in your life during Sort. Not one of them has a place on this table. That is the odd, in-between state a cleared surface lands in, and it is where the second S begins. Choosing to keep a thing is not the same as deciding where it lives, and so the keepers sit here loose, a small tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday. A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess.

Welcome to Part Four, and to Straighten, the second of the six S's. Sort was about removing; Straighten is about arranging. We are done taking things out of the room. From here on we give the things that remain somewhere to belong, one clear spot each, so that keeping the surface clear stops depending on you and starts depending on the room. That is the turn this part of the book makes, and it is a gentler turn than it sounds. Nothing has to leave today. Today, the keepers get homes.

The principle is small enough to hold in a sentence: a place for everything, so everything can find its way back. The point of a home is the return trip. A thing that has a home has a default, a place it belongs when it is not in your hand, and the moment you are done with it the answer to where it goes is already decided. That is what makes Straighten lighter than Sort. It asks almost nothing of you emotionally. It is closer to carpentry than to soul-searching, because you are not judging your possessions or yourself; you are building a few small landing spots and matching each keeper to one.

Before the homes, one honest question: why do things not get put away in the first place? The usual answer blames your character, and that answer is both unkind and wrong. Think of one thing that always goes back without a thought, the forks, the good coat's hook, the remote's spot on the shelf. It goes back because its home is right there and using it is the path of least resistance. Now think of the thing that lives on the counter no matter how often you move it. The difference is almost never you. It is the home, which is across the room, or behind a door, or under a lid, or simply never decided. You are not failing the room. The room is failing you. The problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design, and a design problem has a design fix.

So everything comes down to knowing a good home from a bad one, and there is one plain test for it. A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you. Hold every home you make up to that standard. The keys drop into the tray as your hand passes over it. The coat lands on the hook in the same swing that takes it off your shoulders. The return is folded into a motion you were already making, which is exactly why it happens without fail. The chapter walks the entryway keepers into homes one at a time, keys to a tray or hook, leash to a hook, umbrella to a stand at the door, bag to a hook, tomorrow's mail to an outgoing slot, each one chosen for a single reason, that the return is one easy motion.

And here is the thing the chapter has been circling, the part that matters more than any tray or hook: you were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second. Change the door and the pile goes away, without a single ounce of extra resolve on your part. That is the quiet promise of the second S, and it is the opposite of most advice, which asks you to become a more disciplined person. This asks you to become a slightly better designer of your own small spaces, which is a far more achievable thing, and unlike sheer effort it does not run out on a hard day.
