## Search intent
Retention, not removal. Readers arriving here have already decluttered, or watched a decluttered space slide back, and want the things they kept to stay put without a nightly tidy. Many blame themselves, reading a messy surface as a character flaw. The chapter answers with a reframe and a tool: the failure to put things away is a design problem, a missing or bad home, not laziness, and the fix is to give every keeper one clear home judged by the Home Test, that putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else, in one easy motion. The worked example walks the entryway keepers into homes (keys, leash, umbrella, bag, mail), and the takeaway is a room that helps keep itself.

## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Reframes the failure to put things away as a design problem (a missing or bad home), not a character flaw, instead of prescribing more discipline
- Gives one plain, testable rule for a good home, the Home Test (one easy motion of return), rather than generic bins-and-baskets advice
- Separates deciding what stays (decluttering, already done) from deciding where it lives (this chapter's only job), which most guides blur
- Walks a concrete entryway example that ties each keeper to one home for one stated reason, an easy return, not a product recommendation
- Delivers the anti-shame message directly (you were never messy, your things simply had nowhere to go) that most organizing content leaves implicit or skips
- Keeps scope tight: this gives each keeper a home; it does not place homes by frequency of use (Chapter 13), add labels or visual cues (Chapter 14), or map the whole room (Chapter 15)
