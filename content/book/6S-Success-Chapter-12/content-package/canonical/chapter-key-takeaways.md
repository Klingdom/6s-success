# Chapter 12 Key Takeaways: Straighten: A Place for Everything

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **This chapter opens Straighten, the second S.** It is the first chapter of Part Four. Sort removed what did not belong and what you did not need; Straighten begins the work of arranging what stayed, and it starts by giving every keeper a home.

2. **A cleared surface is not finished.** Sort did honest work, but choosing to keep a thing is not the same as deciding where it lives. The keepers are sitting loose where they landed, a tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday. A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess.

3. **The work turns from removing to arranging.** Deciding what stays and deciding where it lives are two separate acts, and Sort only did the first. Clearing and keeping-clear are separate jobs, which is why so many clean-outs quietly undo themselves: a keeper with nowhere to go is just tomorrow's clutter arriving early.

4. **The principle fits in a sentence: a place for everything, so everything can find its way back.** The point of a home is the return trip. A thing that has a home has a default, so the moment you are done with it the answer to where it goes is already decided.

5. **Straighten is lighter than Sort.** Sort asked hard questions and sometimes stirred up guilt. Straighten asks almost nothing of you emotionally. It is closer to carpentry than to soul-searching: you are not judging your possessions or yourself, you are building a few small landing spots and matching each keeper to one.

6. **Things do not get put away because of the home, not the person.** The thing that always goes back has a good home, near and open and reachable in a single motion. The thing that lives on the counter has a bad home or none at all. When putting a thing away is more work than setting it down where you stand, it will get set down where you stand, every time.

7. **You are not failing the room; the room is failing you.** The problem was never your willpower, it was the design. And a design problem has a design fix, which is good news, because you can change a home far more easily than you can change your character.

8. **The Home Test is the one tool.** A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you. The test is not whether a thing can be returned, but whether returning it beats dropping it on the nearest surface.

9. **The standard is one easy motion.** The keys drop into the tray as your hand passes over it; the coat lands on the hook in the same swing that takes it off. The return is folded into a motion you were already making, which is exactly why it happens without fail. The best homes are the ones you never notice yourself using.

10. **A home that takes effort is a home that fails.** Giving something a spot that is technically away but hard to reach, behind a door, under a lid, across the room, does not work. If putting it back takes three steps and a decision, it will not go back.

11. **Every keeper gets one home, and each landed within reach of the door.** Keys to a tray or hook, leash to a hook, umbrella to a stand or hook, bag to a hook, tomorrow's mail to an outgoing slot. None of the homes is clever and none needs a special product; each was chosen because returning the thing there is a single easy motion.

12. **The umbrella is the one people get wrong most reliably.** The hall closet feels like the proper, grown-up place, but a home you have to open a door and reach past three coats to use fails the test, so the umbrella ends up leaning in the corner. Put it where you actually pass on your way out.

13. **You were never messy.** Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second. The pile by the door was never evidence about you; it was evidence about the door, which had no tray on it and no hook beside it.

14. **This is a design fix, not a willpower fix.** The second S asks you to become a slightly better designer of your own small spaces, not a more disciplined person. A home does the remembering for you, and it keeps working while you are tired, distracted, or running late, which is most of the time.

15. **The friction meter takes its first Straighten step.** Nothing left the house this time, yet the needle steps again, past the Sort-complete tick toward calm, because friction is about how much thought the room costs you, and giving the keepers homes cuts that cost. Chapter 11's position becomes a faint dashed ghost showing the ground covered.

16. **The step is real, and the goal is still ahead.** The Sort-complete tick sits just behind the live needle, and the goal crosshair on the green calm side is still a fair distance off, because Straighten has only begun. Placing homes by frequency, adding visual cues, and mapping the room are the next three chapters, and four more S's follow.

17. **A room that helps keep itself is the prize.** You did not become tidier today; you built small homes that do the tidying that used to fall to you. The surface stays clear not because you keep clearing it but because clear is where things now naturally settle. That is where Chapter 13 goes next: placing each home by how often you actually reach for it.
