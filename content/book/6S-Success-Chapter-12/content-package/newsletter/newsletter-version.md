# Newsletter Edition: Straighten: A Place for Everything

**Subject line (placeholder):** A place for everything, so everything can find its way back

**Preview text (placeholder):** Nothing leaves today. The things that stayed just get homes.

---

Hi friend,

Come back to the entryway one more time, but this time nothing is leaving. The console you cleared during Sort is holding only its keepers now: the keys, the dog's leash, the umbrella, the reusable bag, and tomorrow's mail. Every one of them earned its place in your life. Not one of them has a place on the table. They are sitting loose where they happened to land, a small tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday.

That is the odd, in-between state a cleared surface lands in, and it is worth naming before we start. Sort did honest work. But choosing to keep a thing is not the same as deciding where it lives, and so the keepers rest wherever the hand set them down. A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess.

Welcome to the second S. Sort was about removing; Straighten is about arranging. We are done taking things out of the room. From here on, every keeper gets one clear home, a single spot where it lives and returns to. That is the turn this part of the book makes, and it is a gentler turn than it sounds. Nothing has to leave today. Today, the keepers get homes.

**A place for everything, so everything can find its way back.**

Notice what that sentence points at: the return trip. A thing that has a home has a default, a place it belongs when it is not in your hand, so the moment you are done with it the answer to where it goes is already decided. Straighten asks almost nothing of you emotionally. It is closer to carpentry than to soul-searching.

Before we hand out homes, one question most tidying advice skips: why do things not get put away in the first place? The usual answer blames you, lazy or disorganized or just messy. That answer is both unkind and wrong. Think of the one thing in your home that always gets put back without a thought, the fork, the hook the good coat hangs on. It goes back every time because its home is right there and using it is the path of least resistance. Now think of the thing that lives on the counter no matter how often you move it. The difference is almost never you. It is the home. When putting a thing away is more work than setting it down where you stand, it will get set down where you stand, every time. You are not failing the room. The room is failing you. The problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design. And a design problem has a design fix.

That gives us the one tool this chapter turns on. Call it the Home Test, and hold every home up to it:

**A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you.**

So we take the five keepers one at a time. The keys want a small tray or a hook right at the door, so they drop or hang in the same motion that closes it. The leash wants a single hook by the door, grabbed on the way out and hung on the way back. The umbrella wants a stand or hook at the door, not the back of a closet, and this is the one people get wrong most reliably, because the closet feels like the proper place while the umbrella ends up leaning in the corner instead. The bag wants a hook, ideally right beside the leash. And tomorrow's mail wants one small outgoing slot by the door, so it rides out in your hand the next time you leave. None of these homes is clever. None needs a special product. Each was chosen for one reason only, that returning the thing there is a single easy motion, and because of that the console will stay clear on its own now, without a nightly tidy.

Here is the thing this whole issue has been circling, and it matters more than any tray or hook:

**You were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second.**

The pile by the door was never evidence about you. It was evidence about the door, which had no tray on it and no hook beside it, and so had nothing to offer the keys but a flat surface to slide across. Change the door and the pile goes away, without a single ounce of extra resolve. This asks you to become a slightly better designer of your own small spaces, not a more disciplined person, and a home does the remembering for you while you are tired, distracted, or running late.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it moves today, and in a new way. Nothing left the house this time. Not one thing was carried to the curb. And still the needle steps again, past the Sort-complete tick and a little further toward the calm side. How, if nothing left? Because friction is not only about how much is in a room; it is about how much thought the room costs you, and giving the keepers homes cuts that cost sharply. This is the first step of the second S. Be just as honest about what the meter still shows: the Sort-complete tick sits just behind the live needle now, and the goal crosshair on the calm side is still a fair distance off, because Straighten has only begun and four more S's follow.

**To try this week:** Pick the one thing you use every day and reach for most, and give it a single home within arm's reach of where you use it. Return it there for three days. Watch how fast putting it back stops being a decision. That one small home is the whole method in miniature.
