# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Straighten: A Place for Everything

*Chapter 12, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter draws a line between two acts: deciding a thing stays, and deciding where it lives. Sort did the first; Straighten does the second. Where in your own home have you finished the deciding but never done the placing, so a cleared surface slid back because the keepers had nowhere to go?

2. "A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess." Think of a room you once cleared that did not stay clear. Looking back, was the problem the effort you put into clearing, or the fact that the things you kept were left loose with no home of their own?

3. The principle fits in a sentence: "A place for everything, so everything can find its way back." The chapter insists the point of a home is the return trip, not how nice the room looks. For one thing you own, what would change if you judged its spot only by how easy it is to put back?

4. The chapter runs a small experiment: name one thing that always gets put away effortlessly, the forks or the remote, and one thing that never does, the thing on the counter. What is the honest difference between those two homes, and how much of it is really about you?

5. "You are not failing the room. The room is failing you." Which thing in your home lives permanently on a flat surface no matter how many times you move it, and what does that tell you about the home you have given it, rather than about your character?

6. The reframe is direct: "The problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design. And a design problem has a design fix." Where have you been trying to solve a design problem with more discipline, and what would the design fix look like instead?

7. The Home Test is the one tool: "A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you." Pick one keeper and run the test out loud. Does its current home pass, and if not, what one easy motion would fix it?

8. "The best homes are the ones you never notice yourself using." Which of your homes already work this way, quietly, without a thought? What do they have in common that the homes you struggle with do not?

9. The umbrella is named the one people get wrong most reliably, because the hall closet feels like the proper, grown-up place for it to go, even though reaching past three coats fails the test. Where have you chosen the respectable-looking home over the easy one, and paid for it with a thing that never goes back?

10. The reframe the whole chapter circles: "You were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second." How does reading a past mess as evidence about the room, rather than a verdict on you, change how you feel about it?

11. "A home does the remembering for you." The chapter says a good home keeps working while you are tired, distracted, or running late, which is most of the time. Which single home, built once, would do the most remembering for you on your hardest, busiest days?

12. Nothing left the house in this chapter, yet the friction meter still stepped toward calm, because giving the keepers homes cut how much thought the room costs you. Why might a room feel lighter even when it holds exactly what it held before, and where in your home could you feel that shift without removing a single thing?
