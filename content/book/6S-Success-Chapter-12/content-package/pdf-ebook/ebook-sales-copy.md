# 6S Success: Home Edition (Sales Page Copy)

## The surface is clear, and the keys are still on it.

You finished a Sort pass and stood back to admire the space. The console by the door is empty of everything that did not belong. And yet the keys are sitting on it. So is the dog's leash, the folding umbrella, the reusable bag, and tomorrow's mail. Every one of them earned its place in your life during Sort. Not one of them has a place on this table.

That is the odd, in-between state a cleared surface lands in. The right things are here, but they are sitting loose where the hand set them down, a tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday. "A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess."

## Welcome to the second S.

Chapter 12 opens Part Four, Straighten, the second of the six S's. Sort was about removing; Straighten is about arranging. We are done taking things out of the room. From here on, the things that stayed get somewhere to belong, one clear spot each, so keeping the surface clear stops depending on you and starts depending on the room. Nothing has to leave today. Today, the keepers get homes.

The principle is small enough to hold in a sentence: "A place for everything, so everything can find its way back." The point of a home is the return trip. A thing that has a home has a default, so the moment you are done with it the answer to where it goes is already decided.

## This is a design fix, not a willpower fix.

Most tidying advice asks you to become a more disciplined person. This chapter asks something easier and kinder. Think of the one thing that always gets put back without a thought, the forks or the remote, and the one thing that never does, the thing that lives on the counter no matter how many times you move it. The difference is almost never you. It is the home. The fork has a good one, near and open and reachable in a single motion. The thing on the counter has a bad home or none at all.

"You are not failing the room. The room is failing you." And that is the good news, because "The problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design. And a design problem has a design fix, which is the good news, because you can change a home far more easily than you can change your character."

## What you get in this chapter

- The Home Test, the one tool the chapter turns on: "A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you."
- The reason things do not get put away, reframed from a character flaw to a design flaw, so the pressure comes off in a way that actually helps
- A worked pass through the entryway that gives each keeper one home, keys to a tray or hook, leash to a hook, umbrella to a stand or hook, bag to a hook, tomorrow's mail to an outgoing slot, every home within reach of the door
- The one people get wrong most reliably, the umbrella, and why the hall closet quietly fails the test
- The friction meter's first Straighten step, marked honestly and never oversold

## You were never messy.

The chapter says the thing plainly, and it is worth believing: "You were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second." The pile by the door was never evidence about you. It was evidence about the door, which had no tray on it and no hook beside it. Change the door and the pile goes away, without a single ounce of extra resolve. "A home does the remembering for you."

## The needle steps again.

Nothing leaves the house in this chapter. Not one thing is carried to the curb. And still the friction meter steps again, past the Sort-complete tick toward the calm side, because friction is not only about how much is in a room. It is about how much thought the room costs you, and giving the keepers homes cuts that cost sharply. The step is real, and the goal is still ahead, because Straighten has only begun and four more S's follow.

Read the chapter, pick the one thing you reach for most, and give it a single home within arm's reach of where you use it. Return it there for three days, and watch how fast putting it back stops being a decision.
