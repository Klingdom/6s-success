### Slide 2 · Clear, and still one morning from a mess

- The console is clear, and yet the keys are on it, along with the leash, the umbrella, the bag, and tomorrow's mail. Every one of them earned its place during Sort. Not one of them has a place on this table.
- That is the odd, in-between state a cleared surface lands in. Choosing to keep a thing is not the same as deciding where it lives, so the keepers sit loose, a tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday.
- Welcome to the second S. Sort was about removing; Straighten is about arranging. Nothing has to leave today. Today, the keepers get homes.

### Slide 3 · From removing to arranging

- Chapter 11 carried the out box out the door and left the corner empty, but the keepers were still exactly where the pass had left them, with no proper places of their own. Deciding what stays and deciding where it lives are two different acts, and we had only done the first.
- This is the point people usually miss, and why so many clean-outs quietly undo themselves. Clearing and keeping-clear are separate jobs, and a room emptied of the wrong things will refill with the right things left loose.
- Keep the fences honest. We are not reopening Sort, and we are not yet asking which spot is best, or adding a label, or planning the whole room. Those are the next three chapters. Today has one narrow task: give each keeper a home.

### Slide 4 · What Straighten is

- Straighten is the second of the six S's, and its old name carries its whole meaning. In the original method it is called seiton, usually translated as a place for everything, and everything in its place. That is not a slogan about neatness; it is a claim about where order comes from.
- A room does not stay orderly because someone keeps tidying it. A room stays orderly when every thing in it has one known spot, so that being in order is simply the resting state.
- Definition, verbatim: "Straighten, the second S, gives every keeper one clear home, a single spot where it lives and returns to. A place for everything, and everything in its place, so the space stays easy without effort."

### Slide 5 · The principle

- Here is the principle this chapter turns on, small enough to hold in a sentence.
- The point of a home is the return trip. It does not say a place for everything so the room looks nice, though it will. It says so everything can find its way back. A thing that has a home has a default, so the moment you are done with it the answer to where it goes is already decided.
- You are not choosing in that moment; you are just completing a motion you have made a hundred times before.

### Slide 6 · Why things do not get put away

- Sit with a question most tidying advice skips: why do things not get put away in the first place? The usual answer is a character one, that you are lazy or disorganized or just messy. It is both unkind and wrong.
- Think of one thing that always gets put back effortlessly, the forks or the remote, and one thing that never does, the thing that lives on the counter. The difference is almost never you. It is the home. The fork has a good one, near and open and reachable in a single motion; the thing on the counter has a bad home or none at all.
- When putting a thing away is more work than setting it down where you stand, it will get set down where you stand, every time.

### Slide 7 · You are not failing the room

- Read the difference plainly. You are not failing the room. The room is failing you.
- This is the quiet reframe underneath the whole second S, and it takes the pressure off in a way that actually helps. The problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design. And a design problem has a design fix, which is the good news, because you can change a home far more easily than you can change your character.
- 6S Tip, verbatim: "A good home is closer than the floor. If a thing keeps landing on the counter, its home is too far away or too much work. Move the home, not your willpower."

### Slide 8 · The Home Test

- If the fix is a better home, everything depends on telling a good home from a bad one, and the difference comes down to one measurable thing: the effort of the return. Not how neat the spot looks or how clever the container is. Only this: how much work is it to put the thing back?
- That gives the one tool this chapter turns on. Apply it to every home you make.
- The Home Test, verbatim: "A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you."

### Slide 9 · One easy motion

- So the standard is one easy motion. The keys drop into the tray as your hand passes over it. The coat lands on the hook in the same swing that takes it off your shoulders. The return is folded into a motion you were already making, which is exactly why it happens without fail.
- The best homes are the ones you never notice yourself using.
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Giving something a home that is technically "away" but hard to reach, behind a door, under a lid, across the room. If putting it back takes three steps and a decision, it will not go back. A home that takes effort is a home that fails."

### Slide 10 · Giving the keepers homes

- Back to the entryway, where the console holds its five keepers, each held up to the Home Test one at a time. Keys: a small tray or a hook at the door, dropped or hung in the same motion that closes the door. Leash: one hook by the door, grabbed on the way out and hung on the way back. Umbrella: a stand or hook right at the door, not the back of a hall closet, the mistake people make most reliably. Reusable bag: a hook, ideally beside the leash. Tomorrow's mail: one small outgoing slot by the door, a launching place rather than a resting one.
- Every home landed within reach of the door. None of them is clever. None needs a special product. Each was chosen for one reason only, that returning the thing there is a single easy motion.
- And because of that, the console will stay clear on its own now, without a nightly tidy.

### Slide 11 · You were never messy

- Take a step back from the entryway and say the thing the chapter has been circling, because it matters more than any tray or hook.
- For years the mess in a home gets read as a verdict on the person living in it. That reading is cruel and useless, because it points you at the one thing you cannot easily change, your character, and away from the one thing you can, the arrangement of your stuff. The pile by the door was never evidence about you. It was evidence about the door.
- This asks you to become a slightly better designer of your own small spaces, not a more disciplined person. A home does the remembering for you.

- Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves today, and it is a different kind of move than last time. Not one thing was carried to the curb. And still the needle steps again, past the Sort-complete tick and a little further toward calm.
- How, if nothing left? Because friction is not only about how much is in a room. It is about how much thought the room costs you, and giving the keepers homes cuts that cost sharply. Each thing now has an answer built into the room, so the finding and the putting-back happen without a decision. This is the first step of the second S.

### Slide 13 · A room that helps keep itself (close + soft CTA)

- Look at the console one last time. The keys are in their tray, the leash and bag hang on their hooks, the umbrella stands by the door, and tomorrow's mail sits in its outgoing slot. Nothing left the house, yet the surface is clear in a way that will hold, because every thing on it now has somewhere to be that is easier to use than not.
- That is the real prize of the second S. You did not become tidier today; you built a handful of small homes, and from now on those homes do the tidying that used to fall to you. But the next question is already waiting: each keeper has a home, yes, but not every home is in the best spot it could be.
- One Idea to Keep, verbatim: "Tidy is not a chore you repeat forever. It is a set of homes good enough that things find their own way back. Build the homes once, and the room helps keep itself."
