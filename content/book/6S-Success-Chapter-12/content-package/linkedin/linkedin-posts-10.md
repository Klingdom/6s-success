## 1. A cleared surface is not finished

Sort did honest work. It carried out what did not belong and left you the short list of things you chose to keep. But choosing to keep a thing is not the same as deciding where it lives.

So the keepers sit loose, resting wherever your hand set them down, a tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday.

A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess.

That gap, between a thing being wanted and a thing being placed, is the whole job of the second S. Nothing has to leave today. Today, the keepers get homes.

## 2. Clearing and keeping-clear are two jobs

Here is why so many clean-outs quietly undo themselves.

Deciding what stays and deciding where it lives are two separate acts, and a hard weekend only does the first. You clear a room, admire the space, and three weeks later it looks the way it did before you started.

Nothing was wrong with your effort. A room emptied of the wrong things will refill with the right things left loose, because a keeper with nowhere to go is just tomorrow's clutter arriving early.

The quiet second half, the half nobody puts on a to-do list, is giving each thing that stayed a home. That is what makes the first half finally hold.

## 3. A place for everything

The whole of the second S fits in one sentence.

A place for everything, so everything can find its way back.

Notice it does not say so the room looks nice, though it will. It says so everything can find its way back, because the point of a home is the return trip.

A thing that has a home has a default. The moment you are done with it, the answer to where it goes is already decided. You are not choosing; you are completing a motion you have made a hundred times before.

## 4. You are not failing the room

Why do things not get put away?

The usual answer is a character one: you are lazy, or disorganized, or just messy. That answer is both unkind and wrong.

Think of one thing that always gets put back without a thought, the forks, the remote. Now think of the thing that lives on the counter no matter how many times you move it. The difference is almost never you. It is the home.

When putting a thing away is more work than setting it down where you stand, it will get set down where you stand, every time. You are not failing the room. The room is failing you.

Move the home, not your willpower.

## 5. The Home Test

There is one tool for the whole of Straighten, and it measures just one thing: the effort of the return.

The Home Test: a home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you.

The test is not whether a thing can be returned. Almost anything can, if you will walk across the room and open a cabinet. The test is whether going to the home beats dropping the thing on the nearest flat surface.

If it does not win that small contest, the thing will not go back, and the fault is in the home.

## 6. One easy motion

The standard for a good home is one easy motion.

The keys drop into the tray as your hand passes over it. The coat lands on the hook in the same swing that takes it off your shoulders. The return is not a separate chore you have to remember and summon energy for. It is folded into the motion you were already making.

When a home is that close and that open, putting the thing away costs a fraction of a second and no decision at all, which is exactly why it happens without fail.

The best homes are the ones you never notice yourself using.

## 7. Watch five keepers get homes

Back to the entryway console after Sort. Five things sit loose, each about to get a place of its own, each held up to one test: is returning it a single easy motion?

Keys: a small tray or a hook at the door, dropped in the same motion that closes it. Leash: one hook by the door, grabbed on the way out, hung on the way back. Umbrella: a stand or hook at the door, not the back of a closet. Bag: a hook beside the leash. Tomorrow's mail: one small outgoing slot, so it rides out in your hand.

Every home landed within reach of the door. None is clever. None needs a special product.

## 8. The umbrella people get wrong

The umbrella is the one people get wrong most reliably.

The hall closet feels like the proper, grown-up place for it to go. But a home you have to open a door and reach past three coats to use is a home that fails the test, so the umbrella ends up leaning in the corner instead.

Put it where you actually pass on your way out, and it will go back where it goes.

A home that takes effort is a home that fails. If putting a thing back takes three steps and a decision, it will not go back, and that is the home's fault, not yours.

## 9. You were never messy

You were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second.

For years the mess in a home gets read as a verdict on the person living in it. That reading is not only cruel, it is useless, because it points you at the one thing you cannot easily change, your character, and away from the one thing you can, the arrangement of your stuff.

The pile by the door was never evidence about you. It was evidence about the door, which had no tray on it and no hook beside it.

Change the door and the pile goes away, without a single ounce of extra resolve.

## 10. Nothing left, and the room got easier

Nothing left the house today, and the room still got easier to keep.

Friction is not only about how much is in a room. It is about how much thought the room costs you, and giving the keepers homes cuts that cost. Each thing now has an answer built into the room, so finding and putting-back happen without a decision.

You did not become tidier. You built small homes that do the tidying that used to fall to you. The surface stays clear because clear is now where things naturally settle.
