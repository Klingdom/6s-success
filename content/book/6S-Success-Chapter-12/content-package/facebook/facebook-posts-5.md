## Post 1 · The clear surface that will not stay clear

The console by your door is clear, and yet the keys are on it. So is the leash, and the umbrella, and the bag, and tomorrow's mail. Every one of them earned its place during Sort. Not one of them has a place on this table.

That is the odd, in-between state a cleared surface lands in. The right things are here, but each is just resting wherever the hand set it down, a tidy-looking pile that will scatter again by Thursday. A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess.

Welcome to the second S. Sort was about removing. Straighten is about arranging. Nothing has to leave today. Today, the keepers get homes.

## Post 2 · The one test that tells a good home from a bad one

Everything in Straighten comes down to a single measurable thing: the effort of the return. Not how neat the spot looks, not how clever the container is. Only this. How much work is it to put the thing back?

That gives us the one tool this chapter turns on.

"A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you."

Read that second sentence twice. The test is not whether a thing can be returned. Almost anything can, if you are willing to walk across the room and open a cabinet. The test is whether returning it beats dropping it on the nearest flat surface. If it does not win that small contest, the thing will not go back, and the fault is in the home, not in you.

So the standard is one easy motion. The keys drop into the tray as your hand passes over it. The best homes are the ones you never notice yourself using.

## Post 3 · Why things do not get put away

Think of one thing in your home that always gets put back, effortlessly, without a thought. Maybe it is where the forks go, or the hook the good coat hangs on. You never struggle with those. They simply go back, every time, because their home is right there and using it is the path of least resistance.

Now think of the thing that never gets put away, the one that lives on the counter no matter how many times you move it. The difference between that thing and the fork is almost never you. It is the home. The fork's is near, open, and reachable in a single motion. The other has a bad home or none at all.

The problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design. And a design problem has a design fix, which is the good news, because you can change a home far more easily than you can change your character.

## Post 4 · Five keepers, five homes

Let me make some homes, because the test is easier to trust once you have watched it work. Back to the entryway console and its five keepers, one at a time.

Keys go to a small tray or a hook right at the door, so the hand coming in drops or hangs them in the same motion that closes the door. The leash goes to a single hook, grabbed on the way out and hung on the way back. The umbrella wants a stand or hook near the door, not the back of a hall closet, because a home you have to open a door and reach past three coats to use is a home that fails. The reusable bag gets a hook beside the leash, so it is there when you leave and back in one motion when you return. Tomorrow's mail gets one small outgoing tray by the door, so it rides out in your hand next time you leave.

None of these is clever. None needs a special product. Each was chosen for one reason only, that returning the thing there is a single easy motion. And because of that, the console will stay clear on its own now, without a nightly tidy.

## Post 5 · You were never messy

Here is the thing this chapter has been circling, and it matters more than any tray or hook.

"You were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second."

For years the mess in a home gets read as a verdict on the person living in it. That reading is not only cruel, it is useless, because it points you at the one thing you cannot easily change, your character, and away from the one thing you can, the arrangement of your stuff. The pile by the door was never evidence about you. It was evidence about the door, which had no tray on it and no hook beside it.

Change the door and the pile goes away, without a single ounce of extra resolve on your part. A home does the remembering for you. Build it once, and it keeps working while you are tired, distracted, running late, or simply not thinking about tidiness at all.

Nothing left the house today, and yet the friction meter takes its first Straighten step, because giving the keepers homes cuts how much thought the room costs you. The needle stepped, and the goal is still ahead, because Straighten has only begun.
