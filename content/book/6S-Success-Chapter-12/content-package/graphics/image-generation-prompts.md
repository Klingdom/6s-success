# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 12

**"Straighten: A Place for Everything"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Four · Straighten*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel. Palette to hold across all
five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`,
terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B`, soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single
warm red `#CB4B36` reserved for the one bad-home snag. Natural soft daylight, shallow but not
showy depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the entryway console, the drop zone. Across Part Three, Sort
cleared it and routed the out box out of the house. What is left on the surface now is the short
list of keepers, the things chosen to stay: a set of keys, the dog's leash, a folding umbrella,
a reusable shopping bag, and a small stack of tomorrow's mail. But they are sitting loose where
they landed, none with a place of its own. This chapter opens Part Four, the second S, and gives
each keeper one clear home, a single spot where it lives and returns to, judged only by whether
the return is one easy motion. Nothing is re-decided or removed here; the keepers are settled.
No home is placed by how often a thing is used (that is Chapter 13), no labels or visual cues
are added (Chapter 14), and no whole room is mapped (Chapter 15). Keep the tone calm and kind:
the reader was never messy, the things simply had nowhere to go, and the fix is design, not
willpower. Keep it calm and non-coercive, no shame cues, no force or pressure framing.

Separator discipline: any incidental label uses the middot, for example
`CHAPTER 12 · STRAIGHTEN`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot between words. Compound
terms keep their internal hyphen: one-motion, Sort-complete, anti-shame.

---

## 1. The homeless keepers (the opener)

A warm, low-angle photograph of a cleared entryway console in soft morning daylight, holding
only its keepers sitting loose where they happened to land: a set of keys, a coiled dog's leash,
a folding umbrella, a reusable shopping bag, and a small stack of tomorrow's mail. The surface is
clean and uncluttered, yet nothing is arranged; each thing rests wherever a hand set it down. On
the wall above the console an empty hook waits, and on the surface an empty tray sits ready, both
plainly unused. Warm paper cream and honey wood tones, ink-brown shadows, a gentle slate blue
wall. Editorial cookbook-style photography, shallow focus on the loose keepers and the waiting
empty hook and tray. The room looks calm and clear but not yet settled.

- Mood: the right things are here, none has a place of its own yet, and an empty hook and empty
  tray quietly wait for an answer.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, floating text overlays, an overflowing
  mess or clutter drama, any sense of re-deciding or removing what is on the console, price tags,
  hurry or stress.

## 2. The Home Test (the hero device)

A warm editorial still-life built as a side-by-side comparison of the same set of keys facing
two homes. On the left, a good home: a small open tray on the console right where a hand comes
in, the keys resting in it, an easy single-motion return, calm and effortless, a soft-green
note. On the right, a bad home: the same keys sitting on a counter because their assigned spot
is far across the room, behind a cabinet door and under a lid, clearly too much work to reach; a
single small warm-red accent marks the snag. Warm paper cream background, honey light, ink-brown
shadows, soft-green calm on the good home, calm slate framing, one restrained warm-red note on
the bad home only. Shallow focus shared between the open tray and the out-of-reach lidded spot.

- Mood: a home is only as good as the return trip; the near open tray wins, the far lidded spot
  loses, and the difference is the design of the spot.
- Avoid: harsh warning colors beyond the single small red accent, a scene of failure or blame,
  clutter drama, a sterile showroom look, floating typeset text, any suggestion the person is at
  fault rather than the placement.

## 3. Giving the keepers homes (the worked pass)

A warm editorial still-life of the entryway keepers, each now resting in one assigned home right
by the door: the keys dropped in a small tray or hung on a hook at the door, the dog's leash on a
single hook, the folding umbrella standing in a stand or hung near the door, the reusable bag on
a hook beside the leash, and tomorrow's mail set in one small outgoing slot ready to ride out the
door. Every home is within easy reach of the entry, each thing a single easy motion from where it
is used. Soft daylight, honey and paper tones, ink-brown shadows, soft-green calm notes at the
homes, shallow depth on the tray and hooks. Documentary and unstaged, as if each keeper had just
been placed in its spot for the first time. The surface reads clear and settled, calm to keep.

- Mood: each keeper matched to one clear home by the door, an easy return, so the console stays
  clear on its own without a nightly tidy.
- Avoid: rows of labels or color-coded tags on the homes, any ranking of prime versus far spots,
  a whole-room plan, clutter or urgency, cold clinical light, shame cues, floating typeset text.

## 4. Why things do not get put away (the reframe)

A calm, warm photograph of two small vignettes side by side. On the left, a kitchen drawer or
hook where a fork or a coat has an easy, obvious home, the kind of thing that always goes back
without a thought, near and open and reachable in one motion. On the right, an ordinary thing
resting on a counter because its home is across the room, behind a door, under a lid, or was
never decided at all, so it lands wherever the hand lets go. Both scenes are ordinary and
un-staged. Warm paper cream background, honey light, ink-brown shadows, a soft-green calm note on
the easy home, calm slate on the hard one, one small restrained warm-red accent at the single
snag on the right. The image should read as two designs, not two kinds of person.

- Mood: quiet reframe, the difference between the thing that always goes back and the thing that
  never does is the home, not the owner, and a home placed wrong is a design to fix, not a flaw.
- Avoid: a shaming before-and-after, a chaotic counter, guilt cues, harsh contrast, any framing
  of the person as lazy or messy, glossy retouching, floating typeset text.

## 5. The needle steps into Straighten (the friction meter, the first step)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge
dialing from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right.
The dark live needle has stepped one clear notch further toward the calm side than last chapter
and now rests just past a plainly marked tick labeled the Sort-complete milestone, which now sits
just behind the needle as ground already covered. Just behind the live needle, a faint dashed
ghost needle marks the exact spot the meter held at the end of Chapter 11, showing the step just
taken. A small green marker labels the new live position as where Straighten begins, the first
step of the second S. A slate crosshair labeled the goal, carrying its purpose flag, still sits
further out on the green calm side, clearly ahead of the live needle, because Straighten has only
begun and four more disciplines remain. Warm paper cream background, ink line work, consistent
stroke weight, friendly but not childish.

- Mood: an honest first step into the second S, nothing left the house yet the room grew easier
  to keep, so the needle stepped once, plainly, with plenty of road still ahead.
- Avoid: the live needle held in place with no move, the needle swung all the way to calm, the
  needle reaching or passing the goal crosshair, the dashed ghost drawn solid or bright,
  celebratory confetti, hype, neon color, any sense of Straighten being finished or the journey
  being done. This is only the first step; the rest of Straighten and four more S's still remain.
