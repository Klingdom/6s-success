The console is clear, and yet the keys are still on it. So is the leash, the umbrella, the bag, tomorrow's mail. Sort chose them all. None of them has a place on this table yet. Chapter 12 of 6S Success: Home Edition opens Part Four, the second S: Straighten.

2/
Keeping a thing and deciding where it lives are two different acts, and Sort only did the first. So the keepers sit loose, wherever the hand set them down.

"A surface can be clear and still be one busy morning away from a mess."

(approx. 224 chars)

3/
Straighten is the second S. Its old name, seiton, means a place for everything, and everything in its place. That is not about neatness. A room stays orderly when every thing has one known spot, so order is the resting state and disorder takes an accident.

(approx. 252 chars)

---

4/
The principle, small enough to hold in a sentence:

"A place for everything, so everything can find its way back."

The point of a home is the return trip. When you are done with a thing, the answer to where it goes is already decided.

(approx. 233 chars)

---

5/
Why do things not get put away? The usual answer blames your character: lazy, disorganized, just messy. That answer is unkind and wrong. The thing on the counter is not a verdict on you. It is information about the counter, which offered no home.

(approx. 242 chars)

---

6/
Run the proof yourself. Think of one thing that always gets put back without a thought: the forks, the coat hook, the spot the remote returns to. You do not summon willpower for those. They go back because their home is right there and using it is the easiest path.

(approx. 264 chars)

---

7/
Now the thing that never gets put away, the one that lives on the counter no matter what. The difference from the fork is almost never you. It is the home. When putting a thing away is more work than setting it down where you stand, it will get set down where you stand.

(approx. 267 chars)

---

8/
So the problem was never your willpower. The problem was the design. And a design problem has a design fix, which is good news, because a home is far easier to change than a character.

"Move the home, not your willpower."

(approx. 219 chars)

---

9/
Everything now depends on telling a good home from a bad one, and it comes down to one thing: the effort of the return. Not how neat it looks, not how clever the container is. Only this: how much work is it to put the thing back?

(approx. 226 chars)

---

10/
The one tool this chapter turns on:

"A home works when putting the thing back is easier than putting it down anywhere else. If returning it takes more than one easy motion, the home is wrong, not you."

Call it the Home Test.

(approx. 224 chars)

---

11/
So the standard is one easy motion. The keys drop into the tray as your hand passes over it. The coat lands on the hook in the same swing that takes it off. The return is folded into a motion you were already making. The best homes are the ones you never notice using.

(approx. 264 chars)

---

12/
Give the five entryway keepers homes, each judged by the test. Keys · a tray or hook right at the door. Leash · a single hook, grabbed going out, hung coming back. Both drop into place in one motion, no drawer to open, no tangle to fish through.

(approx. 240 chars)

13/
The umbrella wants a stand at the door, not the back of a closet. That is the one people get wrong: a home you open a door and reach past three coats to use will fail, so the umbrella leans in the corner instead. Put it where you actually pass on the way out.

(approx. 254 chars)

14/
The bag hangs on a hook beside the leash. Tomorrow's mail gets one small outgoing slot at the door, so it rides out in your hand instead of sinking into a pile. Every home landed within reach of the door, because that is where these things are used and let go.

(approx. 256 chars)

15/
The line this chapter has been circling:

"You were never messy. Your things simply had nowhere to go. Give them a home, and putting them away stops taking willpower and starts taking a second."

Change the door and the pile goes.
