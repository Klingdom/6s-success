# An Audit Is a Flashlight, Not a Grade

Nobody has ever been excited to hear the word audit.

It is a cold word, a clipboard word, a word that arrives with bad news and reading glasses. So let me take it away from you for a second and hand it back as something friendlier, because the thing this chapter asks you to do is genuinely small, genuinely kind, and genuinely the difference between effort that works and effort that just makes you tired.

You picked a target space already. One small, high-friction spot, written down and waiting. Maybe it is the entryway drop zone, that little table by the door where the keys and the mail and the sunglasses and the one stray glove all go to argue. I will use that drop zone as the example here, but everything works on whatever space you actually chose.

You are motivated and ready, and the natural urge is to skip straight to fixing it. Resist that for ten more minutes. Before you change a single thing, you are going to look at your target on purpose and put a few honest numbers on it.

That is the whole audit. Not a clipboard, not a grade. Just a calm, structured look at a space you have been walking past on autopilot for months, so you finally see what is working, what is not, and which of the six S's is actually dragging it down. Because a messy space is never equally broken in all six ways. One or two of the S's are usually fine. One or two are the real problem. If you cannot tell which, you pour your energy into the parts that were never the issue and wonder why the space still feels off.

## Measure before you move

Let me make the case for the number, because skipping it feels efficient and is not.

Without a baseline, "better" is just a feeling, and feelings fade. You spend a satisfying hour on your drop zone, step back, feel good, and three weeks later have no honest way to tell whether it actually improved or whether you just remember it fondly. Worse, you have no idea whether the thing you fixed was the thing that was broken.

A number fixes all of that. It turns a vague, guilty "this space is a mess" into a specific, almost cheerful "this space is a fourteen, and the low scores are Standardize and Sustain." One of those sentences makes you feel bad. The other tells you exactly what to do.

A baseline also protects you from fixing the wrong thing. The drop zone that feels chaotic might actually have a perfectly fine amount of stuff. Its real problem might be that nothing has a home. If you spend your hour ruthlessly throwing things away, when the actual issue was the lack of a key dish and a mail tray, you end up with less stuff and the same chaos, and you conclude that 6S does not work. It works. You just aimed at the wrong S because you never measured.

You are not stalling. You are aiming. A carpenter measures twice and cuts once, and nobody accuses the carpenter of procrastinating.

## The 6S Snapshot

Here is the tool. One question per S, a score from zero to five, and a shape. You already know the six S's, so you are not learning anything new. You are just pointing each one at your space like a flashlight and asking, honestly, how is this space doing on this one thing?

- **Sort.** Of everything here, how much do you actually use?
- **Straighten.** Does everything have an obvious home you can find fast?
- **Shine.** Is it clean, with no hidden dust, leaks, or wear?
- **Safety.** Could anything here hurt someone, especially a child?
- **Standardize.** Is the right way obvious to anyone, or only in your head?
- **Sustain.** Is any habit keeping it up, or does it just drift?

Score each one, do not agonize, then connect the six scores into a shape. The shape tells you more in one glance than an hour of staring ever could.

A space that caves in toward the center on Sort is buried. Too much in it, and nothing else can be fixed until the volume comes down. Start by removing, not arranging. A space that scores fine on Sort but collapses on Straighten is a different animal. It does not have too much. It has no homes. That space does not need a purge. It needs a key dish, a mail tray, a hook. And a space that scores well early and falls off a cliff on Standardize and Sustain has been organized before, probably more than once, and keeps sliding back because nothing was ever built to hold the fix in place.

## The worked drop zone

That last shape is the drop zone in our example. Sort is a four. Straighten and Shine are a three each. Safety is a two. Then the floor drops out: Standardize is a one, Sustain is a one. Add it up and you get a baseline of fourteen out of thirty, with the two weakest scores sitting at the very end.

See what just happened. You have not touched the space, and you already know that throwing things away would waste your afternoon, and that the real work is a labeled system and a ten-second habit. That is the snapshot earning its keep. The lowest score is not an insult. It is a map, pointing directly at the cheapest relief in the room.

## A low score is good news

Three small rules keep this honest. Be honest, and score the space as it is on an ordinary Tuesday, not the rare day company is coming. Be fast, about ten seconds per question, because your first instinct is almost always right. And be kind, which is the one people forget.

A low baseline is good news. Read that again, because it runs against every instinct. A space that scores a fourteen has sixteen points of relief sitting in it, available, mostly cheap. A space that scores a twenty-eight is already pretty good, and there is not much joy left to mine from it. So when your target comes back low, do not wince. The worst spaces give the best wins.

One more thing. Audit per area, not the whole house. Score the one space you chose. The house gets audited one target at a time, the same way it gets fixed.

You came in braced for a clipboard and a grade. You leave with a flashlight and a number, which are much friendlier things to carry. Nothing moved, but you can no longer look at your target as a vague, guilty mess. You see a specific shape now, with specific dents, and you know exactly where an hour of work will land hardest.
