# Podcast Script: The 6S Home Audit

**Chapter 5, 6S Success: Home Edition (Part Two, Prepare)**
**Slug:** the-6s-home-audit
**Estimated runtime:** 8 to 12 minutes (roughly 1,550 spoken words)
**Format:** Audio-first, conversational, single host. No visuals required.

---

## INTRO (about 1 minute)

Hey, welcome back. If you have been following along with 6S Success at home, you did something last time that matters more than it sounds. You picked a target. One small, high-friction, low-effort space, written down, waiting for you. Maybe it is a closet. Maybe it is the kitchen counter that collects everything. For a lot of people it is the entryway drop zone, you know the one, that little table or shelf by the door where the keys and the mail and the sunglasses and the one stray glove all go to argue. I am going to use that drop zone as my running example today, but everything I say works on whatever space you actually chose.

And here is where most people go wrong. They are motivated, they are ready, and the very natural urge is to skip straight to fixing it. Today I want to talk you out of that for about ten minutes. Because there is one quiet little step in between that makes every minute of fixing actually land where it helps. That step has a word attached to it that everybody hates. The word is audit. Stick with me, because I think by the end of this you are going to like that word a lot more than you do right now.

## THE REFRAME (about 1.5 minutes)

So let me say it plainly. Nobody has ever been excited to hear the word audit. It is a cold word. A clipboard word. It shows up with bad news and reading glasses. So let me take it away from you for a second and hand it back as something friendlier.

An audit at home is not the IRS. It is not a test you can fail. It is a flashlight. That is the whole reframe. Before you change a single thing about your space, you are going to look at it on purpose and put a few honest numbers on it. That is it. Not a clipboard. Not a grade. Just a calm, structured look at a space you have probably been walking past on autopilot for months, so that for the first time you can see exactly what is working, what is not, and which of the six S's is the one actually dragging the space down.

And here is the part nobody tells you about a messy space. It is never equally broken in all six ways. One or two of the S's are usually fine. One or two are usually the real problem. And if you cannot tell which is which, you are going to pour all your energy into the parts that were never the issue, and then stand there wondering why the space still feels off.

## PERSONAL ASIDE (about 1.5 minutes)

Let me tell you the mistake this whole idea is built to prevent, because I have made it, and I bet you have too.

Picture a space that feels chaotic. Your gut says, too much stuff, get rid of things. So you spend a really satisfying hour purging. Donation bag, trash bag, the whole ritual. You step back, you feel great. And then a week later it is right back to being a mess. And you cannot figure out why, because you worked hard, you got rid of so much.

Here is why. The problem was never too much stuff. The problem was that nothing had a home. The right amount of stuff was always there, it was just floating, landing wherever there was a free inch, because nothing had a designated place to live. So you threw a bunch of things away, and now you have less stuff and the exact same chaos. And the really sad part is what you conclude from that. You decide that this whole organizing thing does not work for you. It works. You just aimed at the wrong S, because you never measured. That is the trap. And two minutes of measuring is how you step around it.

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: WHY MEASURE (about 1.5 minutes)

So let me make the actual case for the number, because skipping it feels efficient and it really is not.

Without a baseline, better is just a feeling, and feelings fade. You will spend that satisfying hour, step back, feel good, and three weeks later you will have no honest way to tell whether the space actually improved or whether you just remember it fondly. A number fixes that. It turns a vague, guilty this space is a mess into a specific, almost cheerful this space is a fourteen, and the low scores are Standardize and Sustain. One of those sentences makes you feel bad. The other one tells you exactly what to do.

A baseline does three quiet things. First, it makes the problem specific, because a space is never just bad, it is bad in particular ways, and particular ways are fixable. Second, it gives you a number to beat, which turns the work into something with a visible score. And third, the one people miss, it protects you from fixing the wrong thing, which is exactly the trap I just described.

Think of a carpenter. Measure twice, cut once. Nobody accuses the carpenter of procrastinating. The two minutes you spend scoring your space is the measure-twice of home organizing. You are not stalling. You are aiming.

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: THE SIX QUESTIONS (about 2 minutes)

Okay, so how do you actually do it. The tool is one plain question per S, a score from zero to five, and a shape. Now, since this is audio, I want you to picture six dials, or just six numbers in a row in a notebook. Here are the six questions. Answer each one with a gut number, zero to five. Zero means this is the whole problem. Five means this is genuinely handled.

Sort. Of everything in this space, how much do you actually use?

Straighten. Does everything here have an obvious home, and can you find what you need without searching?

Shine. Is it clean, and free of the hidden stuff cleaning reveals, the dust traps, the sticky ring, the wobbly leg?

Safety. Is there anything here that could hurt someone, especially a child? The scissors at grabbing height, the cord across the walkway, the heavy thing balanced up high.

Standardize. Is the right way to keep this space obvious to anyone who walks up, or does the system live only in your head?

And Sustain. Is there any habit or routine keeping this space up, or does it just quietly drift back to chaos between heroic cleanups?

Now let me score the drop zone out loud, so you hear what an honest audit sounds like. Sort, most of it earns its place, that is a four. Straighten, mostly but not quite, a three. Shine, decent, another three. Safety, there are scissors right there at kid height, that is a two. Standardize, the system lives only in my head, that is a one. Sustain, nothing is holding it, it just drifts, one. Add those up. Fourteen out of thirty.

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: READ THE SHAPE (about 1.5 minutes)

Now here is the magic, and it works even though you cannot see a chart right now. When you line those scores up, they make a shape, and the shape tells a story.

If a space caves in hard on Sort, it is buried. Too much in it. Nothing else can be fixed until the volume comes down. Start by removing.

If a space is fine on Sort but collapses on Straighten, that is the opposite problem. It does not have too much, it has no homes. That space does not need a purge. It needs a key dish, a mail tray, a hook.

And then there is our drop zone. Good on the first few, then it falls off a cliff at the end, on Standardize and Sustain. This is the single most common shape in a home that has been organized before. It keeps sliding back because nothing was ever built to hold the fix in place. No visible standard, no small habit. It got tidied, and tidying always expires.

So notice what just happened. I have not touched the space, and I already know that throwing things away would be a waste of my afternoon, and that the real work is a simple labeled system and one ten-second habit. That is the audit earning its keep. Your lowest score is not a failure. It is a map, pointing directly at the cheapest relief in the room.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY (about 1.5 minutes)

So if you take one thing out of this episode, take this. Score the space, honest, fast, and kind.

Honest means score it as it actually is on an ordinary Tuesday, not on the rare day company is coming. A flattering audit is a lie you only tell yourself, and it points your effort the wrong way.

Fast means about ten seconds a question. Your first instinct is almost always right. The agonizing that comes after usually just talks you into a kinder number.

And kind, this is the one people forget. A low baseline is good news. Let me say that again, because it runs against every instinct. A space that scores a fourteen has sixteen points of relief sitting in it, available, and mostly cheap. A space that already scores a twenty-eight is pretty good, and there is not much joy left to mine from it. The worst spaces give the best wins. So when your number comes back low, do not wince. That low number is not a verdict on you. It is a measure of how much better your day is about to get for one focused hour.

And one quick guardrail. Do not audit the whole house at once. The audit is per area. Score the one space you chose. The house gets audited one target at a time, the same way it gets fixed.

## OUTRO (about 1 minute)

So here is where you are. You have a number, a shape, and a weakest S. That is more than most people ever know about a space before they start flinging things into bins. That is what prepared means in this book. Not perfect. Not even started. Just seen clearly, measured honestly, and aimed correctly.

So here is your soft homework, and it really does take two minutes. Go score the space you chose. Zero to five on each of the six questions. Add it up for a total out of thirty. Circle your lowest score, because that is your marching order. And then write the total and today's date somewhere you will actually find it again, because we are going to beat that number together, and watching it climb is going to feel like exactly what it is, which is proof.

That is the audit. A flashlight, not a clipboard. If you want it written out, the chapter is free in the online book, and there is a printable Home 6S Audit Form you can grab to score along. Take your baseline this week. And next time, we take the before photo, because the eye is generous, the eye forgives, and the eye quietly edits out the pile you have stopped noticing. The camera does not. I will catch you there.
