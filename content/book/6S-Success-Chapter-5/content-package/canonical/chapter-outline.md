# Chapter 5 Outline: The 6S Home Audit

A section-by-section map of the chapter as written in the final HTML and manuscript. This chapter measures the chosen target; it does not yet fix it.

## Opener: Look before you touch
A full-bleed image of a person crouched at the entryway drop zone with a notebook and coffee, just looking. Caption: "An audit is not a test you can fail. It is a flashlight. Before you change a single thing about your target, you are going to see it clearly, on purpose, with numbers."

## 1. Chapter Opening: The Word Nobody Likes
"Audit" is a cold, clipboard word. The chapter hands it back as something friendly. You chose a target in Chapter 4 (the example is the entryway drop zone). The urge is to skip to fixing. Resist for ten minutes. A messy space is never equally broken in all six ways; one or two S's are usually fine and one or two are the real problem. If you cannot tell which, you waste effort on the parts that were never the issue.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- Reframe an audit as a flashlight, not a grade
- Score any single space, fast, on all six S's
- Read the shape of a space to find its real problem
- Record a baseline number you can beat
- Aim your effort at the one S that buys the most relief

## 3. Why Measure Before You Move
Without a baseline, "better" is a feeling that fades, and you cannot tell whether the work held. A number makes the problem specific, gives you something to beat, and protects you from fixing the wrong thing (purging a space that needed homes). The audit is the measure-twice of home organizing. It comes before the work and before the photograph.
- Callout (6S Tip): You cannot improve what you have not honestly seen.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Skipping the audit because you already know the space is bad. You know it feels bad, not which S is broken.

## 4. The Six-S Snapshot
One question per S, a score zero to five, and a shape. The six questions: Sort (how much do you use?), Straighten (does everything have an obvious home?), Shine (is it clean, no hidden problems?), Safety (could anything hurt someone?), Standardize (is the right way obvious or only in your head?), Sustain (is any habit keeping it up, or does it drift?). Connect the scores into a shape.
- Hero infographic: The 6S Snapshot (six-spoke radar; example drop zone Sort 4, Straighten 3, Shine 3, Safety 2, Standardize 1, Sustain 1; baseline 14/30; Safety in slate)
- Definition box: Score zero to five on each S, add it up, write it down. Lowest score is your biggest opportunity. The total is the baseline you will beat.

## 5. What the Shape Tells You
The shape has a story. Caved in on Sort = buried, start by removing. Fine on Sort but low on Straighten = no homes, assign homes not a purge. Good early then collapses on Standardize and Sustain = organized before, keeps drifting because nothing held the fix; build a visible standard and a tiny habit. The lowest score is a map to the cheapest relief.
- Callout (6S Tip): Your lowest score is not a failure. It is a map.

## 6. Run the Audit on Your Target
Score your actual chosen space with the six questions, zero to five, written down. Worked example on the entryway drop zone: 14/30, fine on Sort, starving on Standardize and Sustain. Without the audit you might purge a space that did not need it; with it you know to build a system and a habit.
- Infographic: The Home 6S Audit scorecard (six questions, 0 to 5, baseline 14/30)
- Callout (Quick Win): Score your target now, in two minutes, from memory if you have to. An imperfect number beats no number.

## 7. Honest, Fast, and Kind
Three rules: Be honest (real numbers, not flattering ones, score it as an ordinary Tuesday). Be fast (ten seconds per question, trust the gut read). Be kind (a low baseline is good news; sixteen points of relief sit in a fourteen; the worst spaces give the best wins).
- Callout (Common Mistake): Auditing the whole house at once. The audit is per area. Score the one space you chose.

## 8. Your Baseline
You have a number, a shape, and a weakest S. Lock it in: write the total and date, circle the lowest score (your marching order). "Prepared" means seen clearly, measured honestly, aimed correctly. The carpenter has measured.
- Checklist (Take your baseline): score each S, add to a total /30, circle the lowest, write total and date, keep it to re-score after the work.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Each person audits their nominated space and reports its weakest S at dinner. Lowest single score goes first.

## 9. Chapter Close: A Number to Beat
The audit moved no objects but changed how you see the space, like learning to see friction in Chapter 1. Keep the number; the jump after the work will feel like proof. The eye forgives and edits out the pile; the camera does not. Next, the photograph.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): An audit is not a grade you take. It is a flashlight you aim. The lowest number is not bad news about you. It is good news about how much relief is sitting there, waiting to be claimed.
