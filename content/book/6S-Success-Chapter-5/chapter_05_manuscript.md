## Chapter Opening

Nobody has ever been excited to hear the word audit.

It is a cold word, a clipboard word, a word that arrives with bad news and reading glasses. So let me take it away from you for a second and hand it back as something friendlier, because the thing this chapter asks you to do is genuinely small, genuinely kind, and genuinely the difference between effort that works and effort that just makes you tired.

Here is the situation. In Chapter 4 you chose a target. One small, high-friction, low-effort space, written down, waiting for you. Maybe it is the entryway drop zone, that little table or shelf by the door where the keys and the mail and the sunglasses and the one stray glove all go to argue. I am going to use that drop zone as our example through this chapter, but everything here works on whatever space you actually chose.

You are motivated. You are ready. And the very natural urge, right now, is to skip straight to fixing it. Resist that for ten more minutes, because there is one quick step that will make every minute of fixing land where it actually helps.

Before you change a single thing, you are going to look at your target on purpose, and put a few honest numbers on it.

That is the whole audit. Not a clipboard. Not a grade. Just a calm, structured look at a space you have been walking past on autopilot for months, so that for the first time you see exactly what is working, what is not, and which of the six S's is the one actually dragging the space down. Because here is the thing nobody tells you about a messy space. It is never equally broken in all six ways. One or two of the S's are usually fine. One or two are usually the real problem. And if you cannot tell which, you will pour your energy into the parts that were never the issue and wonder why the space still feels off.

Two minutes of looking. That is what stands between you and effort that finally lands.

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Reframe an audit as a flashlight, not a grade
- Score any single space, fast, on all six S's
- Read the shape of a space to find its real problem
- Record a baseline number you can beat after the work
- Aim your effort at the one S that will buy the most ease

## Why Measure Before You Move

Let me make the case for the number, because skipping it feels efficient and is not.

Without a baseline, "better" is just a feeling, and feelings fade. You will spend a satisfying hour on your drop zone, step back, feel good, and three weeks later have no honest way to tell whether it actually improved or whether you just remember it fondly. Worse, you will have no idea whether the thing you fixed was the thing that was broken. A number fixes all of that. It turns a vague, guilty "this space is a mess" into a specific, almost cheerful "this space is a fourteen, and the low scores are Standardize and Sustain." One of those sentences makes you feel bad. The other tells you exactly what to do.

A baseline does three quiet, powerful things. First, it makes the problem specific. A space is never just bad. It is bad in particular ways, and the particular ways are fixable in a way that a general feeling of badness is not. Second, it gives you a number to beat, which turns the work into something with a visible score, and a visible score is motivating in a way that a tidy feeling is not. Third, and this is the one people miss, it protects you from fixing the wrong thing. The drop zone that feels chaotic might actually have a perfectly fine amount of stuff. Its real problem might be that nothing has a home. If you spend your hour ruthlessly throwing things away, when the actual issue was the lack of a key dish and a mail tray, you will have less stuff and the same chaos, and you will conclude that 6S does not work. It works. You just aimed at the wrong S because you never measured.

This is the entire reason the audit comes before the work, and before even the photograph in the next chapter. You are not stalling. You are aiming. A carpenter measures twice and cuts once, and nobody accuses the carpenter of procrastinating. The two minutes you spend scoring your space is the measure-twice of home organizing. It is the least glamorous step and one of the most important.

> **6S Tip**
> You cannot improve what you have not honestly seen. Two minutes of looking saves an hour of fixing the wrong thing.

> **Common Mistake**
> Skipping the audit because you already know the space is bad. You know it feels bad. You do not yet know which of the six things is actually broken, and that is the only useful information.

## The Six-S Snapshot

Here is the tool. It is one question per S, a score from zero to five, and a shape.

You already met the six S's in Chapters 2 and 3, so you are not learning anything new here. You are just pointing each one at your chosen space like a flashlight and asking, honestly, how is this space doing on this one thing? Score it zero, for "this is the whole problem," up to five, for "this is genuinely handled." Do not agonize. A gut number you write down in ten seconds is worth far more than a perfect number you never get around to.

Ask the six questions in order.

**Sort.** Of everything in this space, how much do you actually use? A five means almost everything here earns its place. A zero means the space is mostly things that belong somewhere else, or nowhere.

**Straighten.** Does everything here have an obvious home, and can you find what you need without searching? A five means your hand knows where things live. A zero means everything floats.

**Shine.** Is it clean, and free of the hidden problems that cleaning reveals, the dust traps, the sticky ring, the wobbly leg, the slow wear? A five means it is genuinely cared for. A zero means nobody has really looked in a while.

**Safety.** Is there anything here that could hurt someone, especially a child? The scissors at grabbing height, the cord across the walkway, the heavy thing balanced up high. A five means it is sound. A zero means there is a real hazard sitting in plain sight.

**Standardize.** Is the right way to keep this space obvious to anyone who walks up to it, or does the system live only in your head? A five means the space explains itself. A zero means only you know where anything goes, and only on a good day.

**Sustain.** Is there any habit or routine keeping this space up, or does it just quietly drift back to chaos between heroic cleanups? A five means it maintains itself with almost no effort. A zero means it survives only on willpower, which is to say it does not survive.

Now connect the six scores into a shape, and the shape will tell you more in one glance than an hour of staring ever could.

## What the Shape Tells You

The reason the snapshot is a shape and not just a list is that the shape has a story, and once you can read it you will diagnose a space in seconds.

A space that caves in toward the center on Sort is buried. There is simply too much in it, and nothing else can be fixed until the volume comes down. You could give it homes and clean it and it would still feel awful, because the real problem is quantity. The shape tells you to start by removing, not arranging.

A space that scores fine on Sort but collapses on Straighten is a different animal. It does not have too much. It has no homes. The right amount of stuff is here, but it floats, landing wherever there is a free inch, because nothing has a designated place. This space does not need a purge. It needs a key dish, a mail tray, a hook. The shape tells you to assign homes, not to throw things away.

And then there is the space that scores well on the first few S's and falls off a cliff on Standardize and Sustain. This is the most common shape in a home that has been "organized" before, and it is the drop zone in our example. Look at it. Sort is a four, Straighten and Shine are decent, and then the floor drops out at the end. That shape is not telling you the space is dirty or overstuffed. It is telling you that this space has been fixed before, probably more than once, and it keeps sliding back because nothing was ever built to hold the fix in place. No visible standard. No small habit. It got tidied, and tidying always expires. The shape is telling you that your hour should go almost entirely into making the good arrangement obvious and giving it one tiny routine to keep it alive.

See what just happened. You have not touched the space, and you already know that throwing things away would be a waste of your afternoon, and that the real work is a labeled system and a ten-second habit. That is the snapshot earning its keep. The lowest score is not an insult. It is a map, and it is pointing directly at the cheapest relief in the room.

> **6S Tip**
> Your lowest score is not a failure. It is a map. It is telling you exactly where an hour of effort will buy the most relief.

## Run the Audit on Your Target

Enough explaining. Let us score your actual space, the one you chose last chapter.

Go stand in front of it, or near it, or just bring it clearly to mind if you are reading on the couch and fully intend to stay there. Take the six questions and answer each one with a number from zero to five. Say them out loud if it helps. Write the numbers down, in a row, in whatever notebook or note app you will be able to find again in a month, because you are going to want this number later.

Fourteen out of thirty. Notice what that number is and is not. It is not a grade on your character, your worth, or your housekeeping. It is a measurement of one small table by a door, taken so that you can make it better and prove that you did. And notice the pattern, because the pattern is the point. The drop zone is not failing at everything. It is fine on Sort, decent in the middle, and starving on Standardize and Sustain. Without the audit, you might have spent your hour purging a space that did not need purging. With it, you know to spend that hour building a simple, visible system and one small habit to hold it. Same hour. Completely different result.

> **Quick Win**
> Score your target space right now, in two minutes, from memory if you have to. An imperfect number beats no number. You can always re-score later with the space right in front of you.

## Honest, Fast, and Kind

Three small rules will keep this audit useful instead of stressful.

Be honest. The audit only works if the numbers are real. The temptation is to round up out of pride, to give the space a generous four on Sustain because you cleaned it once in March. Resist that as firmly as you resisted staging the before photo back in Chapter 1. A flattering audit is a lie you tell only yourself, and it points your effort in the wrong direction. The space does not care about your feelings. Score it as it actually is on an ordinary Tuesday, not as it is on the rare day company is coming.

Be fast. This is a gut check, not a doctoral thesis. The right amount of time to spend on each question is about ten seconds. Your first instinct is almost always accurate, and the agonizing that comes after it usually just talks you into a kinder number. Trust the quick read. You can always re-score later, and you will, after the work, to see the jump.

And be kind, which is the one people forget. A low baseline is good news. Read that again, because it runs against every instinct. A space that scores a fourteen has sixteen points of relief sitting in it, available, mostly cheap. A space that scores a twenty-eight is already pretty good and there is not much joy left to mine from it. So when your target comes back low, do not wince. The low number is not a verdict. It is a measure of how much better your day is about to get for one focused hour of effort. The worst spaces give the best wins. That is a genuinely lovely thing about all of this.

> **Common Mistake**
> Auditing the whole house at once. The audit is per area. Score the one space you chose in Chapter 4. The house gets audited one target at a time, the same way it gets fixed.

## Your Baseline

So here is where you are. You have a number, a shape, and a weakest S. That is everything the audit was supposed to give you, and it is more than most people ever know about a space before they start flinging things into bins.

Lock it in. Write the total and today's date somewhere you will find it again. Circle, star, or simply remember the lowest score, because that one number is your marching order. It is where the bulk of your effort will go and where the biggest win will come from. You are no longer facing a vague, intimidating mess. You are facing a fourteen with a soft spot at Standardize and Sustain, and you know precisely what to do about it.

That is what "prepared" means in this book. Not perfect. Not even started. Just seen clearly, measured honestly, and aimed correctly. That is preparation doing its quiet job. The next two short steps in Part 2 will sharpen the aim further, and then, in Part 3, you finally get to begin.

> **Take your baseline**
> - Score your target zero to five on each S, using the six questions.
> - Add the six scores for a total out of thirty.
> - Circle your lowest score. That is where the work will mostly go.
> - Write the total and the date in your notebook or phone.
> - Keep it. You will re-score after the work and watch the number climb.

> **Family Challenge**
> Each person audits the space they nominated last chapter and reports its weakest S at dinner. Lowest single score wins the right to go first, because that space has the most room to breathe waiting in it.

## A Number to Beat

You came into this chapter braced for a clipboard and a grade. You leave it with a flashlight and a number, which are much friendlier things to carry.

The audit did not change your space. Not one object moved. But something real changed anyway, the same way it did when you first learned to see friction back in Chapter 1. You can no longer look at your target as a vague, guilty mess. You see a specific shape now, with specific dents, and you know exactly where an hour of work will land hardest. That clarity is worth far more than the two minutes it cost, and it is the whole reason we measure before we move.

Keep your number close. We are going to beat it together, and when we re-score this space after the work, the jump from fourteen to something much higher is going to feel like exactly what it is, which is proof. For now, you have done the prepared thing. You have seen your space honestly and put an honest number on it.

Next, we make that baseline impossible to argue with. Because the eye is generous, and the eye forgives, and the eye quietly edits out the pile you have stopped noticing. The camera does not. In the next chapter we take the picture.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> An audit is not a grade you take. It is a flashlight you aim. The lowest number is not bad news about you. It is good news about how much relief is sitting there, waiting to be claimed.
