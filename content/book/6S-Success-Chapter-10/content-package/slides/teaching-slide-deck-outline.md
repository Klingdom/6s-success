### Slide 2 · The two loose ends

- Chapter 9 ended honestly, with two things unfinished, and they are not the same problem. The first is the out box in the corner, full of unnecessary things. It simply waits; where it goes, given away, sold, kept elsewhere, recycled, or thrown out, is the next chapter's job.
- The second is the one in your hands: a few objects that would not answer the use question, where the honest reply was I do not know. Apart is not a plan, so the maybes need a system.
- And hovering over it all is a late voice, the second thoughts, which needs a plan of its own. Two loose ends, and both are about not being sure.

### Slide 3 · You cannot force a maybe

- Here is the mistake a tidy, motivated person makes at exactly this point: carried by the momentum of the easy calls, you force a verdict onto the thing you cannot read, because forcing feels braver than pausing.
- Force a keep and it goes back on the shelf undecided in all but name. Force a release and you send off a thing you were not ready to let go. That is precisely how remorse is manufactured: not from letting go, but from letting go before you had an answer.
- A genuine maybe does not have an answer yet. Pushing harder only produces a guess wearing the costume of a decision.

### Slide 4 · A maybe is honest

- Read this line twice, because it turns the whole thing around.
- You were never lacking the nerve to decide. You were trying to supply, out of willpower, an answer that only one thing can give you, which is time.
- So we stop forcing and build the system instead: a tag, a home, and a deadline. You are allowed to not know.

### Slide 5 · The Red Tag

- The tool is a tag, scaled down from the factory floor, where something that could not be judged on the spot got a red tag, a marked area, and a set stretch of time to let the days decide. It is the gentlest tool in the book, because it is the one that lets you not know.
- A red tag carries three things, and only three: what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date. A scrap of paper, a strip of tape, an index card on string; the material does not matter and neither does neatness. Those three lines do.
- The date you tagged it is the line people skip and the one that keeps the area honest, telling you at a glance how long a thing has waited.

### Slide 6 · Time makes the call

- Set the tagged thing aside, out of the daily flow, and get on with your day. From there the tag decides for you, and it can only end two ways.
- Reach for it before its decide-by date, a real morning when your hand actually goes to it, and it just proved it was necessary. Pull the tag and keep it, no second-guessing.
- Let the date pass and you never once reached for it, and the maybe just became a no. Not a verdict on you, just the quiet fact that when given real time to matter, it did not. It joins the out box.

### Slide 7 · The Holding Area

- A tagged thing needs somewhere to wait, and that is a holding area: one known spot, out of the daily flow, where the red-tagged maybes sit out their deadlines together. A labeled box, a bin in the hall closet, a single reserved drawer.
- The form does not matter. What matters is that it is one spot, off the path you actually walk, and that everything in it is wearing a date.
- With a deadline, the box empties itself on a schedule: things you reached for leave by the keep door, things you did not leave by the out-box door, so it does not fill up and stay full.

### Slide 8 · The date is the whole point

- Be blunt about the one part that matters most.
- The deadline is what separates a holding area from a junk drawer, because the deadline is the thing that eventually forces an answer. Without it, the maybe waits forever, you feel vaguely productive for having boxed it, and nothing has actually been decided.
- A holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile.

### Slide 9 · Match the date, keep the appointment

- Match the decide-by date to the thing. Give an everyday object about a month, long enough for its ordinary use to come around. Give a seasonal thing a season, because a snow shovel or a beach umbrella cannot prove itself in July or January.
- The window should be long enough for real life to test the thing, and short enough that the maybe cannot become permanent by default.
- Then keep the appointment. Put the dates where you will see them and settle whatever they govern when they come up, because a deadline you do not honor is not a deadline; it is a wish.

### Slide 10 · A red-tag pass on the entryway

- Tag the two maybes, because a system is easier to trust once you have watched it run. This is a short pass; there are only two, and that is normal, because the great majority of any real sort is easy yeses and easy noes.
- The gift umbrella: a fine umbrella you never grab, tangled with a flicker of rudeness the use question cannot cut. Tag it. Date tagged: today. Decide-by: one season, because an umbrella needs weather to prove itself.
- The folding poncho, used exactly once: might be handy, might perish in a bin. Tag it. Decide-by: a month, or the next trip, whichever comes first. Two objects, two tags, two dates, and not a single forced guess.

### Slide 11 · Sorter's remorse, in two kinds

- The second loose end has nothing to do with the objects and everything to do with the fear around them, and it comes in two kinds so different that treating them the same is why the fear wins.
- Anticipated remorse arrives before anything is gone: the what-if that shows up the instant your hand moves toward the out box. It is all imagined, and the holding area is its cure, because while a thing waits under a red tag it is not gone. It is dated, retrievable, one shelf away.
- Real remorse is rarer and more real: the date passes, the thing is truly gone, and weeks later you miss it. It happens. But look honestly at how often, because the fear badly overstates it.

### Slide 12 · The arithmetic of letting go

- Think back over everything you have ever cleared out of a home, then count the things you genuinely, materially missed. For most people the honest number is close to zero, and the few real ones were cheap and easy to replace.
- Keeping everything to guard against a small, rare, replaceable regret guarantees the opposite: the daily, certain weight of living among things you do not use, paid in space and attention every single day.
- The red tag and the holding area are simply how you stop letting the loud fear outvote the quiet math.

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the reframe set in Fraunces medium #2B2622, verbatim: "You almost never regret what you let go. You fear you will, and the fear is louder than the loss." No dots, no sparks; slate does the work. Small Inter caption: "a large certain cost, traded for a small unlikely one."
