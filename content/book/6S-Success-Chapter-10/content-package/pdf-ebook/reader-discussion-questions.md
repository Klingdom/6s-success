# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Red Tags, Holding Areas, and Sorter's Remorse

*Chapter 10, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter separates two loose ends: the out box, which simply waits, and the honest maybes still in your hands, which are today's whole job. In a space you have sorted, which things were genuine maybes rather than easy yeses or easy noes? What made them hard to call?

2. The chapter warns that forcing a verdict onto an uncertain thing is how passes stall or breed regret: "not from letting go, but from letting go before you had an answer." Have you ever released something before you were ready and later felt it? What told you it was too soon?

3. The frozen line is "A maybe is not a failure of nerve. It is honest, and honesty gets a system, not a shove." Where have you treated not-knowing as a lack of willpower rather than accurate information about an object whose future you cannot yet see?

4. A red tag carries three lines: what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date. The chapter argues the middle line, the date you tagged it, is the one people skip and quietly the most useful. Why might knowing how long a thing has waited matter more than it first appears?

5. The chapter says time is the one judge that cannot be argued with: reach for the thing before its date and it earned its keep; let the date pass untouched and the maybe became a no. How is handing the verdict to time different from trying to feel certain on a schedule?

6. "A holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile." Where in your home is there a box or drawer of undated maybes, indecision moved into a tidier container? What would putting a date on it change?

7. The chapter says to match the decide-by date to the thing: about a month for an everyday object, a season for a seasonal one. Pick one maybe you could tag. What window would give it a fair chance to prove itself without letting it become permanent by default?

8. "A deadline you do not honor is not a deadline; it is a wish." What would help you actually keep the appointment when a decide-by date comes up, on a calendar, on your phone, on the lid of the box itself?

9. Sorter's remorse comes in two kinds. Anticipated remorse is the what-if felt before anything is even gone. How does knowing a tagged thing is not gone, only waiting one shelf away, change the fear that shows up as your hand moves toward the out box?

10. The reframe for the rarer, real regret is "You almost never regret what you let go. You fear you will, and the fear is louder than the loss." Count the things you have genuinely missed after clearing a space. What is the honest number, and what does it tell you about the fear?

11. The chapter says neither kind of remorse is a reason to keep everything, and that keeping everything to guard against a small, rare regret guarantees the daily, certain cost of living among things you do not use. Where have you paid that daily cost to avoid an unlikely one?

12. The friction meter holds this chapter, because nothing new left the room; a faint dashed arrow marks where it steps next, when the decide-by dates pass. Why might an honest hold, with a projection of the next step, build more trust than a gauge that swings to reward the effort?
