## 1. Chapter Opening: The Last Two You Could Not Answer
You are almost done with the entryway, and the last two things on your hands are the two you could not answer. The console is clear and holding its five working things, the overflow basket sits empty, and an out box waits in the corner with the unnecessary things. In your hands are one or two honest maybes, not clutter and not keepers. There is also a quieter thing waiting: the late voice that asks, a day after you let something go, whether you should have. So this chapter is short and specific. It handles the two things Chapter 9 could not resolve: a calm system for the genuine maybes, and a way to handle the second thoughts. Neither needs willpower; both need a little structure.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- Handle an item you cannot decide, without forcing a guess
- Write a red tag: what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date
- Set up a holding area where tagged maybes wait for their deadline
- Match a decide-by date to the kind of thing you are tagging
- Quiet the second thoughts, both the fear before and the rare regret after

## 3. The Two Loose Ends
Name the two things Chapter 9 left, because they are not the same problem and do not get the same tool. The first is the out box: it holds the unnecessary things and is not this chapter's business. Deciding a thing is unnecessary and deciding where it goes are separate acts; where the out box goes, given away, sold, kept elsewhere, recycled, or thrown out, gets the next chapter to itself. For now it simply waits. The second loose end is the one in your hands, and it is today's whole job: a few objects would not answer the use question, and the honest reply was I do not know. Apart is not a plan, so the maybes need a system, and the late voice, the second thoughts, needs a plan of its own.

## 4. You Cannot Force a Maybe
The mistake a tidy, motivated person makes here feels like discipline and is the opposite. Carried by the momentum of the easy calls, you force a verdict onto the thing you cannot read. Either you force a keep, and it goes back on the shelf undecided in all but name, or you force a release and send to the out box a thing you were not ready to let go, which is precisely how remorse is manufactured: not from letting go, but from letting go before you had an answer. A genuine maybe does not have an answer yet; pushing harder only produces a guess wearing the costume of a decision. You are allowed to not know. So stop forcing and build the system instead.
- Pull-quote (frozen): A maybe is not a failure of nerve. It is honest, and honesty gets a system, not a shove.

## 5. The Red Tag
The tool is a tag, borrowed and scaled down from the factory floor, where something that could not be judged on the spot got a red tag, a marked area, and a set stretch of time to let the days decide. It is the gentlest tool in the book, because it is the one that lets you not know. A red tag carries three things and only three: what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date. The middle line, the date you tagged it, is the one people skip and the one that keeps the area honest, telling you at a glance how long a thing has waited. Set the thing aside, and the tag ends one of two ways: reach for it before the date and it proved necessary, so untag it; let the date pass untouched and the maybe became a no, so it joins the out box. The tag changes the fear, because you are no longer choosing between keeping forever and releasing now; you are choosing to find out, and time does the finding.
- Hero infographic: The red tag, close up (three labeled lines, "what it is", "date tagged", "decide-by", with two branches: reached for it before the date, keep it, untag (green); date passed, never reached for it, the out box (slate))
- Definition box (Red-tagging): Red-tagging is how you decide the things you cannot decide: label an uncertain item with a decide-by date, set it in a holding area out of the daily flow, and let the deadline answer. Reach for it in time and it earned its keep. Let the date pass untouched and the maybe just became a no.

## 6. The Holding Area
A tagged thing needs somewhere to wait: a holding area, one known spot out of the daily flow, where red-tagged maybes sit out their deadlines together. A labeled box, a bin in the hall closet, a single reserved drawer. What matters is that it is one spot, out of the path you walk, and everything in it is wearing a date. That last part is the whole game: a holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile. The deadline is what separates a holding area from a junk drawer, because it eventually forces an answer and the box empties itself on a schedule. Match the decide-by date to the thing: about a month for an everyday object, a season for a seasonal one. And keep the appointment: put the dates where you will see them and settle whatever they govern, because a deadline you do not honor is not a deadline; it is a wish.
- Infographic: The holding area on a timeline (tagged items enter the holding area, run to a decide-by-date marker, then split to two exits: reached for it, keep (green), or on to the out box, Chapter 11 (slate))
- Callout (6S Tip): A holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile. The date is the whole point; without it, the maybe waits forever and you have decided nothing.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Letting the holding area become permanent. If you never set the decide-by date or never honor it, you have not made a decision, you have only moved the indecision into a tidier box.

## 7. A Red-Tag Pass on the Entryway
Tag the two maybes, because a system is easier to trust once you have watched it run. This is a short pass; there are only two, and that is normal, because the great majority of any real sort is easy yeses and easy noes. The first is a good umbrella that was a gift, tangled with a feeling the use question cannot cut, so tag it: the gift umbrella, dated today, decide-by one season, because an umbrella needs weather to prove itself. The second is a folding rain poncho used exactly once, tag it: the folding poncho, dated today, decide-by a month or the next trip, whichever comes first. Both go to the holding area. That is the entire pass: two objects, two tags, two dates, not a single forced guess. You handed both questions to time and went to make coffee.
- Callout (Quick Win): Red-tag one honest maybe right now. On a slip of tape write what it is and a decide-by date, stick it on, and set the thing in one spot out of the way. If you reach for it before the date, it stays. If not, the decision already made itself.

## 8. Sorter's Remorse
The second loose end has nothing to do with the objects and everything to do with the fear around them. Sorter's remorse comes in two kinds. The first arrives before anything is gone, call it anticipated remorse: the what-if that shows up the instant your hand moves toward the out box. All of it is imagined, and the holding area is its cure, because while a thing waits under a red tag it is not gone; it is dated, retrievable, one shelf away, so the fear has nothing to grip. The system lets you act while unsure. The second kind is rarer and more real: sometimes the date passes, the thing is truly gone, and weeks later you actually miss it. But look honestly at how often, because the fear badly overstates it. Keeping everything to guard against a small, rare, replaceable regret guarantees the daily, certain cost of living among things you do not use. Neither kind is a reason to keep everything.
- Pull-quote (frozen): You almost never regret what you let go. You fear you will, and the fear is louder than the loss.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Everyone red-tags one "I am not sure" thing with the same decide-by date, and you all agree not to argue about anyone else's tags until the date comes. Whatever no one reached for by then goes, no debate.

## 9. The Needle Holds
Go and look at the friction meter, and this time watch it not move, because that is the honest reading. Last chapter the needle stepped toward calm because objects left the room. This chapter, be honest: what actually left? Nothing. The maybes were already set apart; today you gave them a tag, a date, and a shelf, and quieted the second thoughts. That is real work, but it is not a clearing, so the needle holds exactly where Chapter 9 left it. This is the rule the meter has followed since Chapter 8: a gauge that swings when nothing has physically changed is just flattery. But just ahead of the live needle is something new, a faint dashed arrow pointing a short way further toward calm: the projection, a promise the tags have already made, for when the decide-by dates pass and the untouched maybes convert to the out box. The goal crosshair remains ahead of even that.

## 10. Chapter Close: A System for Not Being Sure
Look at the entryway now: the console clear, the basket empty, the out box waiting, and on a shelf out of the way two tagged things with dates on them, quietly running out their clocks. Nothing about that scene required you to be certain, which is the point of the whole chapter. You did not force a single call you did not have; you gave the honest maybes a tag, a home, and a deadline, and met the fear that guards clutter by naming its two shapes and giving each its answer, all without a shred of shame. Which leaves exactly one loose end, the out box in the corner, now genuinely finished and settled, needing only a destination, which is precisely where we go next.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): You do not have to be sure about everything today. Tag the maybes, give them a decide-by date, and let the one thing you cannot argue with, time, make the call for you.
