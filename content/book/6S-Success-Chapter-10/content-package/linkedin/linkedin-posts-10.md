## 1. The honest maybe

You are almost done. The clear yeses and clear noes are made. And in your hands are one or two things that refused to answer: you do not use them, exactly, but you cannot quite say to let them go.

Those are not clutter and they are not keepers. They are honest maybes. Setting them apart was the right move, but apart is not a plan. A maybe left in its own little pile is just a decision you keep postponing.

A maybe is not a failure of nerve. It is honest, and honesty gets a system, not a shove.

## 2. You cannot force a maybe

Here is the mistake a tidy, motivated person makes. The momentum of the easy calls is still with you, so you pick up the thing you cannot read and force a verdict onto it anyway.

One of two things happens, both bad. You force a keep, and the maybe goes back on the shelf undecided in everything but name. Or you force a release, and that is precisely how remorse is manufactured: not from letting go, but from letting go before you had an answer.

A genuine maybe does not have an answer yet. Pushing harder only produces a guess wearing the costume of a decision.

## 3. The red tag

For a thing you honestly cannot call, you do not force a guess. You tag it. The idea is borrowed from the factory floor: when something could not be judged on the spot, nobody guessed. They hung a red tag on it and let time decide.

A red tag carries three things, and only three: what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date. A scrap of paper or a strip of tape will do. Neatness does not matter. Those three lines do.

Then you set it aside, and the tag decides for you.

## 4. The line people skip

A red tag has three lines, and the middle one is the one people skip: the date you tagged it. It feels redundant sitting next to the decide-by date, as though writing down today adds nothing.

But it is quietly the most useful of the three. Weeks from now, when you open the holding area and find a half-forgotten tagged thing, the tagged date tells you the truth at a glance: this has waited a long time on a question I keep not answering, or it only went in last week.

That small stamp of when keeps the whole area honest.

## 5. A nicer junk pile

A tagged thing needs somewhere to wait, and that somewhere is a holding area: one known spot, out of the daily flow, where the maybes sit out their deadlines together.

But the box only works if everything in it is wearing a date. That is the whole game.

A holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile. A box of undated maybes is not a decision and it is not progress. It is the same indecision you started with, moved into a tidier container and given a calmer name.

The deadline is what eventually forces an answer.

## 6. Match the date to the thing

How long should a maybe wait? Match the decide-by date to the thing.

Give an everyday object about a month, long enough for its ordinary use to come around if it is going to. Give a seasonal thing a season, because a snow shovel or a beach umbrella cannot prove itself in July or January, and it would be unfair to ask.

The point is not the exact number of days. The window should be long enough for real life to test the thing, and short enough that the maybe cannot become permanent by default.

## 7. Keep the appointment

Here is the habit most people forget: you have to keep the appointment.

Put the decide-by dates somewhere you will actually see them, on a calendar, in your phone, on the lid of the box itself. When a date comes up, open the holding area and settle whatever it governs. Reached for it since you tagged it? Untag it and keep it. Never touched it? It joins the out box.

The check takes a minute, and it is the minute the whole system rests on. A deadline you do not honor is not a deadline; it is a wish.

## 8. The fear before

Sorter's remorse comes in two kinds. The first arrives before anything is even gone. Call it anticipated remorse: the what-if that shows up the instant your hand moves. What if I need it. What if the one week I release the poncho is the week it pours.

Notice that all of this is imagined. Nothing has actually left. And here is the quiet gift of the holding area: while a thing waits under a red tag, it is not gone. It is right there, dated, retrievable, one shelf away. So the fear has nothing to grip.

The system lets you act while unsure, which is the only way anyone ever acts at all.

## 9. The fear is louder than the loss

The second kind of remorse is rarer and more real. Sometimes the date passes, the thing is gone, and weeks later you actually miss it. This happens, and I will not pretend it never does. But look honestly at how often.

You almost never regret what you let go. You fear you will, and the fear is louder than the loss.

Count the things you genuinely missed after clearing a home. For most people the honest number is close to zero, and the few real ones were cheap to replace. Keeping everything to guard against a small, rare regret guarantees the daily, certain cost of living among things you do not use.

## 10. You do not have to be sure today

Look at the entryway now. The console is clear, the basket is empty, and on a shelf out of the way sit two tagged things with dates on them, quietly running out their clocks. Nothing about that scene required you to be certain.

You did not force a single call you did not have. You gave the honest maybes a tag, a home, and a deadline, and you handed the verdict to the one judge you cannot argue with, which is time.

You do not have to be sure about everything today.
