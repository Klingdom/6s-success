# The Two Things You Could Not Answer

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Last week we ran the Use Test and made the clear calls. You are almost done with the entryway now, and the last two things on your hands are the two you could not answer. You do not use them, exactly, but you cannot quite say to let them go. They are not clutter and they are not keepers. They are honest maybes, and you set them apart rather than force a call you did not have.

There is also a quieter thing waiting, and you may have already felt it. A day after you let something go, a small voice sometimes asks whether you should have. It arrives late, after the box is packed. That voice has a name in this work, and it deserves a plan of its own, because left alone it will creep back into every pass you ever run and talk you out of finishing.

So this chapter is short and specific. It builds a calm system for the genuine maybes, and it handles the second thoughts. Neither needs willpower. Both need a little structure.

## You cannot force a maybe

Here is the mistake a tidy, motivated person makes. The momentum of the easy calls is still with you, so you pick up the thing you cannot read and force a verdict onto it anyway. One of two things happens, both bad. You force a keep, and the maybe goes back on the shelf undecided in everything but name. Or you force a release, and that is precisely how remorse is manufactured: not from letting go, but from letting go before you had an answer.

A genuine maybe does not have an answer yet. That is what makes it a maybe. Pushing harder only produces a guess wearing the costume of a decision. So the honest move is to stop trying to feel certain on a schedule. You are allowed to not know.

**A maybe is not a failure of nerve. It is honest, and honesty gets a system, not a shove.**

## The red tag

The tool is a tag, borrowed from the factory floor. When something on the line could not be judged on the spot, nobody guessed. They hung a red tag on it, moved it to a marked area for a set stretch of time, and let the days decide. It scales down to a home almost perfectly.

A red tag carries three things, and only three: **what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date.** A scrap of paper or a strip of tape will do. The middle line is the one people skip, and the one that keeps the whole area honest, because weeks later it tells you at a glance whether a thing has waited a long time on a question you keep not answering, or only went in last week.

Then time decides. Reach for the thing before its decide-by date and it earned its keep, so untag it. Let the date pass untouched and the maybe just became a no, and it joins the out box.

## The holding area

A tagged thing needs somewhere to wait: a holding area, one known spot out of the daily flow where the maybes sit out their deadlines together. A labeled box, a bin, a reserved drawer. What matters is that everything in it is wearing a date.

**A holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile.**

So match the decide-by date to the thing: about a month for an everyday object, a season for a seasonal one, because a snow shovel or a beach umbrella cannot prove itself in July or January. And keep the appointment. Put the dates where you will see them, and settle whatever they govern when they come up. A deadline you do not honor is not a deadline; it is a wish.

## Sorter's remorse

Now the second thoughts. Sorter's remorse comes in two kinds, and treating them the same is why the fear wins.

The first arrives before anything is gone. The what-if that shows up as your hand moves: what if I need it. Notice that all of this is imagined. And here is the quiet gift of the holding area: while a thing waits under a red tag, it is not gone. It is one shelf away. So the fear has nothing to grip. The system lets you act while unsure, which is the only way anyone ever acts at all.

The second is rarer and more real, and the fear badly overstates it.

**You almost never regret what you let go. You fear you will, and the fear is louder than the loss.**

Count the things you genuinely missed after clearing a home. For most people the honest number is close to zero, and the few real ones were cheap to replace. Keeping everything to guard against a small, rare regret guarantees the daily, certain cost of living among things you do not use.

## The assignment

Red-tag one honest maybe right now. On a slip of tape write what it is and a decide-by date, stick it on, and set the thing in one spot out of the way. If you reach for it before the date, it stays. If not, the decision already made itself.

You do not have to be sure about everything today. Tag the maybes, give them a decide-by date, and let the one thing you cannot argue with, time, make the call for you.
