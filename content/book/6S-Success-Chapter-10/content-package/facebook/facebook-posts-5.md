## Post 1 · You cannot force a maybe

You get to the end of a good sort and there are one or two things left that just will not answer. You do not really use them, but you cannot quite say to let them go. So you stand there and try to be firm, and you force a call anyway, because forcing feels braver than pausing.

Here is the trouble. A genuine maybe does not have an answer yet. That is what makes it a maybe. Push harder and you do not get a decision, you get a guess wearing the costume of one, made just to end the discomfort of not knowing.

So stop trying to feel certain on a schedule. Uncertainty about one umbrella is not a crack in your resolve. It is just accurate information about a thing whose future you cannot yet see.

A maybe is not a failure of nerve. It is honest, and honesty gets a system, not a shove.

## Post 2 · Three lines on a tag

When you cannot decide about a thing, you do not have to. You tag it and let time decide instead.

The idea is borrowed from the factory floor. When something on the line could not be judged on the spot, nobody stood there agonizing over it. They hung a red tag on it, moved it out of the way for a set stretch of time, and let the days make the call.

At home a red tag carries three things, and only three: what it is, the date you tagged it, and a decide-by date.

A scrap of paper, a strip of masking tape, an index card on a string. The material does not matter and neither does neatness. What matters is those three lines. What it is, so a future you knows what was in question. The date you tagged it, so you can see how long it has waited. And the decide-by date, which does the actual work, because it is the moment the thing stops being your problem and becomes time's.

For the thing you cannot decide, this is the whole tool. More in Chapter 10 of 6S Success: Home Edition, free to read online.

## Post 3 · A nicer junk pile

Once a thing is tagged, it needs somewhere to wait. That somewhere is a holding area: one known spot, out of the daily flow, where the tagged maybes sit out their deadlines together. A labeled box on a shelf. A bin in the hall closet. A single drawer you agree to reserve.

But here is the part most people get wrong, so let me be blunt about it.

A holding area without a deadline is just a nicer junk pile.

A box of undated maybes is not a decision and it is not progress. It is the same indecision you started with, moved into a tidier container and given a calmer name. The deadline is the thing that eventually forces an answer. Without it, the maybe waits forever, you feel vaguely productive for having boxed it, and nothing has actually been decided.

So match the date to the thing. Give an everyday object about a month. Give a seasonal thing a season, because a snow shovel cannot prove itself in July. Long enough for real life to test it, short enough that the maybe cannot become permanent by default.

## Post 4 · Two things I could not call

Let me run this on two real things so you can see how fast it goes. By my front door, after a good sort, two objects would not answer.

The first is a good umbrella that was a gift. Better than the one I actually grab, and I never grab it, and every time I think of releasing it I feel a flicker of rudeness, as though giving up the umbrella gives up the person. That flicker is exactly why I cannot call it cleanly. So I do not. Tag it. What it is: the gift umbrella. Date tagged: today. Decide-by: one season, because an umbrella needs weather to prove itself.

The second is a folding rain poncho I used exactly once. Might be handy on some future trip, might sit in a bin until it perishes. I genuinely cannot say. So I do not. Tag it. What it is: the folding poncho. Date tagged: today. Decide-by: a month, or the next trip, whichever comes first.

Both go on the shelf with their dates. Two objects, two tags, two dates, and not a single forced guess. Then I closed the holding area and went to make coffee, which is the correct next step.

## Post 5 · The fear is louder than the loss

Here is where a good pass most often dies, and it has nothing to do with the objects. It is the small voice that asks, a day later, whether you should have let something go.

It comes in two kinds. The first shows up before anything is even gone: what if I need it, what if the one week I release the poncho is the week it pours. Notice that all of it is imagined. And here is the quiet gift of the holding area: while a thing waits under a red tag, it is not gone. It is right there, dated, one shelf away. So the fear has nothing to grip.

The second kind is rarer and more real. Sometimes the date passes, the thing is truly gone, and weeks later you actually miss it. It happens. But look honestly at how often.

You almost never regret what you let go. You fear you will, and the fear is louder than the loss.

Count the things you genuinely missed after clearing a home. For most people the honest number is close to zero, and the few real ones were cheap to replace. You are allowed to let a thing go and be at peace about it, and most of the time you will be.
