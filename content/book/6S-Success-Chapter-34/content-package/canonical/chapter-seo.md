## Search intent
Getting one real room, the dining room, genuinely clean and cared for, its good things safe and its finishes unharmed, after the reader has learned the whole 6S method and wants to apply it to the room the house gathers in. This chapter is the fourth of Part Nine, the applied half of the book, and its centerpiece is the exact, step-by-step way to clean and shine each of the five dining-room zones gently, with the exact inputs to gather. Readers arriving here are facing a room that has quietly become an office, a mailroom, and a dumping ground, its good things dusty and at risk, and they do not just want to tidy it, they want to clean it properly and know precisely how, matching the touch to the finish so nothing valuable is harmed. The chapter answers with one freeing idea and one method plus one governing principle. The idea: you do not clean the dining room, you clean one zone of it, then the next, and the dining room is only five small zones (the dining table, the sideboard surface, the sideboard storage, the china or display cabinet, and the beverage or coffee station). The method: the universal nine-rule Shine method carried from Chapter 31 (work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix). The governing principle: this is a room of good finishes and things that matter, not grease or dust, so match the touch to the finish, lift and read the marks beneath every object, damp then dry on wood so water never sits, and hand-wash the good china. Then, zone by zone, it gives the exact INPUTS (a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a streak-free glass cleaner, colour-coded microfiber, a detail brush, a compact vacuum with upholstery and floor tools, a plastic card for wax, silver polish, dish soap and a basin for the china, a descaler for the drinks station) and the NUMBERED steps in the true order of work, plus an inspect-and-flag list. It names the room's hard calls (the person who works at the table, the clear run you actually need, the tablecloth for a table you do not own, the china settings that carry memory, the second coffee machine), runs the dining room's hazards through the five safety questions (the tall china cabinet is the biggest tip-over risk in the house), and closes with the before-and-after pair, the before kept and the after kept as the standard. The friction meter is retired, replaced by each room's own transformation.
