### Slide 2 · This chapter, like the three before it, is about the clean

- Step out of the two rooms that feed the house and into the one it gathers in. The dining room is different from the kitchen and the pantry in a way that changes how you clean it. Those are working rooms, and you clean them hard: you degrease, you scrub, you soak. The dining room is a room of good finishes and things that matter, a wood table that marks if you look at it wrong, a glass cabinet full of the good china, silver that tarnishes, linen that mildews. Here you do not scrub. You care for the room, and the touch is gentle.
- That is the shift this chapter turns on, and it is worth naming plainly, because the instinct you built in the kitchen will harm things here: a wet cloth left on a wooden tabletop leaves a ring you will not lift next year, a harsh spray strips a finish, the dishwasher ruins a gilt rim. You already have the method from the door, the nine rules of Shine; the dining room adds one governing principle and two habits that follow from it, said up front.
- The book lines, verbatim: "The dining room is the room the house gathers in, and it is different from the two rooms before it in a way that changes how you clean it." "So the governing rule of the dining room is the opposite of the kitchen's: match the touch to the surface, keep it gentle, and above all keep water off wood."

### Slide 5 · The quick passes, then slow down for Shine

- Before the deep clean, four of the other five passes are quick, so we move through them briskly and then slow right down for Shine, which is where the real work is. Sort each zone before you clean it: clear the table to bare wood and give the homework and the laptop a home of their own, measure the tablecloths against your actual table, keep the china settings that fit the largest meal you really host. Straighten the survivors by use: a taped serving run on the sideboard, linens rolled with their napkins, the good china stacked no more than eight high with felt between, the drinks station left to right in use-order. Those two passes clear the ground so the clean can happen.
- Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short. Safety acts on what the clean turned up, and this room has one big item: the tall china cabinet is the largest tip-over risk in the house, so it gets strapped to a wall stud with the weight on the bottom shelf. Standardize is the dining room's friend, because this is a room a good standard runs almost by itself: a count card inside each cabinet door, a taped bare run on the sideboard, the ideal-state photo taped inside the china cabinet. And Sustain hangs it on what already happens: when the plates go to the sink after dinner, everything else on the table travels with them; when the mail comes through the door, it goes straight past the sideboard to the chair where you actually open it. That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

### Slide 7 · The dining room Shine method and the kit

- The principle governs every zone below: gentle, matched to the surface, and dry on wood, every single time. Two habits follow from it. The first is to lift everything and clean the surface itself, because in this room the surface is telling you things: a pale ring a cold glass left, a white heat bloom from a hot dish, a fresh pinhole with a little powder beneath, all only show on the bare wood underneath. The second protects the wood and is the single most important technique in the room: damp then dry, in one motion, because the ring you leave tonight is the ring you cannot lift next year. The good news is the kit is gentle and small, no degreaser for the room itself and nothing harsh, just a neutral cleaner, a glass cleaner, soft cloths, a detail brush, and a few care items for the good things.
- The kit, by type. Cleaners: a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a streak-free glass cleaner, and a descaler for the drinks station. Cloths: a colour-coded microfiber set. Tools: a small detail brush set, a compact vacuum with upholstery and floor tools. Care items for the good things: a plastic card for lifting wax, silver polish, and mild dish soap with a washing basin for the china. And the standardize-and-safety inputs: drawer dividers, a count card, and an anti-tip strap. Types, not brands. Buy for the job.
- The lift-and-read reframe, verbatim: "Clean a dining room to read it. Lift every candlestick, vase, and photo frame rather than dusting around it, because the marks that tell the room's story, a pale ring a cold glass left, a white heat bloom from a hot dish, a fresh pinhole with a little powder beneath, only show on the bare wood underneath. The dust you can see is the least of what a proper clean finds." The gentle-water reframe, verbatim: "Water is the quiet enemy of every wooden surface in this room, and it does its damage slowly and out of sight: under a glass, along a leaf seam, beneath a drip tray. So the rule on wood is damp then dry, in one motion: a barely damp cloth to lift the mark, a dry one straight behind it, and never a wet surface left to sit, because the ring you leave tonight is the ring you cannot lift next year."

### Slide 8 · Zone by zone: the dining table, and the sideboard surface

- Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Gather the inputs into your caddy first, and keep the two rules above every wooden surface: gentle, and dry.
- Zone 1, the dining table, where everyone sits for a shared meal and crumbs and spills hide in the seams and joints, so you clean the whole piece top to bottom and back to front, not just the open top where the plates sat, and it is the biggest visible win. Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber cloth set of a surface cloth and a dry cloth, a compact vacuum with the upholstery and floor tools, the caddy. Steps: clear the table to bare wood and wipe the whole surface with a cloth just damp with cleaner, working the full length in one direction, then dry it at once so no moisture sits on the finish, lifting the centerpiece to get the ring beneath; run a fingernail wrapped in a thin cloth down the leaf seam or extension joint, opening an extension table a hand's width to clean the exposed slides; wipe the underside lip and the apron rail where hands grip, a dry cloth first for cobwebs then a barely damp one; dust the pedestal, legs, and casters down to the feet; wipe the chair seats and back rails, drying a wood seat at once and vacuuming a fabric one; clean the chair rungs and stretchers into the joints; then push the chairs out and vacuum the whole footprint under the table, last.
- Zone 2, the sideboard surface, which holds serving dishes at a meal and a small display between them, where wax, cold-glass rings, and the dust line along the wall are the three things a quick pass leaves behind. Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, streak-free glass and mirror cleaner for the picture frames, a colour-coded set of a surface, glass, and dry cloth, a small detail brush set, a plastic card for lifting wax, a compact vacuum with the crevice tool, the caddy. Steps: lift each vase and candlestick off rather than dusting around it and use the detail brush into the fluting and the neck; do the picture frames and glass with cleaner sprayed onto the cloth, never the frame, so nothing runs behind the glass; clean the cleared wood damp then dry, work any wax off with the edge of the plastic card, and treat the pale rings a cold glass left; vacuum the back edge and the wall seam and run the detail brush behind the frame stands; clean the lamp, shade, and cord, handling the whole length of the cord; wipe the drawer fronts and pulls.

### Slide 9 · Zone by zone: the sideboard storage, and the china cabinet

- Zone 3, the sideboard storage, where the meal-only things live, linens, serving bowls, platters, tapers, cleaned empty and with your nose as much as your cloth, because a musty linen or a spreading tarnish is only catchable while everything is out at once. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the crevice tool, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set of a surface cloth and a dry cloth, silver polish for the silver-plate serving pieces, the caddy. Steps: take each shelf empty, vacuum the corners, then wipe the boards damp and dry them before anything returns; clean the drawer interiors and dividers and dry them fully, because a damp drawer is what a rolled tablecloth soaks up; wipe both drawer runners and slide it a few times to check it runs flush; air each tablecloth and napkin and smell it as you fold it, because a musty note means it went away damp and wants rewashing; wipe the bowls and platters, getting the rims and the feet; dust the silver-plate and look at all of it together for tarnish while it is out, working polish into a dulling piece; wipe the door interior and the count card.
- Zone 4, the china or display cabinet, which keeps the good tableware safe, visible, and usable, and gets the most detail alongside the table, because the china stays usable only if the cabinet holding it is sound, so you wash by hand, clean the glass inside and out, and read the cabinet itself while the shelves are bare. Inputs: streak-free glass and mirror cleaner, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set of a surface, glass, and dry cloth, a small detail brush set, mild dish soap and a plastic washing basin with a towel in the bottom, a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, the caddy. Steps: dust the very top of the case first, high and out of sight where the dust settles thickest, dry cloth then barely damp then dry the wood; do the glass doors inside and out, spraying cleaner onto the cloth and running the detail brush along the glazing bars; wipe the interior glass shelves and liners, the edges and the support pins; hand-wash each piece of china in the basin, never the dishwasher for a gilt rim or hand painting, drying it straight away and setting it back rim up on a clean liner; clean the frame, hinges, lock, and the strap point where the case meets the wall, the detail brush on the hinge knuckles; do the lower shelf and the tureens, under the foot of each.

### Slide 10 · Zone by zone: the beverage or coffee station

- Zone 5, the beverage or coffee station, which makes a drink without crossing into the kitchen, and the one zone that earns a firmer touch, because it runs on water and heat, so you descale it, clear the sticky rings, and clean the hidden underside of the drip tray where mold starts. Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a food-area degreaser for the coffee splatter on the tray, a colour-coded set of a surface cloth and a dry cloth, a descaling solution or white vinegar for the machine and kettle, the caddy.

### Slide 11 · Clean to inspect, and flag; the safety pass fixes

- Cleaning is how you inspect. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the thing you never normally see, a white heat bloom on the wood, a loose apron screw, a frayed wall strap on the cabinet, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. ### Slide 12 · The calls that matter here

- Cleaning to a method makes the dining room lovely, but a few genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays a dining room, and the hardest ones here are about things that carry memory. The first, and the one that undoes every reset, is the person who works at the table: if an activity lands on the table more than three evenings a week, it is not clutter, it is an activity with nowhere else to be, and the fix is a home of its own for it, off the table. The second is the clear run you actually need on the sideboard: set down the largest dish you genuinely serve from there, tape a bare run that fits it, and keep three display objects at the far end and nothing else on the wood.
- The rest are about the good things. The tablecloth for a table you do not own goes if it will not hang at least a hand's depth over your actual table edge, because a cloth that does not fit never gets used. The china settings you keep are the number that fits the largest meal you have really hosted in three years, plus two for breakage, and for the rest you write down whose they were and what you remember and photograph them before they go to someone who will use them. And the second coffee machine earns its place only if it makes drinks people actually drink, so mark it for a fortnight rather than deciding from a generous memory.
- The table-as-desk reframe, frozen: "If an activity lands on the table more than three evenings in a week, it is not clutter, it is an activity with nowhere else to be, and no table standard will ever survive it. The fix is not another polite reset, it is a home of its own for that activity, off the table, so the surface is free to do the one job a dining table is for." Then everyone agrees: whatever arrives on the table for a session leaves with it."
