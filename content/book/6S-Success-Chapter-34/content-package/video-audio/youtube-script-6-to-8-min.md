## SECTION 2: THE DINING-ROOM SHINE METHOD

[HOST]
You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one still holds. The dining room simply adds one governing principle and two habits that follow from it.

[VISUAL: A sideboard with the order of care as a numbered path: match the touch to the finish, a hand choosing a soft cloth over a harsh spray with a "gentle, never harsh on wood, veneer, gilt, or glass" note; then lift and read, a candlestick lifted to reveal a pale ring beneath it; then damp then dry, a barely-damp cloth followed immediately by a dry one with a "never leave water on wood" warning; then wash the good china by hand, a plate in a towel-lined basin. Small icons for "read the marks" and "the clean is an inspection". A foot line reads "match the touch to the finish, lift and read, damp then dry, wash the good china by hand".]

[HOST, voice over]
Here is the principle, frozen. A dining room is a room of good finishes and things that matter, so here you match the touch to the surface: a cloth only just damp on sealed wood, dried at once so no water sits on the finish, the good china washed by hand and never in the machine, and nothing harsh on veneer, gilt, or glass. You are not scrubbing a kitchen, you are caring for a room. That governs every zone: gentle first, the right cloth and cleaner for the finish in front of you, every single time.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Match the touch to the finish. Keep it gentle. Keep water off wood.]

[HOST, on camera]
And here are the two habits. The first: lift every candlestick, vase, and photo frame rather than dusting around it, because the marks that tell the room's story, a pale ring a cold glass left, a white heat bloom from a hot dish, a fresh pinhole with a little powder beneath, only show on the bare wood underneath. The dust you can see is the least of what a proper clean finds. The second, and the most important technique in the room: on wood, damp then dry, in one motion, a barely damp cloth to lift the mark, a dry one straight behind it, and never a wet surface left to sit, because the ring you leave tonight is the ring you cannot lift next year. And the good china is washed by hand, never the machine. Shine finds the hazards; the safety pass fixes them.

## SECTION 3: ZONE BY ZONE, EXACTLY HOW TO CLEAN IT

[HOST]
So let me show you the method cleaning the showcase zone, the dining table, and then take you fast through the rest.

[VISUAL: The dining table cleaned in numbered sequence, 1 to 7. A caddy set down first with its inputs as labelled icons: a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber set, a compact vacuum with the upholstery and floor tools. Then the table cleared to bare wood, the surface wiped damp then dried at once, the centerpiece lifted for the ring, a cloth over a fingernail run down the leaf seam, the apron rail wiped, the casters dusted, the rungs worked into the joints, the footprint vacuumed.]

[HOST, voice over]
Gather the inputs into a caddy first, and hold the two rules above every wooden surface: gentle, and dry. Then, in order. One, clear the table to bare wood and do the top, wipe damp in one direction, then a dry cloth at once so no moisture sits, and lift the centerpiece for the ring beneath. Two, clean the leaf seam, run a fingernail wrapped in a thin cloth down the gap where the leaves meet. Three, do the underside lip and the apron rail where hands grip, a dry cloth first for cobwebs, then a barely damp one. Four, dust the pedestal, legs, and casters. Five, do the chair seats and back rails, drying a wood seat at once and vacuuming a fabric seat along the front edge. Six, clean the rungs and stretchers into the joints. Seven, the floor footprint last, chairs pushed out.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Wipe damp, dry at once. Lift the centerpiece. Footprint last.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the other four run the same way. The sideboard surface, where you lift every object and clean the wood itself, spray the frame glass onto the cloth not the frame, and lift the wax off with the edge of a plastic card. The sideboard storage, where you clean it empty and use your nose, airing and smelling each linen because a musty note means rewash not restack, and checking the silver-plate for tarnish while every piece is out. The china cabinet, where you dust the top first, clean both faces of the glass, and hand-wash the good china in a towel-lined basin, never the machine for a gilt rim or hand painting. And the coffee station, the one working corner, where you clean the shelf underside above the kettle where steam warps it, lift the drip-tray grate to wash the hidden well where mold starts, and descale the machine and kettle on the interval.

## SECTION 4: THE CALLS, AND THE HAZARDS YOU FLAGGED

[HOST]
Cleaning to a method makes the dining room lovely, but a few honest decisions decide whether it stays a dining room, and while you cleaned, you were flagging hazards to act on now.

[VISUAL: Three quick beats, the activity given a home of its own off the table, the serving run measured and taped to the largest dish, the good china settings kept to the largest meal really hosted and the rest photographed. Then the safety pass acting on what the clean flagged: the tall china cabinet strapped to a wall stud, a frayed cord retired, a worn extension latch replaced.]

[HOST, voice over]
The person who works at the table is the one that undoes every reset, so here is the frozen reframe. If an activity lands on the table more than three evenings in a week, it is not clutter, it is an activity with nowhere else to be, and no table standard will ever survive it. The fix is not another polite reset, it is a home of its own for that activity, off the table, so the surface is free to do the one job a dining table is for. Then the serving run, measured to your largest dish and taped, with three display objects at the far end. And the good things: keep the china settings that fit the largest meal you have really hosted, plus two for breakage, and photograph the rest before they go to someone who will use them.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Give the table back its one job.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the dining room's hazards are quieter than the kitchen's, but two are serious, so run the five safety questions once directly. Burn or fire: keep candles clear of a table runner and never leave them lit unattended, and do not chain a lamp, a charger, and a warming tray to one sideboard outlet. Fall, cut, or crush: treat the tall glass-fronted china cabinet as the biggest tip-over risk in the house and strap it to a wall stud with the weight on the bottom shelf, and replace a worn drop-leaf or extension latch that could fold on a hand. Water and electricity: keep the coffee machine, the kettle, and any open jug back from each other with cords clear of the front edge. And poison, choke, or strangle: keep decorative or hand-painted pieces of unknown origin, which can carry lead in the glaze, out of actual food use. Shine found them; the safety pass fixes them.
