# Chapter 45 Podcast Script: The Garage

*Eight to twelve minutes. Conversational, unhurried.*

---

I want to start with a distinction that changed how I think about this room entirely.

Every other room in this book fails from use. Your kitchen gets dirty because you cook in it. The bathroom gets dirty because of water. The laundry room fails because you do laundry.

The garage does not fail from use. It fails from arrival.

Think about how things actually get into a garage. They come in from every other room in the house. Somebody sets them down. And because the door stays shut most of the week, nothing ever comes along to force a verdict on any of them.

That is how you end up with your best chisel and a box you have not opened since you moved in, sitting on the same shelf, treated exactly alike. The room does not distinguish. Nothing in it has been ranked, because nothing in it has been decided.

Which means the sorting question here is unusual, and it is not the one you would expect. It is not "do I still want this". It is "was this ever actually stored, or was it only postponed".

And once you ask it that way, a lot of the room answers immediately. The chair with the wobbly leg. The charity sack that has stood by the side door since spring. The printer you replaced. None of those is in storage. Each one is a decision somebody put down and walked away from, and the garage has been quietly absorbing the cost.

So here is the rule that keeps it clear once you have cleared it, and it is a one-day rule. Anything carried in with no rail, no shelf, no hook, no bin already waiting for it gets its verdict the same day. Not next weekend, because next weekend is how it got here. Otherwise it quietly becomes part of the floor.

Now, the practical order of work, because this room has one and it matters at both ends.

Start at the workbench. Not the pile inside the door, which is what everybody does. The bench, because until one solid surface is clear you have nowhere to lay anything out while you judge it, and everything in this garage is going to get laid out at some point.

And finish on the floor. That sounds backwards, and it is the single most useful piece of sequencing in the chapter. Everything in a garage wants to end up on the slab. So until the walls and the racks have taken what belongs on them, there is no floor to clean. There is only a pile to work around.

Then, when the floor is genuinely clear, you degrease it properly. Once. With degreaser, a stiff brush, and absorbent left on the old stains overnight. After that, a sweep holds it.

And here is what you actually bought with that afternoon, because it is not a cosmetic result.

A pale floor turns every new drip into information.

On a dark, stained slab, a fresh oil spot is invisible. It blends into forty other spots. On a pale one, it is obvious the day it appears. Which means you see the gearbox weeping, or the water heater starting to go, the week it starts rather than the month it fails. The floor becomes an instrument. It is the only room in this book that ends up actively working for you rather than simply staying tidy.

Two more things, and both are about danger, because this is the heaviest room in the house and the one with the most genuinely dangerous chemistry in it.

The first is weight and height. This is the only room where the ordinary hazards are heavy and stored above somebody's head. An overhead rack above the roof of a parked car. A bike on a hook. A rack bolt that went into plasterboard instead of a joist. So the load rules are simple: heavy bins on the lowest rack, light bulky things highest, bolts into joists, nothing overhead you cannot bring down with both hands on a stable ladder, and nothing overhanging the bay where a person opens a car door.

The second is the chemistry, and the key idea is that almost none of it is dangerous by itself. It becomes dangerous by proximity. Petrol is fine in a proper container; it is not fine near the water heater's pilot flame, because the vapour pools low. Fertiliser is fine; it is an oxidiser, so it must never share a shelf with fuel. Lithium packs are fine; they should not sleep on a charger, and they should not sit beside the fuel can.

Two facts inside that which people genuinely do not know, and I want to say both plainly.

Rags soaked with oil, stain, or solvent, balled up in an open bin, can heat on their own as the oil cures. No spark. No flame anywhere near them. That is why they go in a metal can with a lid.

And antifreeze tastes sweet. That is not a figure of speech. A dog will drink a puddle of it, and a small child will drink from a decanted bottle. Which is why nothing in this room is ever poured into a container that does not carry its own label, and why a spill on the slab gets cleaned rather than left to dry.

There are seven hard calls in the chapter, one per zone, and they run from the project that has been on the bench since spring to the box overhead you have never opened. They are all versions of the same question: is this stored, or is it postponed.

Do one zone this week, and make it the bench.
