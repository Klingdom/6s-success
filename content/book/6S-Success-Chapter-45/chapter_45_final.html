Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks

Chapter Forty-Five

The Garage

The garage takes what every other room in the house evicts, and because the door stays shut most of the week nothing ever forces a verdict on any of it. This playbook gives you, for each of the seven zones, the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, so everything that stays gets a rail, a hook, an outline, or a dated bin, the genuinely dangerous chemistry ends up where it belongs, and the slab comes clean enough to read.

The home office was a room full of your own postponed decisions. The garage is the room full of everybody else's.

That is the thing to understand before you start, because it changes who has to be standing there when you do it. Every other room in this book fails from use: the kitchen from cooking, the laundry room from washing, the bathroom from water. The garage does not fail from use. It fails from arrival. Things come in from every other room in the house, they are set down, and because the door stays shut most of the week nothing ever comes along to force a verdict on any of them. That is how your best chisel and a box you have not opened since you moved in end up on the same shelf, treated exactly alike.

So the sorting question here is unusual, and it is not "do I still want this". It is "was this ever actually stored, or was it only postponed". The chair with the wobbly leg. The charity sack that has stood by the side door since spring. The printer you replaced. None of those is in storage. Each one is a decision somebody put down and walked away from, and the garage has been quietly absorbing the cost.

Two other things make this room specific, and both raise the stakes above anything in Part Nine so far.

The first is weight and height. This is the only room where the ordinary hazards are heavy and stored above someone's head: an overhead rack over the roof of a parked car, a bike on a hook, a rack bolt that went into plasterboard instead of a joist. The second is that this room holds the household's genuinely dangerous chemistry. Petrol, antifreeze, weed killer, oxidising feed, oily rags, lithium packs. None of it is dangerous by itself. All of it becomes dangerous by proximity, which is a storage problem, which means it is a 6S problem.

And there is one reward here that no other room offers. Get everything up off the slab, degrease it properly once, and you own a pale floor. A pale floor is not a cosmetic result. It is an instrument, and it starts reporting on your car and your house the week something goes wrong rather than the month it fails.

What You Will Learn

  • How to break the garage into seven small zones you can clean one at a time, starting at the workbench and finishing on the slab.
  • The difference between what is genuinely stored in here and what is only being postponed, and the one-day rule that stops the second kind arriving.
  • The exact inputs and the numbered steps to clean and shine each of the seven zones, from the bench dog holes to the roof deck above the overhead rack.
  • Why the floor is cleaned last and degreased properly once, and what a pale slab then tells you about your car and your house.
  • How to handle the seven calls this room forces, and how to store the heaviest, most dangerous things in the house so that proximity stops being the problem.

The principleSeven Zones, Not One Room

You do not clean a garage. You clear the bench, then the tool wall, then the power tools, then the car shelf, then the sports gear, then the garden rail, then what is overhead. Then, and only then, the floor. Each is a job with a clear finish, and the order matters at both ends: the bench first because it is the only surface you can lay anything out on, and the slab last because until the walls and racks have taken what belongs on them there is no floor to clean, only a pile to work around.

The garage is seven small zones, not one big job: the primary workbench, the hand tool wall and cabinets, the power tool and battery zone, the automotive care zone, the sports and recreation zone, the lawn and garden tool zone, and the bulk and overhead storage. Do them one at a time, and start at the primary workbench, because until one solid surface is clear, everything you pick up just goes back down somewhere else. Leave the slab until last, once the walls and the racks have taken what belongs on them.

This is the longest room in the book, and it is the one most worth spreading over several sessions. Each zone is an hour or so. Nothing is lost by doing one a weekend, provided the bench stays clear between them.

A finished garage

A finished garage is one you can walk into and work in without moving anything first: a bare bench holding only the vise and the task light, every hand tool hanging on its own drawn outline with no blank silhouettes, one shelf per battery system with nothing sleeping on a charger, fluids upright and dated on a clean tray, bikes on wall hooks with bare floor beneath them, long handles head-up on a rail and the garden chemicals latched above head height, overhead bins labelled to be read from the slab, and swept concrete you can see the colour of.

The quick passesThe Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

Sort here asks a question no other room asks: not whether you want the thing, but whether it was ever really stored, and every duplicate gets counted honestly, because most garages hold four spades and dig with one. Straighten is governed by a single sentence, which is that nothing lives on the slab and nothing leans: handles hang head-up, tools hang on outlines, bikes hang on rated hooks, and the floor stays clear. Safety is the reason this chapter is long, and it runs through every zone rather than waiting at the end. Standardize is unusually visual in this room, because a drawn outline, a taped strip, and a large label readable from the slab all report the room's state to anyone standing in the doorway. And Sustain attaches to things you already do: the plug leaving the bench outlet, the bonnet already up for the oil check, the mower cooling after a cut.

The methodHow to Shine the Garage: The Method

The nine rules of Shine hold here exactly as they have all through Part Nine, and two of them do most of the work: top to bottom, because in a room this tall the dust you disturb overhead lands on everything you have already cleaned, and clean to inspect and flag, because this is the room where what you find matters more than what you wipe.

On top of them, the garage adds a layer of four.

The garage takes what every other room in the house evicts, and because the door stays shut most of the week nothing ever forces a verdict on any of it. That is why your best chisel and a box you have not opened since you moved in can sit on the same shelf. So understand what is actually in here: the chair with the wobbly leg, the charity sack that has stood by the side door since spring, the printer you replaced. None of it is being stored. It is being postponed. Anything carried in with no rail, shelf, or bin already waiting for it gets its verdict the same day, or it quietly becomes part of the floor.

The second part is the one that decides the order of work, and it pays you back for years.

Everything in this room wants to end up on the slab, which is why the slab is cleaned last: only once the walls and the racks have taken what belongs on them is there a floor to clean rather than a pile to work around. Then degrease it properly, once, with degreaser, a stiff brush, and absorbent left on the old stains overnight. After that a sweep holds it. And here is what you actually bought with that afternoon: a pale floor turns every new drip into information. You will see the gearbox weeping or the water heater starting to go the week it starts, not the month it fails.

The third part is the one that separates this room from every other one in the book.

Every other room's hazards were small. This room's are heavy, and they are all above somebody. So the load rules are simple and they are not negotiable. Heavy bins go on the lowest rack and light bulky things go highest. Rack bolts land in joists, never in plasterboard alone. Nothing goes overhead that you cannot bring down with both hands on a stable ladder, and nothing you might need in the dark or in a hurry. Nothing overhangs the bay where people open a car door. And a bike hook is rated and driven into a stud, or it is not a bike hook.

And the fourth is the one to read twice.

This is the room where the household's genuinely dangerous chemistry lives, and none of it is dangerous by itself. It becomes dangerous by proximity. Petrol stays out of the house envelope and away from the water heater's pilot flame, because vapour pools low. Fertiliser is an oxidiser and never shares a shelf with fuel. Rags soaked with oil, stain, or solvent go into a metal can with a lid, because they can heat on their own with no flame anywhere near them. Lithium packs never sleep on a charger, and never sit beside the fuel can. And nothing, ever, is decanted into an unlabelled bottle.

Quick Win

Clear the workbench top down to bare wood before you touch anything else. Everything else in this garage is going to get laid out on it while you judge it, and it is the one part of the room that pays you back the same afternoon. It also gives you the honest test for every other zone: if a thing has no rail, hook, shelf, or bin waiting for it, it does not go back up.

6S Tip

Degrease the slab properly once, with degreaser, a stiff brush, and absorbent granules left on the old stains overnight. After that a sweep with the push broom holds it. What you are really buying is a pale floor, because a pale floor turns every new drip into information: you see the gearbox weeping the week it starts, not the month it fails.

The centrepieceZone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the heart of the chapter. For each of the seven zones you get the inputs to gather before you start and the exact order of steps to clean and shine it. Work top to bottom within every zone, and leave the slab for the end of the last session. Every zone finishes the same way, with a look for the things to flag for the Safety pass.

Zone 1 · The Primary Workbench

This is the one surface in the house where things get fixed, glued, cut, and assembled, and it is the surface every other zone in this garage will be laid out on. Clean it top to bottom, back wall to slab, and finish by oiling the vise so the bench is genuinely ready to work rather than merely tidy.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and light machine oil.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
  • A compact vacuum with the crevice and brush tools, and your caddy.
  • A push broom, and a metal can with a lid for oily rags.

Steps

  1. Clear the top down to bare wood, giving every jar-lid of screws, plugged glue bottle, and saved offcut a verdict before anything is allowed back up.
  2. Start high and at the back: run the vacuum brush along the top edge of the pegboard and down its face to pull off the sawdust film before it drifts onto the clean top, then wipe the wall above with a barely damp cloth.
  3. Dust the task light shade and arm with a dry cloth, then draw a just-damp cloth down its cord to lift the greasy grit, keeping the cloth dry around the fitting itself.
  4. Wipe the bench outlet faceplate and the power strip housing with a dry cloth only, and work a dry brush around the socket surrounds where sawdust packs in, keeping every drop of moisture away from the sockets.
  5. Vacuum the whole bench top, working the crevice tool hard along the back seam where filings pack in, then wipe the sealed top with the neutral pH cleaner following the grain and buff it dry so nothing is left to rust a tool set down on it.
  6. Push the crevice nozzle into each bench dog hole to draw out sawdust and filings, or run the detail brush round the rim, since a packed hole stops a dog seating flush.
  7. Wipe the vise jaws and body clean of grit, wind it fully open and closed, then lay a drop of light machine oil along the screw and slide and work it back and forth to carry the oil in.
  8. Wipe each clamp bar as it goes back on the rail, then tip the job tray out, clear its corners, and wipe it before the parts return.
  9. Pull the bench drawer right out, tip the grit from its back corners, and wipe both runners.
  10. Sweep and vacuum the slab under the bench until bare concrete shows, and put any oil, stain, or solvent rags into the closed metal can rather than an open bin.

Inspect and flag as you go: rust blooming on the vise screw, the bench dogs, or any bare steel on the top, which is the first sign the bench is holding damp; a hairline crack or a soft spongy patch in the top or a leg joint, and any wobble when you lean your weight on it; a white salt bloom or a spreading damp patch on the slab beneath, which says water is tracking in under the wall; and scorching, browning, or a warm smell at the bench outlet or along the light cord as your hand passes over it. Flag each.

Zone 2 · The Hand Tool Wall and Cabinets

This keeps the tools you reach for weekly visible, findable, and provably all present. Every tool comes off the wall, gets an oiled wipe, and goes back on its own drawn outline. Rust starts at the handle end where your hands leave salt, so that is exactly where you look as you clean.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and light machine oil with a rag.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
  • A compact vacuum with the brush tool, and your caddy.
  • A portable label maker or marker for the outlines, and blade guards or sheaths.

Steps

  1. Reach the top of the wall cabinet first with a dry cloth or the vacuum brush, because the dust settled up there drops onto everything below the moment you disturb it.
  2. Lift each tool off the wall, then vacuum the pegboard face and its holes.
  3. Wipe the pegboard with a barely damp cloth and let it dry fully before anything goes back.
  4. Wipe each pegboard hook and reseat it firmly in its holes, clearing any grit from the hole that would stop it sitting tight.
  5. Wipe every tool down with an oiled rag as it returns to its outline, working the handle end where skin salt sits, and sheath the saw teeth and chisel edges before they touch the wall.
  6. Clean the cabinet door faces and handle with the neutral pH cleaner, and work the detail brush into the latch and hinge seams where oily dust builds up.
  7. Empty each cabinet shelf, vacuum the corners, wipe the boards, and dry them before the tools go back sorted.
  8. Vacuum along the skirting under the cabinet where filings and small offcuts gather out of sight.
  9. Set the wall back deliberately: the tape measure, utility knife, and number two driver at chest height nearest the door, heavier tools lower and further along, and every silhouette traced so a blank outline is a question.

Inspect and flag as you go: rust starting at the handle end of chisels, pliers, and wrenches before it creeps up the blade; a pegboard hook that has worked loose in its holes, or a hole worn wide enough that the hook rocks; a soft, swollen, or stained patch on the pegboard, which means damp is coming through the wall behind it; and a cabinet latch that no longer catches on the first push, along with any droppings or gnaw marks behind the cabinet. Flag each.

Zone 3 · The Power Tool and Battery Zone

This holds the powered tools, their packs, and their chargers so a tool is always ready to run and nothing is left charging unattended. Dust is what turns a warm tool into a hot one, so the motor vents come first. Work back to front along each shelf, clearing vents and contacts before you touch the slab underneath.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and electrical contact cleaner.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a soft brush or compressed air.
  • A compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy.
  • A shelf location label set, one label per battery system.

Steps

  1. Unplug every corded tool and take the battery out of every cordless one before you touch a single tool in this zone. Nothing here gets cleaned while it can still start. Set the packs aside on the shelf, not in the tools.
  2. Wipe each charger case at the shelf front edge, then clear the terminal slots with a dry brush or a puff of air, never a damp cloth, so nothing conductive is left in the contacts.
  3. Lift the packs and cases off, vacuum the shelf board, wipe it with the neutral pH cleaner, and dry it fully before anything sits back down.
  4. With each tool still unplugged or its battery still out, brush or blow the motor vents of each drill, driver, and saw clear of packed dust, working from the intake side through, because a vent left clogged runs the motor hot enough to shorten its life.
  5. Wipe down each tool housing and grip once its vents are clear.
  6. With the tool unplugged or its battery out, hold each retracting blade guard open, wipe the blade and the inside of the guard clean of resin and dust, then let it spring back and clean the outside, confirming it springs back freely on its own. Wipe along the blade, never across the teeth, and keep your fingers behind the cutting edge the whole time.
  7. Wipe each battery pack and clean its contact rails with a dry brush or a touch of electrical contact cleaner, lifting grit or corrosion from between the terminals.
  8. Tip out the shallow bits, blades, and discs drawer underneath, vacuum the filings from its corners and runner track, and wipe it before the contents go back sorted.
  9. Sweep and vacuum the slab under the shelf, especially the strip where meltwater runs off a car onto the concrete.
  10. Set the zone back by battery system, one shelf each with its charger mounted at the front edge, no pack left sitting on a charger, and the whole shelf well clear of the fuel can and the rag bin. Batteries go back into tools only when the zone is finished and dry.

Inspect and flag as you go: a battery pack that feels warm, looks swollen, or has a split case, spotted while you wipe it; green or white corrosion crusting on pack contacts or charger terminals; a cracked or heat-discoloured tool housing, or a charger case browned around its own vents; and damp or rust spreading on the slab under a charger base, where water off the car reaches the power. Flag each.

Zone 4 · The Automotive Care Zone

This keeps the fluids, cables, and cleaning kit for the cars you actually own upright, labelled, and separate from everything the household touches. Wipe every bottle shoulder and read the drip tray as you go, because the tray tells you what is seeping before the floor does.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and oil-absorbent granules.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and nitrile gloves.
  • A compact vacuum with attachments, a push broom, and your caddy.
  • A shallow drip tray, a metal can with a lid for rags, and a permanent marker for dates.

Steps

  1. Lift each bottle, wipe its shoulder and cap where oil and coolant weep down, and stand it back squarely on the tray with its dated shoulder facing out.
  2. With the bottles and tray lifted off, wipe the shelf board itself with the neutral pH cleaner, lifting the greasy film and any fluid that has crept under the tray unseen.
  3. Slide the drip tray out, wipe it clean, and read what is on it as you go, since a fresh ring shows exactly which bottle has started to seep.
  4. Wipe the jumper cable clamps, coil the leads back into their bag, and wipe the tyre gauge before it goes in beside them, keeping that bag right by the door.
  5. Wipe the outside of the metal rag can and check its lid closes flat, leaving the oily rags sealed inside rather than loose on a shelf.
  6. Wash the wash mitts and microfibre drying towels on their own load, never with house laundry, so old wax and grit stay out of clean fabric, and dry them fully before they go back.
  7. Sweep the parking bay, then work oil-absorbent granules into any fresh spots on the concrete and leave them to draw the oil up.
  8. Sweep the granules off once they have worked, and wipe the area, keeping the used granules out of any drain.
  9. Write the month you opened it on every bottle from today onward, and confirm each one matches a vehicle parked at this address.

Inspect and flag as you go: a fresh ring on the drip tray under one bottle, the sign it has started to seep; a sweet-smelling puddle anywhere on the slab, which is leaking antifreeze and deadly to a pet or a child; an oil stain spreading on the concrete under where the car sits, telling you the car itself is dropping fluid; and a bottle whose printed label has washed or faded illegible, along with any rust weeping from the base of the rag can. Flag each.

Zone 5 · The Sports and Recreation Zone

This holds the gear for the sports this household currently plays, with the in-season kit easiest to grab. Air out the liners, hose the mud off the cleats, and never pack a single thing away damp. Work down from the bikes on the wall to the bare floor beneath them.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a mild gear disinfectant.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
  • A compact vacuum with attachments, and your caddy.
  • A garden hose for the cleats, baking soda for the cooler, and a sports ball bin.

Steps

  1. Wipe the bike frames down and lift the chain grit off with a rag, working top to bottom, then wipe the wall hooks themselves before the wheels go back up.
  2. Clear the shelf of this season's kit, vacuum it, wipe it with the neutral pH cleaner, and set the in-season gear back at the front where it is grabbed.
  3. Take the helmet liners out where they lift, air them, and wipe the pads and shells with a cloth and a mild gear disinfectant.
  4. Let every liner and pad dry all the way through before it goes back in a bin, because anything packed away damp comes out smelling of last season.
  5. Hose the caked mud off the cleats outdoors, knock the studs clean, and dry them right through before they return to the shelf.
  6. Wash the cooler inside, dry it, then leave it propped open with a rag of baking soda inside so it does not come back smelling of last summer.
  7. Tip each labelled activity bin out, wipe the grit and sand from its corners, and refit the lid before it goes back on the shelf.
  8. Vacuum the bare floor under the hanging bikes where dried mud and grass drop off the tyres.
  9. Check as you rehang that every bike hook is rated and driven into a stud, that no wheel hangs where a car door swings into it, and that camp stove canisters and lantern fuel are not stored among these bins.

Inspect and flag as you go: a hairline crack in a helmet shell or a crushed, compressed liner, the sign that helmet is finished even if the shell looks fine; a bike wall hook working loose in its anchor, or the wood around it starting to split; rust or seized links on a bike chain, and frayed or torn straps on helmets and pads; and mildew growing in the cooler seal or in the corners of a bin that went away damp. Flag each.

Zone 6 · The Lawn and Garden Tool Zone

This stores the yard tools and outdoor supplies so long handles stay off the floor and garden chemicals stay latched away from everything else. Scrape, oil, and sharpen the blades as they come off the rail, and rinse the spreader before old feed eats it from the inside out. The chemicals stay latched and above head height the whole time you work around them.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner.
  • Light machine oil, a wire brush and scraper set, and a flat file.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and nitrile gloves.
  • A compact vacuum with attachments, a garden hose, and your caddy.

Steps

  1. Wipe the tool rail and the wall behind it high up first, then lift each spade, rake, and hoe down to clean it, so nothing rests handle-first in a corner.
  2. Scrape the caked soil off each blade with the wire brush or scraper, working over a sheet so the soil is caught rather than tracked.
  3. Wipe each blade with an oiled rag and run the flat file along the cutting edge so it bites next time, before the tool goes back head-up on the rail.
  4. Wipe the hose reel, coil the hose back onto it evenly, and rinse any dried soil out of the nozzle.
  5. Empty the spreader hopper completely, rinse it out with the hose, and dry it, because leftover fertiliser eats the metal from the inside over a winter.
  6. Wipe the string trimmer and hedge shear cutting heads down, clear the grass from their guards, and hang them with the cutting ends pointing away from the aisle you walk.
  7. Wipe the chemical shelf front and the outside of each container while the shelf stays latched and above head height, reading every label as you go and decanting nothing.
  8. Sweep the aisle, then mop it with the floor-material-compatible cleaner so soil and spilled feed do not get tracked around the garage.
  9. Check before you finish that the fertiliser is nowhere near the fuel, since feed is an oxidiser, and that every long handle hangs head-up on its taped strip with nothing leaning in a corner.

Inspect and flag as you go: rust pitting along a spade or shear edge, and a handle splitting or working loose where the head meets the shaft; fertiliser crust eating the inside of the spreader hopper into flaking metal; a garden chemical container that is weeping, crusted, or swollen on the latched shelf, flagged without decanting or handling it further; and a perished hose washer or a split fitting dripping at the reel. Flag each.

Zone 7 · The Bulk and Overhead Storage

This holds the things you genuinely need once or twice a year, high up and out of the way, without becoming the place decisions go to hide. The depth of dust on a lid tells you how long a bin has sat untouched. While you are up there, read the roof deck above the rack for the brown stain that means water is getting in.

Inputs

  • A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and an extendable duster.
  • A compact vacuum with attachments, and your caddy.
  • A stable step ladder, impact-rated safety glasses, and water-resistant permanent labels.

Steps

  1. Set the step ladder level and open fully, and bring the bins down to the floor before you judge any of them, because nobody sorts honestly from the third step with one arm through a lid.
  2. From a stable step, wipe the underside of the roof deck and the ceiling above the rack and clear the cobwebs, so a new stain would show against clean board.
  3. Wipe the rack rails and the underside of the rack with a damp cloth, working the settled dust off the steel.
  4. Once a level is cleared, wipe the plywood or wire deck the bins sit on, since grit ground under a base scratches the bin and hides damp.
  5. Dust each bin lid with the extendable duster or a cloth, noting the depth of dust as you go, since it tells you how many years since anything came out of that bin.
  6. Wipe the bin sides and the label face so the letters read from the slab, and update the last-opened month and year on any bin you have opened.
  7. Wipe down the rack uprights and clear the grit from the bolt plates where the rack meets the wall and the ceiling.
  8. Sweep and vacuum the floor under the rack, especially the strip right at the edge of the parking bay.
  9. Reload with the heavy bins on the lowest rack and the light bulky things highest, grouped by the time of year they come down, nothing overhanging the parking bay, and a map taped beside the light switch saying which bay holds what.

Inspect and flag as you go: a brown water stain spreading on the roof deck, the rack underside, or the ceiling, the sign the roof is letting water in; a bin gone soft, stained, or damp at its base, and any musty smell when you lift a lid; rust, or a bend or sag, in a rack upright or a rail carrying the load; and a rack bolt that has drawn part-way out of its hole, or that lands in plasterboard rather than a joist, along with any rodent droppings on a lid. Flag each.

The hard callsThe Calls That Matter Here

Seven decisions in this room are the whole job, one per zone, and every one of them has been waiting years.

The project that has been there since spring. Name the half-finished thing on your bench right now. It gets one of three verdicts today: a specific weekend written on it to finish it, a breakdown that returns the timber and hardware to stock, or the skip. Anything that has sat untouched through one full season is not a project any more, it is furniture with your intentions stacked on top. Do not solve it by lifting it onto a shelf; that only moves the decision somewhere darker and costs you the bench in the meantime.

The third hammer. You almost certainly own three claw hammers and four adjustable wrenches, and you have never thinned them because each one, held on its own, still works. Working is not the test. The test is which one your hand actually goes to. Keep that one, plus exactly one deliberate spare parked where a second person would need it. A hammer you keep because it still works and never choose is taking the hook that would have made the hammer you love findable in one glance from the door.

The tool whose batteries no longer exist. Somewhere on that shelf is a good tool on a dead platform. Decide it with money rather than loyalty: price one replacement pack today, and if a pack costs more than half what a current equivalent tool costs, or you cannot buy one at all, the tool is finished. Sell it with its charger while it still means something to somebody. Two battery systems is a cost you can defend. Three means a hunt through three shelves every time you want to put a hole in a wall.

The fluids for the car you no longer own. The hardest bottles are the two-thirds-full ones: real money, not gone off, and they fit nothing on your driveway. Every bottle must match a vehicle parked at this address, and no match means no keep. Offer them this week to the neighbour who drives that model, and take whatever is unclaimed to household hazardous waste, never down a drain. Then date every bottle from today, because this shelf only grew this deep because nobody could tell which ones were still good.

The gear from the sport you stopped playing. Skis, golf clubs, a good tent, the road bike. Each was expensive, each still works, and each stands for a version of your week that has not happened in years. Two full seasons of the right weather with no use means you are not on a break, you have stopped. Sell it while the bindings and the groupset are still current, or hand it to someone starting out. The one exception worth honouring is a named date: if you can finish the sentence "I will use this on ___", write that date on the bin.

The half-used chemicals. Start with the unlabelled sprayer, because it is the easy call: you cannot safely use what you cannot name, so it goes to hazardous waste this month without being opened again. For the rest, apply one season of intent. If you will not genuinely use it before the next growing season ends, you are storing a poison above your children's heads for no reason. Never pour any of it down a drain, into a gutter, or onto the ground behind the shed.

The box you have never opened. There is at least one up there that moved in with you. Bring it down and open it on the floor with three things beside you: a rubbish sack, a donation box, and one empty container. Whatever you truly want to keep has to fit inside that single container and has to have a living person's name attached to it. Do not tape it up and send it back overhead, because a box that goes back unopened has just bought itself another five years and taught the rack that this is allowed.

The Postponed Pile Challenge

Walk the garage with the household and name out loud what is genuinely being stored and what is only being postponed. The chair with the wobbly leg. The charity sack by the side door. The printer you replaced. Anything in the second group gets its verdict today, because a thing with no rail, shelf, or bin waiting for it does not get stored in a garage. It becomes part of the floor.

The hazardsWatch for in the Garage

This is the heaviest room in the house and the one with the most genuinely dangerous chemistry in it, so the Safety pass here is not a tidy-up at the end. It is most of the point.

Burn or fire is the question this room answers first, and it has four answers: oily rags, petrol vapour near a pilot, lithium packs charging unattended on wood, and fertiliser stored against fuel. Poison, choke, or strangle is the one people underrate, because antifreeze tastes sweet and a spilled puddle or a decanted unlabelled bottle will kill a dog or a curious child. Fall, cut, or crush covers everything sharp at hand height, every guard wedged open, every loaded rack, and a car held on a jack with no axle stands under the sills. Fall is the ladder rocking on a sloped slab. Water and electricity is a charger base sitting where meltwater runs off the car.

Two of those deserve a second sentence, because they are the ones people genuinely do not know.

Oily rags do not need a flame. Rags soaked with oil, stain, or solvent, balled up in an open bin, can heat on their own as the oil cures, and that is why they go in a metal can with a lid rather than a plastic bucket under the bench.

And antifreeze is sweet. That is not a metaphor; a dog will drink a puddle of it, and a small child will drink from a decanted bottle. Which is why nothing in this room is ever poured into a container that does not carry its own label, and why a spill on the slab gets cleaned rather than left to dry.

Everything else follows the shape of the room. Sharp edges get sheathed before they touch the wall. Guards spring back or the tool is retired. Loads go low, bolts go into joists, and nothing overhangs the bay where a person opens a car door.

Common Mistake

Starting with the pile inside the door instead of the bench, so there is nowhere to lay anything out; cleaning the slab first, so you scrub around a pile you are about to move anyway; and solving the half-finished project by lifting it onto a shelf, which only moves the decision somewhere darker and costs you the bench in the meantime.

The ritualBefore and After

Take the before photo before you touch a thing. Stand in the doorway with the whole depth of the garage in frame, bench, walls, and slab, and do not move the pile inside the door first, because that pile is the most honest thing in the picture.

Then work the seven zones, finish on the floor, and take the after from the same spot.

This is the garage the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, a bare bench holding the vise and the task light, tools hanging on their drawn outlines, fluids upright and dated, the long handles head-up on their rail, the bins overhead labelled to be read from the slab, and concrete you can see the colour of. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then keep the floor pale, because from now on the slab is the instrument that tells you what your car and your house are doing before either of them tells you themselves.

That last part is worth sitting with, because it is the only reward of its kind in the book. Every other room you have finished stays finished by being maintained. This one starts working for you. A pale slab is a diagnostic surface: the drip that appears under the front of the car, the damp patch creeping out from the wall, the rust ring under the water heater. On a dark, stained floor every one of those is invisible until it is expensive.

One Idea to Keep

A garage is not clear because you stacked it neatly. It is clear because you stopped letting the rest of the house postpone its decisions in here: everything that came in got a verdict the same day, everything that stayed got a rail, a hook, an outline, or a dated bin, and the floor got clean enough to read. Do one zone this week, and make it the workbench, because until one solid surface is clear, everything you pick up just goes back down somewhere else.
