## The room in one line
The garage is where the rest of the house sends what it could not decide about, and because the door stays shut most of the week nothing ever forces a verdict, which is why your best chisel and a box you have not opened since you moved in are sitting on the same shelf, above the only floor in the house that will tell you the truth about your car.

## The garage Shine layer (the nine rules from Ch 31 PLUS)
- **Nothing here is being stored, it is being postponed**: the chair with the wobbly leg and the charity sack by the side door are not in storage, they are awaiting a verdict nobody will give them. Anything carried in without a rail, shelf, or bin already waiting for it gets its verdict the same day.
- **Get it all off the floor, then read the floor**: the slab is cleaned LAST, once the walls and racks have taken what belongs on them, and it is degreased properly once. After that a pale floor turns every new drip into information.
- **Weight and height are the hazard here**: heavy low and light high, bolts into joists rather than plasterboard, nothing overhead you cannot bring down with both hands on a stable ladder, and nothing overhanging the bay where people get out of a car.
- **This room holds the real chemistry**: petrol, antifreeze, weed killer, oxidising feed, oily rags, and lithium packs. Every one of them is fine where it belongs and genuinely dangerous where it does not.

## Seven zones (start at the Primary Workbench; the slab comes last)
1. **Primary Workbench** - the one surface in the house where things get fixed; clear it first because everything else gets laid out on it.
2. **Hand Tool Wall and Cabinets** - the weekly tools, visible, findable, and provably all present against drawn outlines.
3. **Power Tool and Battery Zone** - one shelf per battery system; dust is what turns a warm tool into a hot one.
4. **Automotive Care Zone** - fluids upright and dated on a tray; the tray tells you what is seeping before the floor does.
5. **Sports and Recreation Zone** - gear for the sports this household plays now; nothing goes away damp.
6. **Lawn and Garden Tool Zone** - long handles head-up on a rail; the chemicals latched above head height.
7. **Bulk and Overhead Storage** - the once-or-twice-a-year things, high and dated; the depth of dust on a lid is the report.

## FROZEN STRINGS (byte-verbatim in manuscript AND final.html)

### Seven zones
The garage is seven small zones, not one big job: the primary workbench, the hand tool wall and cabinets, the power tool and battery zone, the automotive care zone, the sports and recreation zone, the lawn and garden tool zone, and the bulk and overhead storage. Do them one at a time, and start at the primary workbench, because until one solid surface is clear, everything you pick up just goes back down somewhere else. Leave the slab until last, once the walls and the racks have taken what belongs on them.

### The rule (postponed, not stored)
The garage takes what every other room in the house evicts, and because the door stays shut most of the week nothing ever forces a verdict on any of it. That is why your best chisel and a box you have not opened since you moved in can sit on the same shelf. So understand what is actually in here: the chair with the wobbly leg, the charity sack that has stood by the side door since spring, the printer you replaced. None of it is being stored. It is being postponed. Anything carried in with no rail, shelf, or bin already waiting for it gets its verdict the same day, or it quietly becomes part of the floor.

### The definition (defbox)
A finished garage is one you can walk into and work in without moving anything first: a bare bench holding only the vise and the task light, every hand tool hanging on its own drawn outline with no blank silhouettes, one shelf per battery system with nothing sleeping on a charger, fluids upright and dated on a clean tray, bikes on wall hooks with bare floor beneath them, long handles head-up on a rail and the garden chemicals latched above head height, overhead bins labelled to be read from the slab, and swept concrete you can see the colour of.

### Off-the-floor reframe (the floor is an instrument)
Everything in this room wants to end up on the slab, which is why the slab is cleaned last: only once the walls and the racks have taken what belongs on them is there a floor to clean rather than a pile to work around. Then degrease it properly, once, with degreaser, a stiff brush, and absorbent left on the old stains overnight. After that a sweep holds it. And here is what you actually bought with that afternoon: a pale floor turns every new drip into information. You will see the gearbox weeping or the water heater starting to go the week it starts, not the month it fails.

### Weight-and-height reframe (the hazard that is not dirt)
Every other room's hazards were small. This room's are heavy, and they are all above somebody. So the load rules are simple and they are not negotiable. Heavy bins go on the lowest rack and light bulky things go highest. Rack bolts land in joists, never in plasterboard alone. Nothing goes overhead that you cannot bring down with both hands on a stable ladder, and nothing you might need in the dark or in a hurry. Nothing overhangs the bay where people open a car door. And a bike hook is rated and driven into a stud, or it is not a bike hook.

### Real-chemistry reframe (fine where it belongs, dangerous where it does not)
This is the room where the household's genuinely dangerous chemistry lives, and none of it is dangerous by itself. It becomes dangerous by proximity. Petrol stays out of the house envelope and away from the water heater's pilot flame, because vapour pools low. Fertiliser is an oxidiser and never shares a shelf with fuel. Rags soaked with oil, stain, or solvent go into a metal can with a lid, because they can heat on their own with no flame anywhere near them. Lithium packs never sleep on a charger, and never sit beside the fuel can. And nothing, ever, is decanted into an unlabelled bottle.

### Safety reframe (the five questions in the heaviest room)
Burn or fire is the question this room answers first, and it has four answers: oily rags, petrol vapour near a pilot, lithium packs charging unattended on wood, and fertiliser stored against fuel. Poison, choke, or strangle is the one people underrate, because antifreeze tastes sweet and a spilled puddle or a decanted unlabelled bottle will kill a dog or a curious child. Fall, cut, or crush covers everything sharp at hand height, every guard wedged open, every loaded rack, and a car held on a jack with no axle stands under the sills. Fall is the ladder rocking on a sloped slab. Water and electricity is a charger base sitting where meltwater runs off the car.

### Before-and-after caption
This is the garage the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, a bare bench holding the vise and the task light, tools hanging on their drawn outlines, fluids upright and dated, the long handles head-up on their rail, the bins overhead labelled to be read from the slab, and concrete you can see the colour of. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then keep the floor pale, because from now on the slab is the instrument that tells you what your car and your house are doing before either of them tells you themselves.

### One Idea to Keep
A garage is not clear because you stacked it neatly. It is clear because you stopped letting the rest of the house postpone its decisions in here: everything that came in got a verdict the same day, everything that stayed got a rail, a hook, an outline, or a dated bin, and the floor got clean enough to read. Do one zone this week, and make it the workbench, because until one solid surface is clear, everything you pick up just goes back down somewhere else.

## Four callouts (frozen)
- **Quick Win (win)**: Clear the workbench top down to bare wood before you touch anything else. Everything else in this garage is going to get laid out on it while you judge it, and it is the one part of the room that pays you back the same afternoon. It also gives you the honest test for every other zone: if a thing has no rail, hook, shelf, or bin waiting for it, it does not go back up.
- **6S Tip (tip)**: Degrease the slab properly once, with degreaser, a stiff brush, and absorbent granules left on the old stains overnight. After that a sweep with the push broom holds it. What you are really buying is a pale floor, because a pale floor turns every new drip into information: you see the gearbox weeping the week it starts, not the month it fails.
- **The Postponed Pile Challenge (family)**: Walk the garage with the household and name out loud what is genuinely being stored and what is only being postponed. The chair with the wobbly leg. The charity sack by the side door. The printer you replaced. Anything in the second group gets its verdict today, because a thing with no rail, shelf, or bin waiting for it does not get stored in a garage. It becomes part of the floor.
- **Common Mistake (mistake)**: Starting with the pile inside the door instead of the bench, so there is nowhere to lay anything out; cleaning the slab first, so you scrub around a pile you are about to move anyway; and solving the half-finished project by lifting it onto a shelf, which only moves the decision somewhere darker and costs you the bench in the meantime.

## The calls that matter here
The project that has been there since spring (one of three verdicts today: a specific weekend written on it, a breakdown that returns the timber and hardware to stock, or the skip, because anything that has sat untouched through a full season is not a project any more but furniture with your intentions stacked on top, and lifting it onto a shelf only moves the decision somewhere darker); the third hammer (working is not the test, the test is which one your hand actually goes to, so keep that one plus exactly one deliberate spare parked where a second person would need it, and every remaining duplicate goes today, because a hammer you keep and never choose is taking the hook that would have made the hammer you love findable in one glance from the door); the tool whose batteries no longer exist (decide with money rather than loyalty: price one replacement pack today, and if a pack costs more than half what a current equivalent tool costs, or you cannot buy one at all, the tool is finished, so sell it with its charger while it still means something to somebody); the fluids for the car you no longer own (every bottle must match a vehicle parked at this address, no match no keep, offered to a neighbour this week or taken to household hazardous waste and never down a drain, and from today every bottle wears the month you opened it); the gear from the sport you stopped playing (two full seasons of the right weather with no use means you have not paused, you have stopped, so sell it while the bindings and the groupset are still current, unless you can finish the sentence "I will use this on ___" with a real date, which goes on the bin); the half-used chemicals (start with the unlabelled sprayer, because you cannot safely use what you cannot name, so it goes to hazardous waste this month unopened, then apply one season of intent to the rest and never pour any of it down a drain, into a gutter, or onto the ground behind the shed); the box you have never opened (bring it down and open it on the floor with a rubbish sack, a donation box, and one empty container beside you, and whatever you truly want to keep has to fit that one container and carry a living person's name, because a box that goes back up unopened has just bought itself another five years).

## Watch for in the garage (five safety questions)
- **Burn or fire**: rags soaked with oil, stain, or solvent balled in an open bin, which can heat on their own with no flame anywhere near them; petrol vapour pooling low near the water heater's pilot; lithium packs left charging overnight on a wooden shelf, or stored beside the petrol can and the rag bin; the mower's petrol can sharing a shelf with fertiliser, which is an oxidiser; camp stove canisters and lantern fuel stored in among the sports bins.
- **Poison, choke, or strangle**: antifreeze, which tastes sweet, as a spilled puddle or in a decanted unlabelled bottle, and will kill a dog or a curious child; concentrated weed killer decanted into an unlabelled bottle; slug pellets or granular feed left where a dog can reach and will eat them; an unlatched low cabinet holding loose box-cutter blades, screws, and drill bits within a toddler's reach.
- **Fall, cut, or crush**: unsheathed saw teeth and chisel edges hung at hand height, and chisels or utility knives lying edge-up on the bench where a hand sweeps sawdust without looking; heavy wrenches on shallow hooks directly above where you stand; a circular saw stored with its guard wedged open; a trolley jack stored still loaded, or a car held up on a jack with no axle stands under the sills; a bike coming off an overhead hook onto a car roof or a person, and skis stored edge-out at head height; rakes and forks standing loose in a corner, and unguarded hedge shear blades on an open shelf; an overloaded rack, or bins overhanging the rails directly above where people get in and out of a car.
- **Fall**: working from a ladder rocking on a sloped slab, or coming down with a bin in both arms and no free hand for the stile; standing on the cooler rather than a ladder to reach over the ball rack.
- **Water and electricity**: the bench outlet or a power strip sitting low on a slab that runs wet when a car comes in from the rain; charger bases on the slab where meltwater runs off the car; extension leads daisy-chained to feed a whole shelf.
