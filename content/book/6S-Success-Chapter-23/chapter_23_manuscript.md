## Chapter Opening

Stand back and look at what you have built. A home cleared of what does not belong, arranged so everything has a place, kept clean on a light rhythm, and made safe for the people in it. Four of the six S's, done. It is a genuinely good home, and you should feel good about it.

Now notice something a little uncomfortable about it. Almost all of it lives in exactly one place, and that place is you. You know which drawer things go back into, and why. You know the best way to load the dishwasher so everything actually gets clean, and the order of the bedtime routine that makes it go smoothly, and the trick with the launch pad that keeps the mornings calm. Nobody wrote any of it down, because you did not need to; you simply know it. Which means the home you built works beautifully, right up until the day you are sick, or away, or just tired and out of patience, and then it quietly stops working, because the person it was all running on is not available, and the good ways were never anywhere but in your head.

That is the gap the fifth S closes, and it is why Standardize exists. Standardize is not about adding rules or turning your warm home into an office with procedures. It is about taking the good ways you have already found, the ones that currently live in your memory and your hands, and writing them down, so they stop depending on you. That is the whole of it, and it is more of a relief than it sounds, because it means you finally get to set down a weight you have been carrying without noticing: the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works.

## Why Things Drift Back

You have probably noticed, over the course of this book, a quiet worry running underneath the work: will it last? You sort a drawer and it is perfect, and some part of you knows that in three months it could be a mess again. That worry is not paranoia. It is an accurate reading of how homes behave, and it is exactly what this S is built to answer.

> Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you.

Things drift back for a reason that has nothing to do with anyone being lazy or careless. They drift back because a way of doing something that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and "their own way" is rarely the way you worked out. You put the mixing bowls in the low cupboard for a good reason; someone who does not know the reason puts them back on the counter, and now the system you designed is quietly being undone by a person who is genuinely trying to help. Multiply that by every task in the home and every person in it, and the drift is not a moral failing. It is just what happens when the best way is a secret.

The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, saw the same thing and gave this S a blunt job: prevent reversion back to the old way. Not through willpower or nagging, but by making the good way visible and shared, so that following it is the easy, obvious thing and everyone is working from the same page. A standard is how you make the best way visible. It is the difference between a home that holds its shape and one that slowly slides back the moment your attention moves elsewhere.

## A Standard Is a Recipe

So let me say plainly what a standard is, because the word itself does the chapter a disservice. "Standard" sounds cold. It sounds like rules, clipboards, and someone checking whether you complied. Put all of that out of your mind, because a home standard is a much warmer and simpler thing.

> A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day.

Think about an actual recipe, the kind on a card in a kitchen. It is not a rule anyone is enforcing. It is a gift: someone worked out how to make the thing turn out well, and they wrote it down, so that you do not have to work it out again from scratch, and so it comes out right whether you are sharp that day or half asleep. A recipe takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read. That is precisely what a home standard does, for the recurring tasks of a home instead of for a cake.

> A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found.

Notice what that definition asks for and what it does not. It asks that the way be the best you currently know, written plainly, and followable by anyone. It does not ask for length, formality, or completeness. A good home standard is usually a few lines, in plain words, about one task. If it is so long or so fussy that nobody will read it, it has failed on the one thing that matters, which is being used. The test of a standard is never how thorough it looks. It is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten.

## The Best Way We Know Today

There is one more word in the title, and it is the most important one, because it is what keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people fear.

> The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve.

This matters more than it might seem, because it dissolves the main objection to standardizing anything at home, which is a good and healthy objection: I do not want to lock the household into one rigid way of doing things forever. You are right not to want that, and a standard, properly understood, is the opposite of that. A standard is a starting point, not a finish line. It captures the best way you know as of today, so that you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced the moment someone works out something better. When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the standard does not win the argument by virtue of being written down. The better way wins, and then you spend thirty seconds updating the card, and now the better way is the standard.

Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick. Without a written standard, a good new idea gets used once and forgotten. With one, the improvement gets captured, and the next person starts from there instead of from scratch. The card on the cupboard door is not the household's rule-book. It is its running record of the best way anyone has found so far, and the whole point of writing it in pencil, so to speak, is that it is meant to be improved.

> A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony.

## Write Your First Standard

Enough concept. Let me walk you through making one, because it is much smaller than it sounds, and doing one is the fastest way to feel how light a standard really is.

Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it. Not the hardest thing in the home; the opposite. Choose something small and ordinary that you have quietly become the keeper of, loading the dishwasher, the morning launch-pad routine, taking out the recycling on the right night in the right way. Now, instead of inventing a "best way", just capture the one you already use, because you have almost certainly worked out a good way already without ever writing it down. Watch yourself do the task once, and write the steps as you go, in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps. If you find yourself writing step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task; cut back to the handful of steps that actually matter to the result.

Then, and this is the part people skip, put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible. A dishwasher standard lives taped inside the cabinet above the dishwasher, not in a binder in the office. The whole value of a standard is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it, and a perfect document nobody opens is worth less than three scrappy lines stuck in the right spot. And there is a lovely shortcut for finding the best way in the first place: if someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, do not invent a standard from scratch, just ask them to show you, and write down what they do. The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands. A standard is often just the act of making one person's good habit into everyone's.

> **Quick Win**
> Pick one recurring thing that only really works when you do it, loading the dishwasher, the bedtime routine, taking out the recycling, and write down the best way in five plain steps. Stick it where the task happens. One standard, today.

> **6S Tip**
> Write the standard where the work happens, not in a binder. A note taped inside the cupboard door beats a perfect document nobody opens. The best standard is the one you cannot avoid seeing.

## The Needle Holds, and Stops Sliding Back

Go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice what it does not do. It does not move. And that is not a disappointment; it is the whole point of this S, showing up honestly on the dial.

Every chapter until now, the needle traveled because the home changed: clutter left, things found homes, surfaces got clean and read, dangers were removed. This chapter changed nothing in the rooms. You wrote down a way of doing something you were already doing. Nothing was cleared, so the needle holds exactly where Safety left it, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise.

> A standard is the best way you know today, written down.

But look closer, because for the first time the dial shows something new, and it is behind the needle rather than ahead of it. Until now, every gain the needle recorded was quietly reversible. A sorted room can re-clutter. A clean surface re-soils. A good habit lapses. Every position the needle ever reached, it could also slide back from, and some part of you has known that all along. Standardize is the first S whose job is not to move the needle forward but to keep it from sliding back, so a small catch appears behind the needle, a ratchet holding it against the pull toward friction, and the backslide that used to be always possible is, for the first time, blocked. The needle did not advance today. It got something arguably more valuable: it stopped being able to retreat. The goal is still ahead on the calm side, because Standardize has only begun and Sustain still follows, and holding a gain in place is exactly the work these last two S's exist to do.

> **Family Challenge**

## Standardize Has Only Begun

You changed nothing in the rooms this chapter, and you may have changed something more durable than any single room: you took one good way of doing things out of your own head and put it where the whole household can reach it. That is the fifth S in its smallest form, and it is the move that makes everything before it capable of lasting.

What you have not done yet is the rest of it, and that is deliberate. One card on one cupboard door is a proof of concept, not a system. Turning a handful of standards into something the whole home runs on takes a few more tools: a way to make sure recurring work actually happens and nothing is forgotten, a sense of when things get done and by whom, and, for the tasks where a picture beats a paragraph, a way to show the right result rather than describe it. Those are the next two chapters. For now, hold on to the one idea that all of Standardize grows from, which is also the warmest way to think about it: you are not writing rules, you are writing down the best way you know today, so that the good you have built can be followed by anyone and improved by everyone.

> **Common Mistake**
> Writing a standard as a strict rule and then defending it forever. A standard you are not allowed to change becomes a cage, and people quietly route around a cage. Keep it the best way for now, and let anyone propose a better one.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> Write down the best way you know today. It takes the good you have built out of your head and your hands and puts it somewhere it can hold without you, be followed by anyone, and be improved by everyone. That is all a standard is, and it is how the gains last.
