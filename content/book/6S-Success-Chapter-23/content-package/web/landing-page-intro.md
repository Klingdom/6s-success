# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 23, Standardize: The Best Way We Know Today

Four S's are behind you. Your home is sorted, arranged, clean, and safe, real work, actually done, and if you have come this far you have every right to feel it. But there is a quiet problem sitting underneath all of it, and this chapter is where we finally say it out loud. Almost everything you have built lives in exactly one place: you. You know the best way to load the dishwasher so it all comes out clean. You know the order of the bedtime routine that keeps it calm. You know the small trick that keeps mornings from tipping over. And almost none of it was ever written down, because you did not need to write it down, you simply know it. So the home works beautifully, right up until the day you are sick, or away, or just out of patience, and then it quietly stops, because the person the whole thing was running on is not available. That is not a failure. It is just what a home looks like when the best way lives in one head.

That is the gap the fifth S closes, and it closes it with the plainest tool in the whole book: you write the best way down. Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you. And here is the part worth saying before anything else, because the word "standard" sounds cold, like rules and clipboards and someone checking whether you complied: that is not what this is. Things drift back not because anyone is lazy or careless, but because a way that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. Multiply that by every task and every person in the home, and the drift is not a moral failing. It is just what happens when the best way is a secret. A standard is how you make the best way visible.

So what is a standard, really? Not bureaucracy. A recipe. A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day. A recipe is not a rule anyone is enforcing; it is a gift. Someone worked out how to make the thing turn out well and wrote it down, so you do not have to work it out from scratch, and it takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read. That is all a home standard is. It does not ask for length or formality. A good one is usually a few plain lines about one task, and the test is never how thorough it looks, it is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten.

The most important word in the title is the smallest one: today. It is the word that keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people are right to fear. The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve. Nobody wants to lock the household into one rigid way forever, and a standard is the opposite of that: a starting point, not a finish line. When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card. Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick. A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony.

And making one is much smaller than it sounds. Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it, something small and ordinary you have quietly become the keeper of. Instead of inventing a best way, capture the one you already use: watch yourself do the task once and write the steps as you go, in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps; if you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task. Then, the part people skip, put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible, because the whole value is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it. And there is a lovely shortcut: if someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, do not invent a standard from scratch, ask them to show you and write down what they do, because the best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands.

None of this turns a warm home into an office full of rules. It is the opposite. A home whose good ways live only in one person's head is the normal case, not a failing, and writing the best way down is more of a relief than it sounds: it is the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works, finally set down. Read Chapter 23 free in the online book, then take the smallest possible first step: pick one recurring thing that only really works when you do it, write down the best way in five plain steps, and stick it where the task happens. One standard, today. When you go and look at the friction meter this time, notice what it does not do: it does not move, because nothing in the rooms changed. But for the first time something appears behind the needle, a catch, a ratchet, holding it against the pull back toward friction. Every gain until now was quietly reversible; a written standard is the first thing that holds a gain in place. The needle did not advance today; it got something arguably more valuable, it stopped being able to retreat. The goal stays ahead on the calm side, because Standardize has only begun.
