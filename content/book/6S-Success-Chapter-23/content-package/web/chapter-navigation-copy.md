## You are here blurb
You are at Chapter 23, the first chapter of Part Seven, Standardize, and it opens the fifth of the six S's. The four S's before it made your home sorted, arranged, clean, and safe, real work, done. But there is a quiet problem underneath all of it, and this chapter names it: almost everything you have built lives in exactly one place, you. You know the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick that keeps mornings calm, and none of it was ever written down, because you simply know it. So the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, away, or tired, and then it quietly stops, because the person it was all running on is not available. That is the gap the fifth S closes. Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do; a standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you. Things drift back not because anyone is careless but because a way that lives in one head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and the best way stays a secret. The people who first named these steps gave this S a blunt job, prevent reversion back to the old way, done not by willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared. The chapter's hero dissolves the fear that standardizing a home means rules and clipboards: a standard is not bureaucracy, it is a recipe, the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day. The word in the title doing the quiet work is today, because it keeps a standard from becoming the cage people fear: a standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken, it is simply what you improve. A standard is not a rule carved in stone; it is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony. The move is small: pick one recurring task that only really works when you do it, watch yourself do it once, write the steps in plain words, three to six of them, and stick it where the task happens, not in a folder; and if someone in the home already does it best, ask them to show you and write down what they do. This chapter does not build the checklist, schedule, and responsibility system (Chapter 24), the visual and pictorial standards (Chapter 25), or the long-term adherence and audit system (Sustain), and it does not re-teach Sort, Straighten, Shine, or Safety. Because writing down the best way changes nothing in the rooms, the friction meter holds exactly where Safety left it and does not move; but for the first time something appears behind the needle, a catch, a ratchet, holding it against the pull back toward friction. Every gain until now was quietly reversible, a sorted room re-clutters, a clean surface re-soils, a habit lapses; a written standard is the first thing that blocks the backslide. The needle did not advance today; it got something arguably more valuable, it stopped being able to retreat. This is a hold with a new anti-backslide catch, not a move and not a milestone, and the goal, out on the calm side, stays ahead, because Standardize has only begun and Sustain still follows. The rule to carry out: a standard is the best way you know today, written down.
