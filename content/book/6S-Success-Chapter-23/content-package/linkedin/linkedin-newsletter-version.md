# You Built a Good Home. Right Now Almost All of It Lives in One Person's Head, and That Is the Quiet Problem the Fifth S Solves.

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Stand back and look at what four S's have built. A home cleared, arranged, kept clean on a light rhythm, and made safe. It is a genuinely good home, and you did that. Now notice something uncomfortable, because it is the reason there is a fifth S at all: almost all of it lives in exactly one place, and that place is you.

You know which drawer things go into and why, the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick with the launch pad by the door. Nobody wrote any of it down, because you did not need to; you simply know it. Which means the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, or away, or tired and out of patience, and then it quietly stops.

**Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you.**

That is the gap the fifth S closes. Standardize is not about adding rules or turning a warm home into an office with procedures. It is about taking the good ways you have already found and writing them down so they stop depending on you. It is more of a relief than it sounds, because you finally get to set down the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works.

## Why things drift back

A quiet worry runs underneath this whole book: will it last? You sort a drawer and know that in three months it could be a mess again. That worry is an accurate reading of how homes behave, and it is exactly what this S is built to answer.

Things drift back not because anyone is lazy but because a way of doing something that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else. So everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. You put the mixing bowls in the low cupboard for a good reason; someone who does not know the reason puts them back on the counter, undoing the system while genuinely trying to help. Multiply that by every task and every person, and the drift is not a moral failing; it is just what happens when the best way is a secret.

**A standard is how you make the best way visible.**

The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, gave this S a blunt job: prevent reversion back to the old way, not through willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared.

## A standard is a recipe

Say plainly what a standard is, because the word itself does the chapter a disservice. "Standard" sounds cold, like rules, clipboards, and someone checking whether you complied. Put all of that out of your mind, because a home standard is warmer and simpler. Think about an actual recipe on a card in a kitchen: not a rule anyone is enforcing, but a gift. Someone worked out how to make the thing turn out well and wrote it down, so you do not have to work it out from scratch and it comes out right whether you are sharp or half asleep.

**A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day.**

Notice what a standard asks for and what it does not. The way should be the best you currently know, written plainly, and followable by anyone; it does not ask for length, formality, or completeness. A good home standard is usually a few lines about one task. The test of a standard is never how thorough it looks. It is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten.

**A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found.**

## The best way we know today

There is one more word in the title, and it is the most important, because it keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people fear. The word is today. This dissolves the healthy objection to standardizing anything at home: nobody wants to lock the household into one rigid way forever. A standard is the opposite of that, a starting point, not a finish line. It captures the best way you know as of today so you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced the moment someone finds something better. When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card.

**The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve.**

Without a written standard, a good new idea gets used once and forgotten; with one, the improvement is captured and the next person starts from there. It is written in pencil, so to speak, meant to be improved.

**A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony.**

## Write your first standard

Enough concept. Making one is much smaller than it sounds, and doing one is the fastest way to feel how light a standard is. Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it, not the hardest thing in the home but the opposite, something small and ordinary you have quietly become the keeper of: loading the dishwasher, the morning launch-pad routine, taking out the recycling. Instead of inventing a best way, capture the one you already use: watch yourself do the task once and write the steps as you go, in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps; if you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task.

Then, the part people skip, put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible. A dishwasher standard lives taped inside the cabinet above the dishwasher. The whole value is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it, and a perfect document nobody opens is worth less than three scrappy lines stuck in the right spot. And there is a lovely shortcut: if someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, ask them to show you and write down what they do.

**The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands.**

## The needle holds, and stops sliding back

Go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice what it does not do: it does not move. That is not a disappointment; it is the whole point of this S showing up honestly on the dial. Every chapter until now, the needle traveled because the home changed; this chapter changed nothing in the rooms. You wrote down a way of doing something you were already doing, nothing was cleared, so the needle holds exactly where Safety left it.

But look closer, because for the first time the dial shows something new, behind the needle rather than ahead of it. Until now, every gain the needle recorded was quietly reversible: a sorted room can re-clutter, a clean surface re-soils, a good habit lapses. Standardize is the first S whose job is not to move the needle forward but to keep it from sliding back, so a small catch appears behind the needle, a ratchet holding it against the pull toward friction, and the backslide that used to be always possible is, for the first time, blocked.

**The needle did not advance today. It got something arguably more valuable: it stopped being able to retreat.**

The goal is still ahead on the calm side, because Standardize has only begun and Sustain still follows.

## The assignment

Right now, before you do anything else, pick one recurring thing that only really works when you do it, loading the dishwasher, the bedtime routine, taking out the recycling, and write down the best way in five plain steps. Watch yourself do it once and write as you go; keep it to as few steps as will do the job. Then stick it where the task happens, not in a binder, taped inside the cupboard door where the next person meets it at the moment they need it. One standard, today. And if someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, take the shortcut: ask them to show you and write down what they do, because a standard is often just the act of making one person's good habit into everyone's.

**A standard is the best way you know today, written down. Write one down, and the good you have built stops depending on you: it can hold without you, be followed by anyone, and be improved by everyone.**
