# Chapter 23 Key Takeaways: Standardize: The Best Way We Know Today

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **This chapter opens Standardize.** It is the first chapter of Part Seven and opens the fifth S. Four S's have made the home sorted, arranged, clean, and safe, and this chapter names the quiet problem underneath all of it and delivers the concept that answers it: what a standard is, and why it keeps the gains from slipping away.

2. **The good you have built lives in one place, and that place is you.** You know the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick that keeps mornings calm, and none of it was ever written down because you simply know it. So the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, away, or tired, and then it quietly stops, because the person it was all running on is not available.

3. **The drift reframe, frozen.** Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you.

4. **Things drift back for a reason that is not carelessness.** A way of doing something that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. Multiply that by every task and every person in the home, and the drift is not a moral failing; it is just what happens when the best way is a secret.

5. **Standardize has a blunt job: prevent reversion.** The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, gave this S the job of preventing reversion back to the old way, not through willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared, so following it is the easy, obvious thing. A standard is how you make the best way visible.

6. **A standard is a recipe, not bureaucracy.** The word "standard" sounds cold, like rules and clipboards and someone checking whether you complied. A home standard is warmer and simpler. Like a recipe, it takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read.

7. **The recipe reframe, frozen.** A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day.

8. **The definition of a standard, frozen.** A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found.

9. **A standard is measured by use, not by thoroughness.** A good home standard is usually a few lines, in plain words, about one task. It does not ask for length, formality, or completeness. If it is so long or fussy that nobody reads it, it has failed on the one thing that matters. The test is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten.

10. **The word doing the quiet work is today.** A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve.

11. **The today reframe, frozen.** The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve.

12. **A standard is a starting point, not a finish line.** It dissolves the healthy objection to standardizing anything at home: nobody wants to lock the household into one rigid way forever. A standard captures the best way as of today so you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced. When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card.

13. **A standard is what makes improvement stick.** Without a written standard, a good new idea gets used once and forgotten. With one, the improvement is captured, and the next person starts from there instead of from scratch. It is a running record of the best way anyone has found so far, written in pencil so it is meant to be improved.

14. **The living-standard line, frozen.** A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony.

15. **Write your first standard by capturing what you already do.** Pick a small, ordinary recurring task that mostly works only when you do it. Watch yourself do it once and write the steps in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps. If you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task.

16. **Put the standard where the task happens.** A dishwasher standard lives taped inside the cabinet above the dishwasher, not in a binder in the office. The whole value is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it, and a perfect document nobody opens is worth less than three scrappy lines stuck in the right spot.

17. **The best way usually already lives in someone's hands.** If someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, do not invent a standard from scratch; ask them to show you, and write down what they do. A standard is often just the act of making one person's good habit into everyone's.

18. **The rule, frozen (hero).** A standard is the best way you know today, written down.

19. **The needle holds, and for the first time cannot slide back.** The dial does not move, because nothing in the rooms changed; you wrote down a way you were already doing. But for the first time something appears behind the needle: a catch, a ratchet, holding it against the pull back toward friction. Every gain until now was quietly reversible; a written standard is the first thing that blocks the backslide. The needle did not advance; it stopped being able to retreat.

20. **Standardize has only begun, and the goal is still ahead.** One card on one cupboard door is a proof of concept, not a system. The next chapters add the tools, checklists, schedules, and responsibilities in Chapter 24, then pictures and visual standards in Chapter 25, and Sustain follows. One Idea to Keep: write down the best way you know today, so the good you have built can hold without you, be followed by anyone, and be improved by everyone. That is all a standard is, and it is how the gains last.
