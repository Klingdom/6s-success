# Chapter 23 Outline: Standardize: The Best Way We Know Today

A section-by-section map of the chapter as written in the manuscript. This chapter is the first chapter of Part Seven (Standardize) and opens the fifth S. It takes the home that four S's made sorted, arranged, clean, and safe, names the quiet problem underneath it (almost all of it lives in one person's head and hands, so it lasts only as long as their memory and energy and drifts back the day they are tired or away), and delivers the concept that answers it: what a standard is, and why it prevents reversion. It establishes the drift reframe (a way that lives in one head is invisible, so everyone does it their own way and the best way is a secret), delivers the hero (a standard is a recipe, the best way you know today, written down plainly so it turns out right for anyone, carried by the recipe reframe and the definition box), delivers the living-standard heart (the word today: the current best word, rewritten without ceremony, a starting point not a cage, carried by the today reframe and the living-standard line), walks the reader through writing one plain standard and putting it where the work happens, and reads the friction meter as a HOLD with a new catch behind the needle. It does not build the checklist, schedule, and responsibility system (Chapter 24), the visual and pictorial standards (Chapter 25), or the long-term adherence and audit system (Sustain), and it does not re-teach the earlier S's. Because writing down the best way changes nothing in the rooms, the needle does not move, but for the first time a catch appears behind it that keeps a gain from sliding back.

## 1. Chapter Opening: The good you built lives in one place, and that place is you
Stand back and look at what four S's have built: a home cleared, arranged, kept clean on a light rhythm, and made safe. It is a genuinely good home. Now notice something uncomfortable: almost all of it lives in exactly one place, and that place is you. You know which drawer things go into and why, the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick with the launch pad. Nobody wrote it down, because you did not need to; you simply know it. Which means the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, or away, or tired and out of patience, and then it quietly stops working. That is the gap the fifth S closes. Standardize is not about adding rules or turning a warm home into an office with procedures; it is about taking the good ways you have already found and writing them down so they stop depending on you. It is more of a relief than it sounds, because you finally get to set down the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- See why the good ways you have built drift back when they live only in your head
- Understand what a standard actually is: the best way you know today, written down
- Treat a standard as a recipe, not a rule, so it stays human and easy to follow
- Keep a standard living by rewriting it the moment someone finds a better way
- Write your first standard for a recurring task and put it where the work happens

## 3. Why Things Drift Back
A quiet worry runs underneath the whole book: will it last? You sort a drawer and know that in three months it could be a mess again. That worry is an accurate reading of how homes behave, and it is what this S is built to answer. Things drift back not because anyone is lazy but because a way of doing something that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. You put the mixing bowls in the low cupboard for a good reason; someone who does not know the reason puts them back on the counter, undoing the system while genuinely trying to help. Multiply that by every task and every person, and the drift is not a moral failing; it is just what happens when the best way is a secret. The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, gave this S a blunt job: prevent reversion back to the old way, not through willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared. A standard is how you make the best way visible.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the drift reframe): Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you.

## 4. A Standard Is a Recipe
Say plainly what a standard is, because the word itself does the chapter a disservice. "Standard" sounds cold, like rules, clipboards, and someone checking whether you complied. Put all of that out of your mind, because a home standard is warmer and simpler. Think about an actual recipe on a card in a kitchen: not a rule anyone is enforcing, but a gift. Someone worked out how to make the thing turn out well and wrote it down, so you do not have to work it out from scratch and it comes out right whether you are sharp or half asleep. A recipe takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read, and that is precisely what a home standard does for the recurring tasks of a home. Notice what the definition asks for and what it does not: the way should be the best you currently know, written plainly, and followable by anyone; it does not ask for length, formality, or completeness. A good home standard is usually a few lines about one task. The test is never how thorough it looks; it is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the recipe reframe): A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day.
- Definition box (frozen): A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found.

## 5. The Best Way We Know Today
There is one more word in the title, and it is the most important, because it keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people fear. The word is today. This dissolves the healthy objection to standardizing anything at home: nobody wants to lock the household into one rigid way forever. A standard is the opposite of that, a starting point, not a finish line. It captures the best way you know as of today so you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced the moment someone finds something better. When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the standard does not win by virtue of being written down; the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card. Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick. Without a written standard, a good new idea gets used once and forgotten; with one, the improvement is captured and the next person starts from there. It is written in pencil, so to speak, meant to be improved.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the today reframe, the living-standard heart): The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the living-standard line): A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony.

## 6. Write Your First Standard
Enough concept: making one is much smaller than it sounds, and doing one is the fastest way to feel how light a standard is. Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it, not the hardest thing in the home but the opposite, something small and ordinary you have quietly become the keeper of (loading the dishwasher, the morning launch-pad routine, taking out the recycling). Instead of inventing a best way, capture the one you already use: watch yourself do the task once and write the steps as you go, in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps; if you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task. Then, the part people skip, put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible. A dishwasher standard lives taped inside the cabinet above the dishwasher. The whole value is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it, and a perfect document nobody opens is worth less than three scrappy lines stuck in the right spot. And there is a lovely shortcut: if someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, ask them to show you and write down what they do. The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands.
- Infographic (a sample standard card): a single warm "standard card" at a realistic size, drawn like a recipe card or a note you would tape up. A plain title at the top (for example "Loading the Dishwasher"), four or five short numbered steps in plain language (for example: 1. Scrape, do not rinse. 2. Big items along the sides and back. 3. Bowls and cups angled down on the top rack. 4. Knives point down. 5. Run it when it is full, not before), and a small line at the bottom reading "Lives: inside the cabinet above the dishwasher". A little curled piece of tape at one corner. Warm paper card, slate text, one honey accent on the title. Caption: a whole standard, start to finish, a plain title, a handful of steps anyone can follow, and one line saying where it lives; this is not a document, it is a recipe card for a recurring task, and it took about five minutes to make.
- Callout (Quick Win): Pick one recurring thing that only really works when you do it, loading the dishwasher, the bedtime routine, taking out the recycling, and write down the best way in five plain steps. Stick it where the task happens. One standard, today.
- Callout (6S Tip): Write the standard where the work happens, not in a binder. A note taped inside the cupboard door beats a perfect document nobody opens. The best standard is the one you cannot avoid seeing.

## 7. The Needle Holds, and Stops Sliding Back
Go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice what it does not do: it does not move. That is not a disappointment; it is the whole point of this S showing up honestly on the dial. Every chapter until now, the needle traveled because the home changed; this chapter changed nothing in the rooms. You wrote down a way of doing something you were already doing, nothing was cleared, so the needle holds exactly where Safety left it, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. But look closer, because for the first time the dial shows something new, behind the needle rather than ahead of it. Until now, every gain the needle recorded was quietly reversible: a sorted room can re-clutter, a clean surface re-soils, a good habit lapses. Standardize is the first S whose job is not to move the needle forward but to keep it from sliding back, so a small catch appears behind the needle, a ratchet holding it against the pull toward friction, and the backslide that used to be always possible is, for the first time, blocked. The needle did not advance today; it got something arguably more valuable: it stopped being able to retreat. The goal is still ahead on the calm side, because Standardize has only begun and Sustain still follows.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the rule): A standard is the best way you know today, written down.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Ask the person who does a task best to teach it to everyone else, and write down what they do. The best way usually already lives in someone's hands; a standard just makes it everyone's.

## 8. Standardize Has Only Begun
You changed nothing in the rooms this chapter, and you may have changed something more durable than any single room: you took one good way of doing things out of your own head and put it where the whole household can reach it. That is the fifth S in its smallest form, and it is the move that makes everything before it capable of lasting. What you have not done yet is the rest of it, and that is deliberate. One card on one cupboard door is a proof of concept, not a system. Turning a handful of standards into something the whole home runs on takes a few more tools: a way to make sure recurring work actually happens and nothing is forgotten, a sense of when things get done and by whom, and, for the tasks where a picture beats a paragraph, a way to show the right result rather than describe it. Those are the next two chapters. For now, hold on to the one idea all of Standardize grows from, which is also the warmest way to think about it: you are not writing rules, you are writing down the best way you know today, so that the good you have built can be followed by anyone and improved by everyone.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Writing a standard as a strict rule and then defending it forever. A standard you are not allowed to change becomes a cage, and people quietly route around a cage. Keep it the best way for now, and let anyone propose a better one.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): Write down the best way you know today. It takes the good you have built out of your head and your hands and puts it somewhere it can hold without you, be followed by anyone, and be improved by everyone. That is all a standard is, and it is how the gains last.
