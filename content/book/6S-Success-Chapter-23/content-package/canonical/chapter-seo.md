## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Defines a standard in plain, human terms (the best way you know today, written down) rather than as corporate procedure or documentation, correcting the assumption that standardizing a home means rules and clipboards
- Names why gains drift back (a way that lives in one head is invisible, so everyone does it their own way and the best way is a secret), rather than blaming carelessness or willpower
- Frames a standard as a recipe and a gift, not bureaucracy: skill that lived in one person's hands, written down so it turns out right for anyone, even on a tired day
- Makes the word "today" the heart of the idea: a standard is the current best word, not the last word, rewritten without ceremony when a better way is found, so it is a starting point and not a cage
- Shows that a standard is measured by use, not thoroughness: a few plain lines about one task, and if nobody reads it, it has failed, correcting the instinct to write long, formal documents
- Gives a concrete, five-minute worked example (pick a recurring task, write three to six plain steps, put it where the work happens) and the shortcut of asking the person who does it best to teach it, instead of an abstract call to "create standards"
- Keeps the fence to Chapter 24 clean: it writes one plain standard as an example, and does not build the checklist, schedule, and responsibility system, or the visual standards (Chapter 25), or the long-term adherence and audit system (Sustain)
- Reads the friction meter honestly: the needle holds because nothing in the rooms changed, but for the first time a catch appears behind it that blocks the backslide every earlier gain could quietly suffer, so the reader is not sold a false forward step
- Keeps the register warm and anti-shame, with no force or control language, no hype, and no brand names: a standard is a gift to the household, not a compliance demand
