# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 23

**"Standardize: The Best Way We Know Today"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Seven · Standardize*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to
hold across all five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`,
terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B`, soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single warm
red `#CB4B36` reserved for the smallest marks only and used barely, if at all, on these calm images.
Natural soft daylight, shallow but not showy depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the finished home the reader has cleared, arranged, cleaned, and made
safe across the first four S's. This chapter, the FIRST of Part Seven, OPENS Standardize, the fifth S,
and does one turn: it names the quiet problem underneath a good home (almost all of it lives in one
person's head and hands, so it lasts only as long as their memory and energy and quietly drifts back
the day they are tired or away) and delivers the concept that answers it. It teaches the drift reframe
(a way that lives in one head is invisible, so everyone does it their own way and the best way is a
secret; the job of this S is to prevent reversion by making the good way visible and shared), delivers
the hero (a standard is a recipe, the best way you know today, written down plainly so it turns out
right for anyone, not bureaucracy but a gift, carried by the recipe reframe and the definition box),
delivers the living-standard heart (the word today, the current best word not the last word, rewritten
without ceremony the moment a better way is found, a starting point not a cage), walks the reader
through writing one plain standard and putting it where the work happens (with the shortcut of asking
the person who already does it best to teach it), and reads the friction meter as a HOLD with a new
catch behind the needle. Nothing of the earlier four S's is re-taught here. No checklist, schedule, or
responsibility system is built (Chapter 24), no visual or pictorial standards (Chapter 25), and no
long-term adherence or audit system (Sustain, a later S, named only as still to come). Keep the tone
calm, warm, and freeing: a home whose good ways live in one person's head is the normal case, not a
failing, and things drift back not because anyone is careless but because a way that lives in one head
is invisible. A standard is a recipe and a gift, never a cage, never corporate; the register is relief,
the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works, finally set down. Crucially, describe
only the calm, warm illustrated house style: no corporate office look, no clipboards or binders
presented as virtue, no one looking over a shoulder, no force or control framing (no enforce, comply,
lock down), no cages, no photoreal polish; a home standard is a warm recipe card on a real surface, not
a policy.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical
object in the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real sheet, or the reading on a real
gauge, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental hand-lettered label: use the
middot, for example `CHAPTER 23 · STANDARDIZE`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot between
words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: living-standard, standard-card, launch-pad, best-way,
thought-bubble, cupboard-door, recipe-card, anti-backslide, Safety-complete, Shine-complete,
Straighten-complete, Sort-complete, bedtime-routine, five-minute.

## 1. Knowledge moves from one head to the walls (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of one cared-for home shown twice, split gently by an idea rather
than a hard line, in soft daylight. On the left, a single person stands with a large, full
thought-bubble crammed with little pictures of how everything is done (a drawer, a plate, a clock)
while two family members stand nearby looking slightly unsure, each with a small question mark, because
all of it lives in that one head. A broad terracotta arrow, hand-lettered written down, leads to the
right, where the same home, the same rooms unchanged, now carries small slate standard cards at the
points where the work happens: a card taped inside a cupboard door, a card by the dishwasher, and a
card at the launch pad by the door. On the right the people are at ease and doing things, each with a
small soft green check, because the knowledge has come out of one head and gone onto the walls where
anyone can reach it. Warm paper cream and honey wood tones, ink-brown line work, slate for the cards,
soft green for the checks of ease, one terracotta for the written-down arrow and the question marks, no
warm-red anywhere. Editorial cookbook-style illustration. The same home, the same rooms, only now the
good ways live where everyone can reach them.

- Mood: a home that runs on one person's memory works beautifully right up until that person is tired
  or away, and then it quietly stops; the fix is not more effort but writing the best way down and
  putting it where the work happens, so the good you built stops depending on you; the register is
  relief, the weight of being the only keeper set down, not blame.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, a corporate or office look, floating typeset text
  overlays, any clipboard or binder presented as the answer, anyone being scolded or looking over a
  shoulder, a cage or lock, any warm-red accent, brand names or recognizable products; keep the left
  home warm but quietly fragile and the right home the same rooms unchanged, only now with small slate
  cards at the points of work and everyone at ease.

## 2. The living-standard loop (the best way we know today)

A warm editorial illustration of a friendly, calm loop of arrows, drawn by hand as a gentle open
circle rather than a rigid flowchart, with five stops turning clockwise, in soft daylight. Starting at
the top and going clockwise: the best way you know today; then write it down, the standard; then
everyone follows it; then someone finds a better way, drawn in a terracotta accent; then rewrite the
card; and the arrow curves back to a fresh the best way you know today. In the centre, a small
hand-written note reads a standard is meant to be improved. The stops are soft rounded cards in slate,
joined by gentle slate arrows, with one card and one arrow in terracotta at someone finds a better way,
because that is the stop that keeps the standard living. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line
work, slate for four stops and their arrows, one terracotta accent, no warm-red anywhere. A picture of
one plain idea: a standard is a stop on a loop, not the end of the road, and the word today keeps the
loop turning.

- Mood: a standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a
  better way you simply rewrite the card; the loop is open and turning, never closed and rigid; the
  word today is what keeps a standard from becoming a cage, so it captures improvement instead of
  freezing it out; calm, warm, quietly hopeful.
- Avoid: a boxed technical flowchart, a rigid closed loop that reads as a lock or a cage, gridlines
  everywhere, cold clinical light, floating typeset text, any clipboard or binder, more than five
  stops, any warm-red, brand names or recognizable products; keep the loop a gentle friendly circle
  with clear air between the stops, one terracotta accent on someone finds a better way, the register a
  good idea that stays open to a better one.

## 3. A sample standard card (the whole thing, once)

A warm editorial illustration of a single standard card, drawn by hand like a taped-up recipe card or a
note you would stick to a cupboard door, at a realistic size, in soft daylight. A small curl of tape
sits at the top-left corner. The plain title, in a warm honey accent, reads Loading the Dishwasher.
Below it are five short numbered steps in plain slate words: one, scrape, do not rinse; two, big items
along the sides and back; three, bowls and cups angled down on the top rack; four, knives point down;
five, run it when it is full, not before. A small line at the bottom reads Lives, inside the cabinet
above the dishwasher. Warm paper cream card, ink-brown line work, slate step numbers and text, one
honey accent on the title, soft rule hairlines, no warm-red anywhere. A picture of one plain idea: a
whole standard is small and light, a plain title, a handful of steps anyone can follow, and one line
saying where it lives.

- Mood: making a standard is much smaller than it sounds; capture the best way you already use, in as
  few plain steps as will do the job, and put it where the task happens; this is not a document, it is a
  recipe card that took about five minutes; warm, doable, unintimidating.
- Avoid: a formal form, a policy, a printed clinical document, a clipboard or binder, cold clinical
  light, more than five or six steps, legalistic or corporate wording, floating typeset overlays, any
  warm-red, brand names or recognizable products; keep the card small, warm, and hand-made, the steps
  few and plain, the curl of tape saying it lives on a real surface, the register a five-minute gift
  stuck in the right spot.

## 4. A recipe, not a rulebook (the reframe)

A calm, warm illustration of the chapter's central turn, drawn as two gentle facing panels, read left
to right as the two things the word standard can mean, in soft daylight. The first panel, what the word
sounds like: a cold, formal binder or clipboard, thick and closed and unread, drawn dim and
low-contrast and a little tired, the register of a rule nobody opens. The second panel, what a home
standard actually is: a warm recipe card on a kitchen surface with a few plain hand-written steps and a
curl of tape, a gift someone worked out and wrote down so you do not have to work it out from scratch,
drawn in full warm color. Between or beneath the two, a quiet hand-written line carries the reframe: a
recipe takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read. To
the right, a quieter, dimmer, unlit panel outline stands for the chapters where standards get their
tools, checklists and schedules and pictures, kept low-contrast and clearly ahead. Warm paper cream
background, ink-brown line work, soft slate for the tabs, the recipe panel warm and open and the binder
panel dim and closed, the right group dim and unlit, no warm-red anywhere. The image reads as one plain
idea: a standard is not bureaucracy, it is a recipe, a gift written down so the thing turns out right
for anyone.

- Mood: the word standard sounds cold, like rules and clipboards and someone checking whether you
  complied, but a home standard is the warm opposite, a recipe and a gift; put the cold picture out of
  your mind; the register is warmth and relief, not correction.
- Avoid: drawing the feared binder as villainous or the recipe as flimsy, any scolding or shaming tone,
  a cage or lock or someone looking over a shoulder, a corporate office look, teaching any next-chapter
  content in the dim right panel, any warm-red, floating typeset text, hype; keep the binder dim and
  closed and a little tired and the recipe card visibly warm and open and used, the register a gift
  rather than a compliance demand.

## 5. The needle holds, and stops sliding back (the friction meter)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge dialing
from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right. This chapter
the dark live needle does not move: it holds exactly at the Safety-complete position it reached last
chapter, resting on the fourth slate milestone tick, which is now itself a passed marker, with Sort
complete, Straighten complete, and Shine complete sitting further back as small passed slate markers.
What is new appears behind the needle rather than ahead of it: a small slate ratchet, a catch, holding
the needle against the pull back toward friction, and a faint dashed arrow pointing back toward the
friction side that is barred with a small red block, hand-marked no backslide, because the standard
holds. A slate crosshair labeled the goal, carrying its purpose flag, still sits further out on the
green calm side, clearly ahead of the live needle, with visible room left between them, because the
needle reaches the goal only at the very end and the rest of Standardize and Sustain remain. Warm paper
cream background, ink line work, consistent stroke weight, friendly but not childish.

- Mood: the needle does not move because nothing in the rooms changed; you wrote down a way you were
  already doing, so it would be dishonest to show a forward step; but for the first time the dial shows
  a catch behind the needle, a ratchet that keeps a gain from sliding back, and the backslide that used
  to be always possible is, for the first time, blocked; the needle did not advance, it stopped being
  able to retreat, and that is worth as much as a move; the goal is still ahead, because Standardize has
  only begun and Sustain follows.
- Avoid: the live needle advancing, moving forward, or reaching the goal crosshair, drawing this as a
  new milestone or a forward step, a completion glow or celebratory confetti, the passed markers or the
  dashed backslide arrow drawn solid or bright, no room left between the needle and the goal, hype, neon
  color, any sense of the journey being finished. The needle holds exactly where Safety left it, a new
  catch appears behind it, and clear room remains between it and the goal.
