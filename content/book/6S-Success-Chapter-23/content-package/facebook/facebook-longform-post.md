# Facebook Longform Post: The Whole House Lived in My Head. Here Is How I Got It Out.

A longer, story-driven post from Chapter 23 of 6S Success: Home Edition, the first chapter of Part Seven and the one that opens Standardize, the fifth S. It takes the home four S's made sorted, arranged, clean, and safe, names the quiet problem underneath it (almost all of it lives in one person's head, so it drifts back the day they are away), and delivers the concept that answers it: a standard is a recipe, the best way you know today written down, kept living by the word today. Conversational, like talking to a friend. A standard is framed as a gift and a relief, never corporate. Ends with a soft CTA. No em dashes. Body hard-capped 300 to 450 words.

---

For a long time I was quietly proud that I knew how everything in my house worked. Which drawer things go in and why. The best way to load the dishwasher. The order of the bedtime routine. The little trick that keeps mornings from falling apart. None of it was written down, because I never needed to write it down. I just knew it.

Then I was away for a few days, and the whole thing gently came undone. Not because anyone was careless, but because a way of doing something that lives in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way. That was the sentence that got me.

"Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you."

The word standard made me flinch. It sounds like rules and clipboards and someone checking whether you complied. But the book puts it a different way.

"A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day."

That I could do. So I wrote one. I watched myself load the dishwasher once and wrote the steps in plain words, five of them, and taped the card inside the cabinet right above it. It took about five minutes. Most home tasks come down to a handful of steps, and if you reach step eleven you are describing your own quirks, not the task.

The part I did not expect was how much it freed me. A standard is not a cage.

"A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony."

When my kid finds a faster way to fold laundry, the better way wins and I spend thirty seconds updating the card. Nothing in the rooms changed. What changed is that the good I had built finally stopped living only in my head.
