# Back Cover Copy: Standardize: The Best Way We Know Today

## Back-cover text

Four S's in, stand back at what they have built: a home that is sorted, arranged, kept clean on a light rhythm, and safe. It is a genuinely good home. Now notice the uncomfortable thing underneath it. Almost all of that lives in exactly one place, and that place is you. You know which drawer things go into and why, the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick that keeps mornings calm, and none of it was ever written down, because you simply know it. Which means the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, or away, or tired and out of patience, and then it quietly stops. That is the gap the fifth S closes. "Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you."

Chapter 23 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the first chapter of Part Seven, and it opens Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. It is not about adding rules or turning a warm home into an office with procedures; it is about taking the good ways you have already found and writing them down so they stop depending on you, and it is more of a relief than it sounds, because you finally get to set down the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works. Things drift back not because anyone is careless but because a way that lives only in one head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way. "It is just what happens when the best way is a secret." The answer is warm and simple, and the chapter takes the cold out of the word: "A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day." And the most important word in the title keeps it from becoming the cage people fear: "The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve." A standard is a starting point, not a finish line.

Then the chapter puts it in your hands. Pick one small recurring task that only really works when you do it, watch yourself do it once and write the best way in a few plain steps (most home tasks are three to six; reach step eleven and you are describing your personal quirks, not the task), and stick it where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible, so the next person meets it at the moment they need it. There is a lovely shortcut, too: "The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands," so if someone already does a task best, ask them to show you and write down what they do. Then go and look at the friction meter, and notice what it does not do. It does not move, because you changed nothing in the rooms. "The needle does not move this chapter, because nothing new was cleared. But for the first time, something appears behind it: a catch that keeps it from sliding back. Everything you built until now could quietly reverse; a written standard is the first thing that holds a gain in place. The goal is still ahead, and the rest of Standardize, and Sustain, are about making sure the needle, having come this far, stays." That is the whole rule of the chapter: "A standard is the best way you know today, written down." Four of the six S's are done, and Standardize has only begun.
