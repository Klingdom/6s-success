# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

Four S's in, stand back at what they have built: a home that is sorted, arranged, kept clean on a light rhythm, and safe. It is a genuinely good home. Now notice the uncomfortable thing underneath it. Almost all of that lives in exactly one place, and that place is you. You know which drawer things go into and why, the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick that keeps mornings calm, and none of it was ever written down, because you simply know it. Which means the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, or away, or tired and out of patience, and then it quietly stops. Chapter 23 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where you close that gap, and it is more of a relief than it sounds, because you finally get to set down the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works.

"Standardize: The Best Way We Know Today" is the first chapter of Part Seven, and it opens Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. Four S's have made the home sorted, arranged, clean, and safe; this chapter delivers the concept that makes all of it capable of lasting and the first written standard. It is not about adding rules or turning a warm home into an office with procedures, and it opens no new way to tidy and re-teaches none of the earlier S's; it takes the good ways you have already found and writes them down so they stop depending on you. As the chapter puts it, "Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you."

The chapter starts with why things drift back, because a quiet worry runs underneath the whole book: will it last? You sort a drawer and know that in three months it could be a mess again. That worry is an accurate reading of how homes behave. Things drift back not because anyone is lazy but because a way of doing something that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. You put the mixing bowls in the low cupboard for a good reason; someone who does not know the reason puts them back on the counter, undoing the system while genuinely trying to help. Multiply that by every task and every person, and the drift is not a moral failing; "It is just what happens when the best way is a secret." The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, gave this S a blunt job: prevent reversion back to the old way, not through willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared. "A standard is how you make the best way visible."

Then the chapter takes the cold out of the word, because "standard" sounds like rules, clipboards, and someone checking whether you complied, and a home standard is warmer and simpler than that. Think of an actual recipe on a card in a kitchen: not a rule anyone is enforcing, but a gift. Someone worked out how to make the thing turn out well and wrote it down, so you do not have to work it out from scratch, and it comes out right whether you are sharp or half asleep. That is precisely what a home standard does. "A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day." And the definition asks for nothing grand: "A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found." It does not ask for length, formality, or completeness; a good home standard is usually a few lines about one task. "The test of a standard is never how thorough it looks. It is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten."

Then comes the most important word in the title, and it keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people fear. The word is today. "The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve." This dissolves the healthy objection to standardizing anything at home, the fear of locking the household into one rigid way forever. "A standard is a starting point, not a finish line." It captures the best way you know as of today so you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced; when your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card. "Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick." Without a written standard, a good new idea gets used once and forgotten; with one, the improvement is captured and the next person starts from there. As the chapter puts it, "A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony."

Enough concept: making one is much smaller than it sounds, and doing one is the fastest way to feel how light a standard is. Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it, something small and ordinary you have quietly become the keeper of. Instead of inventing a best way, capture the one you already use: watch yourself do the task once and write the steps as you go, in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps; if you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task. Then, the part people skip, put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible; a dishwasher standard lives taped inside the cabinet above the dishwasher, because the whole value is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it. And there is a lovely shortcut: if someone in the home already does a task better than everyone else, ask them to show you and write down what they do. "The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands," and "A standard is often just the act of making one person's good habit into everyone's."

Then go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice what it does not do: it does not move. That is not a disappointment; it is the whole point of this S showing up honestly on the dial. Every chapter until now, the needle traveled because the home changed; this chapter changed nothing in the rooms, so the needle holds exactly where Safety left it. But look closer, because for the first time the dial shows something new, behind the needle rather than ahead of it. Until now, every gain the needle recorded was quietly reversible: a sorted room can re-clutter, a clean surface re-soils, a good habit lapses. Standardize is the first S whose job is not to move the needle forward but to keep it from sliding back, so a small catch appears behind the needle, a ratchet holding it against the pull toward friction, and the backslide that used to be always possible is, for the first time, blocked. "The needle did not advance today. It got something arguably more valuable: it stopped being able to retreat." As the chapter's rule has it, "A standard is the best way you know today, written down."

That is the fifth S in its smallest form, and it is the move that makes everything before it capable of lasting. What you have not done yet is the rest of it, and that is deliberate; one card on one cupboard door is a proof of concept, not a system. Turning a handful of standards into something the whole home runs on takes a few more tools, and those are the next chapters: Chapter 24 adds the checklists so nothing is forgotten, the schedules so recurring work has a time, and the responsibilities so every standard has a name, then Chapter 25 adds pictures and visual standards, and Sustain follows. For now, hold on to the one idea all of Standardize grows from, which is also the warmest way to think about it: "you are not writing rules, you are writing down the best way you know today, so that the good you have built can be followed by anyone and improved by everyone."
