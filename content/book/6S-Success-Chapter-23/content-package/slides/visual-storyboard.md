**Two fences to hold visually.** First, Chapter 23 OPENS Standardize by delivering the CONCEPT of a standard and ONE written standard; it does NOT build the checklist, schedule, and responsibility system (that is Chapter 24), the visual and pictorial standards (that is Chapter 25), or the long-term adherence and audit system (that is Sustain, Chapters 26 to 30). ## Beat 3 · Why things drift back

Why removal and rhythm alone do not hold: a good way that lives only in one head is invisible, so everyone does it their own way, and the best way is a secret. The blunt job of this S is to prevent reversion by making the best way visible.

- Composition: two calm panels on panel #FBF7EF. On the LEFT, headed "A secret," the best way locked inside one head while everyone else quietly does it their own way, one or two soft spark red #CB4B36 drift marks as the system comes gently undone by people genuinely trying to help (the mixing bowls put back on the counter by someone who does not know the reason they belong in the low cupboard). On the RIGHT, headed "Visible," the same best way written on a slate #3C5A6B card on the wall, so everyone does the one good way.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the panel frames and the card, a sparing spark red #CB4B36 on the drift marks. Calm and legible; no verdict scolding, because the drift is not a moral failing.
- Caption, from the book: "It is just what happens when the best way is a secret."
- Point: things drift back not because anyone is lazy but because a way that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. Multiply that by every task and every person, and the drift is just what happens when the best way is a secret. The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, gave this S a blunt job: prevent reversion back to the old way, not through willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared. A standard is how you make the best way visible.

## Beat 4 · A standard is a recipe

The hero device made visual: a standard is not bureaucracy, it is a recipe, the best way you know today, written down plainly so it turns out right for anyone, measured by use and not by thoroughness.

- Composition: a definition card on panel #FBF7EF, the frozen definition set large in Fraunces with slate #3C5A6B rules. Beside it, a warm recipe card on a kitchen counter with a curl of tape at one corner, a honey #DDA63A title, plain slate #3C5A6B steps, drawn plainly as a gift rather than a rulebook. Three small slate #3C5A6B tags name what a standard asks for, "current best way," "written plainly," "anyone can follow," and one faded soft #6A625A tag is crossed off, "long, formal, complete."
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the frame and the tags, honey #DDA63A on the recipe-card title, terracotta #BC4B2A accent on the word recipe. Calm; a gift, not a rulebook, and nothing corporate.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "a gift, measured by use, not by thoroughness."
- Point: the word "standard" sounds cold, like rules, clipboards, and someone checking whether you complied. Put all of that out of your mind. Think of an actual recipe on a card in a kitchen: not a rule anyone is enforcing, but a gift. A recipe takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read, and that is precisely what a home standard does. It does not ask for length, formality, or completeness; a good home standard is usually a few lines about one task. The test is never how thorough it looks; it is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten.
- The frozen recipe reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day." The frozen definition box sits in Fraunces on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found." The line "The test of a standard is never how thorough it looks. It is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten." may run beneath in Fraunces.

## Beat 5 · The best way we know today

The living-standard heart: the word today, which keeps a standard from becoming the cage people fear. A standard is a starting point, not a finish line, the current best word, rewritten without ceremony the moment a better way is found.

- Composition: the today reframe set large in Fraunces on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules, the word today drawn in terracotta #BC4B2A. Beside it, a standard card written in pencil with a small eraser resting on it, ready to be improved, not carved in stone. A small before-and-after: a card with the old fold-the-laundry way, and the same card rewritten in thirty seconds when the teenager finds a faster one.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the card and the frame, terracotta #BC4B2A on the word today, soft #6A625A on the pencil and eraser. Warm; a starting point, not a cage.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "written in pencil, meant to be improved."
- Point: there is one more word in the title, and it is the most important, because it keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people fear. A standard is the opposite of a rigid rule forever; it captures the best way you know as of today so you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced the moment someone finds something better. When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the standard does not win by being written down; the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card. Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick.
- The frozen today reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve." The frozen living-standard line sits as a Fraunces box beneath: "A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony." The lines "A standard is a starting point, not a finish line." and "Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick." may run small.

## Beat 6 · The living-standard loop

The supporting device that makes the word today visual: a standard is a stop on a loop, not the end of the road, and the loop is what keeps a standard alive instead of freezing it into a cage.

- Composition: a simple, friendly loop diagram on panel #FBF7EF, a gentle circle of slate #3C5A6B arrows, not a rigid flowchart. Stops around the loop: "The best way you know today" leads to "Write it down (the standard)" leads to "Everyone follows it" leads to "Someone finds a better way" leads to "Rewrite the card" and curves back to a fresh "The best way you know today." A small note in the centre reads "a standard is meant to be improved."
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the arrows and the stops, one terracotta #BC4B2A accent on "Someone finds a better way," soft #6A625A on the centre note. Calm; a loop, not a straight line, and never a rigid flowchart.
- Caption, from the book: "A standard is a stop on a loop, not the end of the road."
- Point: hold the whole idea as a loop, not a straight line, because that is what the word today buys you. You write down the best way you know today; everyone follows it; someone finds a better way; you rewrite the card; and you are back at a fresh best way you know today. The loop is what keeps a standard alive: without it, a written way slowly becomes a rule nobody is allowed to question; with it, every improvement anyone finds gets captured instead of frozen out. A cage is a way held in place after a better one is found; a living standard is the same card, rewritten without ceremony the moment the better way appears.
- Frozen figure caption, verbatim: "A standard is a stop on a loop, not the end of the road. You write down the best way you know today, everyone follows it, someone finds a better way, and you rewrite the card. The word "today" is what keeps the loop turning, so a standard captures improvement instead of freezing it out."

## Beat 7 · The sample standard card

The second supporting device, the whole craft in one object: a single warm standard card, a plain title, a handful of steps, and one line saying where it lives, made in about five minutes.

- Composition: a single warm "standard card" at a realistic size, drawn like a recipe card or a note you would tape up. A plain honey #DDA63A title at the top ("Loading the Dishwasher"), four or five short numbered steps in plain slate #3C5A6B language (1. Scrape, do not rinse. 2. Big items along the sides and back. 3. Bowls and cups angled down on the top rack. 4. Knives point down. 5. Run it when it is full, not before.), and a small line at the foot reading "Lives: inside the cabinet above the dishwasher." A little curled piece of tape at one corner.
- Palette: warm paper card, slate #3C5A6B text, one honey #DDA63A accent on the title, rule #D9CDB8 on the tape hairline. Calm and legible; this is not a document, it is a recipe card for a recurring task.
- Point: making a standard is much smaller than it sounds. Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it, not the hardest thing in the home but the opposite. Instead of inventing a best way, capture the one you already use: watch yourself do the task once and write the steps in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps; if you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task. Then put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder, so the next person meets it at the moment they need it.
- Frozen figure caption, verbatim: "A whole standard, start to finish. A plain title, a handful of steps anyone can follow, and one line saying where it lives. This is not a document; it is a recipe card for a recurring task, and it took about five minutes to make. That is the entire craft of standardizing, done once." The Quick Win sits in a green #6E8B5B bordered callout, verbatim: "Pick one recurring thing that only really works when you do it, loading the dishwasher, the bedtime routine, taking out the recycling, and write down the best way in five plain steps. Stick it where the task happens. One standard, today." The 6S Tip sits in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Write the standard where the work happens, not in a binder. ## Beat 9 · The best way already lives in someone's hands

The warmest reframe of the fifth S, given the Family Challenge treatment: you are not imposing a way, you are honouring the best way already in the house and making one person's good habit into everyone's.

- Composition: a warm vignette on panel #FBF7EF: one family member showing another how they do a task well, a green #6E8B5B good-habit mark on their hands, while a second person writes it onto a slate #3C5A6B standard card. The good habit flows from one pair of hands to a card everyone can reach.
- Palette: paper ground, green #6E8B5B on the good habit and the hands, slate #3C5A6B on the card, terracotta #BC4B2A accent on the phrase everyone's. Warm; noticing and sharing, not authority handing down rules.
- Caption, from the book: "The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands."
- Point: there is a lovely shortcut. You do not always have to work out the best way yourself, because often it already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands. If someone in the home already loads the dishwasher so it always comes out clean, or gets the toddler down without a fight, do not invent a standard from scratch; ask them to show you, and write down what they do. Held this way, standardizing is not an act of authority handing down rules; it is an act of noticing who already does a thing well and making their good habit available to everyone. That reframe takes any last trace of the corporate out of the fifth S.
