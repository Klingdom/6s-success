### Slide 2 · The good you built lives in one place, and that place is you

- Stand back and look at what four S's have built: a home cleared, arranged, kept clean on a light rhythm, and made safe. It is a genuinely good home. Now notice something uncomfortable: almost all of it lives in exactly one place, and that place is you. You know which drawer things go into and why, the best way to load the dishwasher, the order of the bedtime routine, the trick with the launch pad. Nobody wrote it down, because you did not need to; you simply know it.
- Which means the home works beautifully right up until the day you are sick, or away, or tired and out of patience, and then it quietly stops working. That is the gap the fifth S closes. Standardize is not about adding rules or turning a warm home into an office with procedures; it is about taking the good ways you have already found and writing them down so they stop depending on you.
- It is more of a relief than it sounds, because you finally get to set down the weight of being the only person who knows how the home works. Verbatim: "It is the difference between a home that holds its shape and one that slowly slides back the moment your attention moves elsewhere."

### Slide 4 · Why things drift back

- A quiet worry runs underneath the whole book: will it last? You sort a drawer and know that in three months it could be a mess again. That worry is an accurate reading of how homes behave, and it is what this S is built to answer. Things drift back not because anyone is lazy but because a way of doing something that lives only in one person's head is invisible to everyone else, so everyone else does it their own way, and their own way is rarely the way you worked out. You put the mixing bowls in the low cupboard for a good reason; someone who does not know the reason puts them back on the counter, undoing the system while genuinely trying to help.
- Multiply that by every task and every person, and the drift is not a moral failing. Verbatim: "It is just what happens when the best way is a secret." The people who first named these steps, on the factory floors of Toyota, gave this S a blunt job: prevent reversion back to the old way, not through willpower or nagging but by making the good way visible and shared. Verbatim: "A standard is how you make the best way visible."
- The drift reframe, frozen: "Everything you have built so far lives in your head and your hands, which means it lasts exactly as long as your memory and your energy do. A standard is how a good way of doing something stops depending on you."

### Slide 5 · A standard is a recipe

- Say plainly what a standard is, because the word itself does the chapter a disservice. "Standard" sounds cold, like rules, clipboards, and someone checking whether you complied. Put all of that out of your mind, because a home standard is warmer and simpler. Think about an actual recipe on a card in a kitchen: not a rule anyone is enforcing, but a gift. Someone worked out how to make the thing turn out well and wrote it down, so you do not have to work it out from scratch and it comes out right whether you are sharp or half asleep. A recipe takes skill that lived in one person's hands and makes it available to anyone who can read, and that is precisely what a home standard does for the recurring tasks of a home.
- Notice what the definition asks for and what it does not: the way should be the best you currently know, written plainly, and followable by anyone; it does not ask for length, formality, or completeness. A good home standard is usually a few lines about one task. Verbatim: "The test of a standard is never how thorough it looks. It is whether the next person actually follows it and gets the result you would have gotten."
- The recipe reframe, frozen: "A standard is not bureaucracy. It is a recipe: the best way you know to make something turn out right, written down so it turns out right every time, for anyone, even on a tired day." Definition box, frozen: "A standard is the current best way to do a recurring task, written down plainly enough that anyone in the home can follow it and get the same result, and kept open enough that it is rewritten the moment a better way is found."

### Slide 6 · The best way we know today

- There is one more word in the title, and it is the most important, because it keeps a standard from becoming the very cage people fear. The word is today. This dissolves the healthy objection to standardizing anything at home: nobody wants to lock the household into one rigid way forever. A standard is the opposite of that, a starting point, not a finish line. It captures the best way you know as of today so you are not forever reinventing it, and it stays fully open to being replaced the moment someone finds something better.
- When your teenager finds a faster way to fold the laundry, the standard does not win by virtue of being written down; the better way wins, and you spend thirty seconds updating the card. Verbatim: "A standard is a starting point, not a finish line." Verbatim: "Held that way, a standard is not a constraint on improvement; it is the thing that makes improvement stick." Without a written standard, a good new idea gets used once and forgotten; with one, the improvement is captured and the next person starts from there. It is written in pencil, so to speak, meant to be improved.
- The today reframe, frozen, the living-standard heart: "The word in the title doing the quiet work is today. A standard is not the last word, it is the current best word, and the moment someone finds a better way, the standard is not broken. It is simply what you improve." The living-standard line, frozen: "A standard is not a rule carved in stone. It is the best way you have found so far, held in place only until you find a better one, and then rewritten without ceremony."

### Slide 7 · The living-standard loop

- Hold the whole idea as a loop, not a straight line, because that is what the word today buys you. A standard is a stop on a loop, not the end of the road. You write down the best way you know today; everyone follows it; someone finds a better way; you rewrite the card; and you are back at a fresh best way you know today. The loop is what keeps a standard alive: without it, a written way slowly becomes a rule nobody is allowed to question; with it, every improvement anyone finds gets captured instead of frozen out.
- This is the difference between a standard that helps and a standard that cages. A cage is a way held in place after a better one is found. A living standard is the same card, rewritten without ceremony the moment the better way appears. The word today is the hinge the whole loop turns on.

### Slide 8 · Write your first standard

- Enough concept: making one is much smaller than it sounds, and doing one is the fastest way to feel how light a standard is. Pick a single recurring task that mostly works only when you do it, not the hardest thing in the home but the opposite, something small and ordinary you have quietly become the keeper of (loading the dishwasher, the morning launch-pad routine, taking out the recycling). Instead of inventing a best way, capture the one you already use: watch yourself do the task once and write the steps as you go, in plain words, as few as will do the job. Most home tasks come down to three to six steps; if you reach step eleven, you are describing your personal quirks, not the task.
- Then, the part people skip, put the standard where the task happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible. A dishwasher standard lives taped inside the cabinet above the dishwasher. The whole value is that the next person meets it at the moment they need it, and a perfect document nobody opens is worth less than three scrappy lines stuck in the right spot.
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Pick one recurring thing that only really works when you do it, loading the dishwasher, the bedtime routine, taking out the recycling, and write down the best way in five plain steps. Stick it where the task happens. One standard, today." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Write the standard where the work happens, not in a binder. A note taped inside the cupboard door beats a perfect document nobody opens. The best standard is the one you cannot avoid seeing."

### Slide 9 · The best way already lives in someone's hands

- There is a lovely shortcut hiding in all of this. You do not always have to work out the best way yourself, because often it already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands. If someone in the home already loads the dishwasher so it always comes out clean, or gets the toddler down without a fight, do not invent a standard from scratch; ask them to show you, and write down what they do. Verbatim: "The best way usually already exists, fully formed, in somebody's hands."
- Held this way, standardizing is not an act of authority handing down rules; it is an act of noticing who already does a thing well and making their good habit available to everyone. Verbatim: "A standard is often just the act of making one person's good habit into everyone's." That reframe matters, because it takes any last trace of the corporate out of the fifth S: you are not imposing a way, you are honouring the best way that is already in the house and sharing it.
- Family Challenge, verbatim: "Ask the person who does a task best to teach it to everyone else, and write down what they do. The best way usually already lives in someone's hands; a standard just makes it everyone's."

### Slide 12 · One card is a proof, not a system

- Be honest about the scale of what you have and have not built. Turning a handful of standards into something the whole home runs on takes a few more tools: a way to make sure recurring work actually happens and nothing is forgotten, a sense of when things get done and by whom, and, for the tasks where a picture beats a paragraph, a way to show the right result rather than describe it. Those are the next two chapters. This one deliberately stops at the concept and one written card, so the idea lands clean before the tools arrive.
- The one thing to guard against is the very failure the word today exists to prevent: writing a standard as a strict rule and then defending it forever. Keep it the best way for now, and let anyone propose a better one. A standard held open stays a gift; a standard defended becomes a cage.
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Writing a standard as a strict rule and then defending it forever. A standard you are not allowed to change becomes a cage, and people quietly route around a cage. Keep it the best way for now, and let anyone propose a better one."
