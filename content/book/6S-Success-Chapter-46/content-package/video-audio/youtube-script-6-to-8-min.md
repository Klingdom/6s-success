# Chapter 46 YouTube Script: The Workshop

*Six to eight minutes.*

---

**[Cold open, at the door of a workshop]**

This is the room 6S came from. Everything in this book was invented somewhere like this, and it is the only room where a bad standard can cost you a finger rather than an afternoon.

**[Title]**

**[Section 1: the homecoming]**

The traced outlines, the labels, the photograph of the ideal state: none of that was invented for pantries. It came off shop floors, where a tool out of place was expensive and an outline on a board was how you knew a chisel was loose somewhere in the building.

So this is a homecoming. And the stakes here are genuinely different. Every other room in this book fails into inconvenience. A drifted pantry costs you a duplicate jar. This room fails into injury.

**[Section 2: the smallest zone leads]**

Which changes the order of work, and this is the only room in the book where it is true: you start at the smallest zone.

The PPE station. A board of hooks by the door, under an hour of work, and it goes first because everything else in here involves a blade, a solvent, or a cloud of fine dust.

Do it the other way round and you know what happens, because you have done it. Working without the muffs because they are on the far wall. Cutting with hazed glasses pushed up on your forehead.

The PPE station is not the reward at the end. It is the permission to begin.

**[Section 3: vacuum, never sweep]**

One cleaning instruction that overrides habit.

A broom does not remove the finest sawdust. It launches it, back into the air where it hangs, and then it settles onto your wet finish and into your lungs. And the fines are the fraction that actually burns.

So: vacuum. Suck the dog holes rather than brushing them. And empty the extractor at the end of a session, not the start of the next one, when you are keen to get going and will not bother.

**[Section 4: the three verdicts]**

Now the part that decides whether your shop actually changes.

The mess in a workshop is never really about storage. It is three verdicts you keep postponing: on offcuts, on half-finished projects, and on paint.

And you know which ones you avoid, because here is the pattern: you will sort the fasteners twice before you touch the offcut pile once. Tipping out a jar of screws feels like progress. Putting good wood in the fire bucket feels like waste.

So the drawers get neat, repeatedly, and the pile actually holding the room back does not move.

The offcut pile has a fix that works because it removes your judgment. Two limits: a size floor, anything shorter than your forearm, and one bin with no overflow. When the bin is full, the shortest piece leaves. You are not judging whether a piece is good wood. You are judging whether it beats the worst piece already in there.

**[Section 5: the guard]**

One safety point worth the whole video. A blade guard is a spring-loaded part living in sawdust, and sawdust makes springs sticky. It degrades slowly, silently, and the first time you notice is the time it does not come back.

Thumb-test it every time you clean that zone, tool unplugged. If it hesitates at all, that tool is out of service.

**[Close]**

Six zones, six hard calls, including the visitor rule, which is the only decision in the book about a person rather than an object.

Do one zone this week, and make it the PPE station. Chapter 46. Next: the mudroom.
