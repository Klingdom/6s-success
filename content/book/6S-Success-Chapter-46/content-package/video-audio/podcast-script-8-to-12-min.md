# Chapter 46 Podcast Script: The Workshop

*Eight to twelve minutes. Conversational, unhurried.*

---

We have come a long way around to get here, and I want to say it plainly before anything else.

This is the room 6S came from.

Everything we have been doing for forty-five chapters, the traced outlines, the labels, the photograph of the ideal state, the whole idea that a place should tell you at a glance whether something is missing: none of that was invented for pantries. It was invented on shop floors, by people for whom a tool out of place was not untidy but expensive, and for whom an outline traced on a board was a way of knowing that a chisel was loose somewhere in the building.

So this chapter is a homecoming. And it is also the one chapter where the stakes are genuinely different.

Every other room in this book fails into inconvenience. A drifted pantry costs you a duplicate jar of paprika. A drifted laundry room costs you a second tumble. This room fails into injury.

A guard that has stopped springing shut. A blade stored flat in a drawer where your hand goes in first. A rag balled up in a bin. Those are not tidiness problems wearing a safety costume. They are the reason the discipline exists at all.

Which changes the order of work, and this is the only room in the book where this is true: you start at the smallest zone.

The PPE station. It is a board of hooks by the door, it takes under an hour, and it goes first because everything else in that room involves a blade, a solvent, or a cloud of fine dust. You want your glasses findable and your extinguisher gauge green before you spend a Saturday handling any of it.

Do it the other way round and you know exactly what happens, because you have done it. You work without the muffs because they are on the far wall past the bench. You cut with hazed glasses pushed up on your forehead. You tell yourself you will find the dust mask before the sanding starts, and then you do not.

The PPE station is not the reward at the end of the clean. It is the permission to begin.

Now, one cleaning instruction that overrides habit, and it is worth changing your practice over.

Vacuum. Never sweep.

A broom does not remove the finest sawdust. It launches it. Back into the air, where it hangs for a while, and then settles: onto wet finish, so your topcoat is gritty, and into your lungs, which is the part that matters more. And the fines are the fraction that actually burns, which is why a shop with dust hanging in the air is a categorically different room from a shop with dust on the floor.

So the vacuum comes out instead of the broom, the dog holes get sucked rather than brushed, and the extractor gets emptied at the end of a session rather than at the start of the next one, when you are keen to get going and will not bother.

And now the part that actually decides whether your workshop changes, because I suspect this is the one that will land.

The mess in a workshop is never really about storage.

It is three verdicts you keep postponing. On offcuts. On half-finished projects. And on paint.

That is it. That is the whole diagnosis. And you already know which ones you avoid, because here is the pattern in every shop I have ever seen: you will sort the fasteners twice before you touch the offcut pile once.

Why? Because tipping out a jar of screws feels like progress. It is sortable, it is satisfying, it has a right answer. Whereas putting good wood in the fire bucket feels like waste, and the half-finished project feels like admitting something, and the paint feels like it might be needed for a touch-up one day.

So the drawers get neat. Repeatedly. And the pile that is actually holding the room back stays exactly where it was.

The zones you keep skipping are the zones holding the room back. If you take one sentence from this chapter, take that one.

The offcut pile in particular has a fix that works, and it works because it takes the judgment away from you. Set two limits. A size floor, anything shorter than your forearm or narrower than your palm. And a bin cap, one bin and no overflow. When the bin is full, the shortest piece in it leaves to make room for the newest.

You are not judging whether a piece is good wood. Every piece in there is good wood, that is the problem. You are only judging whether it is better than the worst piece already in the bin.

Two more quick things.

The blade guard. It is a spring-loaded part that lives in sawdust, and sawdust is exactly what makes a spring sticky. So it degrades slowly, it degrades silently, and the first time you notice is the time it does not come back. Work it with your thumb every time you clean that zone, on an unplugged tool. If it hesitates at all, the tool is out of service until it does not.

And the visitor rule, which is the only decision in this whole book that is about a person rather than an object. Your brother-in-law or your fourteen year old wanders in while the saw is running and wants to help. You have been handling that case by case, which means you have been handling it badly. Decide it once, and hang it on the wall: anyone who works in this shop has their own labelled set of glasses and muffs, there is one spare set on a hook marked for visitors, and if a visitor will not wear it, they stay on the near side of the doorway and watch.

Saying that once, in advance, is far easier than saying it while holding a running grinder.

Do one zone this week, and make it the PPE station.
