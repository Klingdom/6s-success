# Chapter 46 LinkedIn Posts: The Workshop

Ten standalone posts, each under 150 words. Product types only, no brands.

## 1. The room 6S came from
Everything in this book started here. The traced outlines, the labels, the photograph of the ideal state: all of it was invented on a shop floor by people for whom a missing tool was a stopped line and a sticky guard was a lost hand.

Which makes this chapter a homecoming, and the one chapter where the stakes are genuinely different.

## 2. Inconvenience against injury
Every other room in this book fails into inconvenience. A drifted pantry costs a duplicate jar. A drifted laundry room costs a second tumble. This room fails into injury. That is not a safety costume worn over a tidiness problem. It is the reason the discipline exists at all.

## 3. The smallest zone goes first
The PPE station is the smallest zone in a workshop, and it is the one you do first. Everything else in there involves a blade, a solvent, or a cloud of fine dust, so you want your glasses findable before you spend a Saturday handling any of it.

The PPE station is not the reward at the end of the clean. It is the permission to begin.

## 4. Vacuum, never sweep
A broom does not remove the finest sawdust. It launches it, back into the air where it hangs, then settles onto wet finish and into your lungs. And the fines are the fraction that actually burns.

So the vacuum comes out instead of the broom, and the extractor gets emptied at the end of a session rather than the start of the next one.

## 5. Three verdicts, not a storage problem
A workshop's mess is never really about storage. It is three verdicts you keep postponing: on offcuts, on half-finished projects, and on paint.

## 6. The zone you keep skipping
You will sort the fasteners twice before you touch the offcut pile once. Tipping out a jar of screws feels like progress. Putting good wood in the fire bucket feels like waste. So the drawers get neat while the pile actually holding the room back stays exactly where it was.

The zones you keep skipping are the zones holding the room back.

## 7. The offcut bin, settled by limits
The offcut pile is the hardest decision in a workshop, because every piece in it is genuinely usable. So stop judging pieces and set two limits instead: a size floor, anything shorter than your forearm, and one bin with no overflow. When the bin is full, the shortest piece leaves.

You are only ever judging whether a piece is better than the worst piece already in the bin.

## 8. The guard that degrades silently
A blade guard is a spring-loaded part living in sawdust, and sawdust is exactly what makes a spring sticky. It degrades slowly, it degrades silently, and the first time you notice is the time it does not come back.

Work it with your thumb every time you clean that zone. If it hesitates at all, the tool is out of service.

## 9. The visitor rule
Your brother-in-law wanders in while the saw is running and wants to help. You have been deciding this case by case, which means badly. Decide it once and hang it on the wall: own labelled set for anyone who works here, one spare set for visitors, and the near side of the doorway for anyone who will not wear it.

Saying that in advance is far easier than saying it while holding a running grinder.

## 10. Start with the smallest zone
"A workshop is not tidy because you sorted the screws. It is safe because you took the three verdicts you had been avoiding."
