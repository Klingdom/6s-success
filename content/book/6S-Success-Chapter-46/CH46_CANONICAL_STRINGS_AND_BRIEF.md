## Six zones (start at the Safety and PPE Station, the smallest zone in the room)
1. **Safety and PPE Station** - gear so easy to reach that putting it on is faster than skipping it; done first because it sets the terms for the other five.
2. **Main Workbench** - the one surface where a project actually gets built; clean it to read it.
3. **Power Tool Storage** - every tool guarded, complete, and ready to pick up; the dust these tools breathe is the dust that shortens them.
4. **Fastener and Hardware Zone** - one size per compartment with a sample glued to the face; read the corners while the drawers are empty.
5. **Material Rack** - the wood and metal you will genuinely use, stored so it stays straight and falls on nobody; the mess hides behind the rack.
6. **Finishing and Chemical Zone** - paint, glue, stain and solvent stored so they stay usable and never start a fire.

## FROZEN STRINGS (byte-verbatim in manuscript AND final.html)

### Six zones
The workshop is six small zones, not one big job: the safety and PPE station, the main workbench, the power tool storage, the fastener and hardware zone, the material rack, and the finishing and chemical zone. Do them one at a time, and start at the safety and PPE station even though it is the smallest zone in the room, because it sets the terms for the other five: once the glasses hang by the door and the extinguisher gauge reads green, you can work through the blades and the solvents without improvising.

### The rule (the room 6S came from)
This is the room 6S came from, and the only one in the house where a bad standard can cost you a finger rather than an afternoon. Everywhere else in this book, a standard that slips costs you time, or a smell, or a morning putting things right. Here it costs you a hand. So the rules in this room are not preferences and they are not tidiness: a traced outline is how you know a chisel is loose in the room, a guard that springs back is the difference between a cut and an injury, and the glasses on the hook by the door are the reason you will still be doing this in twenty years.

### The definition (defbox)
A finished workshop is one you can walk into and start cutting in inside a minute: safety glasses and muffs on labelled hooks at the door you come in through, an extinguisher with its gauge in the green and clear floor in front of it, a bare bench holding only the vise and the task lamp, every chisel and square back on its traced outline, each tool stored guarded with the accessories that make it usable, one fastener size per compartment with a sample glued to the face, boards flat and supported with the heaviest at the bottom, and every finish in its original labelled container inside a closed metal cabinet.

### Start-at-the-PPE reframe (the zone that sets the terms)
The smallest zone in this room goes first, and it is the only room in the book where that is true. Everything else in here involves a blade, a solvent, or a cloud of fine dust, so you want your glasses findable and your extinguisher gauge green before you spend a Saturday handling any of it. Do it the other way round and you spend the whole day improvising: working without muffs because they are on the far wall, cutting with hazed glasses pushed up on your forehead, telling yourself you will find the mask before the sanding starts. The PPE station is not the reward at the end of the clean. It is the permission to begin.

### Vacuum-never-sweep reframe (the fines are what matter)
There is one cleaning instruction in this room that overrides habit, and it is that you vacuum rather than sweep. A broom does not remove the finest sawdust, it launches it: back into the air, where it hangs, then settles onto wet finish and into your lungs. And the fines are the fraction that actually burns, which is why a shop full of airborne dust is a different kind of room from a shop with dust on the floor. So the vacuum comes out instead of the broom, the dog holes get sucked rather than brushed, and the extractor gets emptied at the end of the session rather than the start of the next one.

### Three-verdicts reframe (the zones you keep skipping)
The workshop's mess is never really about storage. It is three verdicts you keep postponing: on offcuts, on half-finished projects, and on paint. And you already know which ones you avoid, because you will sort the fasteners twice before you touch the offcut pile once. Tipping out a jar of screws feels like progress and putting good wood in the fire bucket feels like waste, so the drawers get neat while the pile that is actually holding the room back stays exactly where it was. The zones you keep skipping are the zones holding the room back, and that is the whole diagnosis.

### Safety reframe (the room where the stakes are physical)
Burn or fire is the question this room answers first, and its sharpest answer is a rag: a cloth soaked in oil finish generates its own heat as it cures and can ignite with nothing touching it, so it goes in a lidded metal can or gets spread flat outdoors to dry. Fall, cut, or crush is the guard that has stopped springing shut on its own, the chisel lying edge up in an open tray, the loose blade stored flat in a drawer where your hand goes in first, and the sheet of ply leaning at too shallow an angle. Water and electricity is cracked cord sheathing at the strain relief, used while you stand on damp concrete. And poison is thinner decanted into a drink bottle, or solvent finish brushed in a closed shop with the door shut.

### Before-and-after caption
This is the workshop the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, the glasses and muffs on labelled hooks at the door, the extinguisher gauge in the green with clear floor in front of it, a bare bench holding the vise and the lamp, every outline on the tool wall filled, the offcut bin at its written size floor, and every finish sealed in its labelled container behind a metal door. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then hang it where you stand, because in this room the standard is not about how it looks. It is about whether you can start work in a minute and still have ten fingers at the end of it.

### One Idea to Keep
A workshop is not tidy because you sorted the screws. It is safe because you took the three verdicts you had been avoiding, on the offcuts, the half-finished projects, and the paint, and because the gear you need to begin is hanging where you walk in. Do one zone this week, and make it the PPE station, because it is the smallest zone in the room and it is the one that gives you permission to do the other five.

## Four callouts (frozen, budgeted ~260 words total)
- **Quick Win (win)**: Do the PPE station first. It is the smallest zone in the room and it takes under an hour: wash the lenses in soap and water, wipe the muff cushions, check the extinguisher gauge reads green with clear floor in front of it, and hang everything on labelled hooks at the door you actually walk in through.
- **6S Tip (tip)**: Vacuum, never sweep. A broom launches the finest sawdust back into the air, where it settles onto wet finish and into your lungs, and the fines are the part that actually burns. Empty the extractor at the end of every session rather than the start of the next one.
- **The Skipped Zone Challenge (family)**: Name the zone you have been avoiding. It will be the offcuts, the half-finished project, or the paint, because those are verdicts rather than tidying. Do that one next, not the fasteners again.
- **Common Mistake (mistake)**: Sorting the fasteners twice before touching the offcut pile once; sweeping a shop rather than vacuuming it; and leaving the PPE station until last, so the whole day is spent improvising around gear you cannot find.

## The calls that matter here
The project that has been on the bench since spring (say out loud what the next physical action is and what date this month you will do it, and if you cannot name both in one breath the project is finished, so take it apart, return the hardware to the drawers and the stock to the rack, because keeping it assembled is not keeping the project alive, it is only keeping the surface unusable); the expensive tool you never use (two questions settle it, has it done work in the last twenty-four months, and if the job came up tomorrow would you happily hire one for a day instead, because a tool losing value on your shelf is not an asset you are holding, it is a decision you are storing); the inherited jar of mixed hardware (price the alternative first, since a full box of the screws you actually drive costs less than the evening you would spend sorting, so give it one sitting, keep only the sizes you already have a compartment for, and whatever is left goes to metal recycling that same day, never tipped back into a smaller jar); the offcut that is still good wood (the hardest decision in the workshop, settled by two limits rather than judgment: a size floor, anything shorter than your forearm or narrower than your palm, and a bin cap of one bin with no overflow, so when the bin is full the shortest piece leaves to make room for the newest, and you are only ever judging whether a piece is better than the worst piece already in the bin); the wall of half-used paint (keep only colours currently on a surface in this house, and keep them properly, one small sealed jar of each labelled with the room and the finish rather than the original half-empty tin sitting open to the air, with everything else going to hazardous waste the same day you sort it, because a box of condemned paint by the door for a month is the same problem relocated); the visitor rule (decide it once and hang it on the wall rather than case by case: anyone who works in this shop has their own labelled set of glasses and muffs, there is one spare set on a hook marked for visitors, and if a visitor will not wear it they stay on the near side of the doorway and watch, because saying that once in advance is far easier than saying it while holding a running grinder).

## Watch for in the workshop (five safety questions)
- **Burn or fire**: oil finish rags balled up in a bin liner or draped over the bench, which generate their own heat as they cure and can ignite with nothing touching them; solvent stored within reach of a water heater flame or any pilot light; lithium battery packs left on chargers indefinitely, especially on a wooden shelf under a bench full of sawdust; a dry offcut pile stacked against the wall near a heater or an extension lead; an extinguisher with its gauge out of the green, no inspection date on it, or timber leaning in front of it.
- **Fall, cut, or crush**: a blade guard that has stopped springing shut on its own, which is the injury waiting in this room; chisels and marking knives lying edge up in an open tray; loose blades stored flat in a drawer where your hand goes in first; a vise whose jaws have worn smooth enough to release a workpiece mid-cut; sheet goods sliding off a shallow lean, and long boards pulled from the middle of a stack bringing the ones above onto your feet; a loaded small-parts cabinet pulling off the wall; safety glasses so scratched that you take them off to see the cut line, which is the moment the chip flies.
- **Water and electricity**: cracked cord sheathing at the strain relief, used on an unearthed outlet or while standing on bare damp concrete; the task lamp or a tool cord running across the bench or down onto a damp floor where you stand to work.
- **Poison, choke, or strangle**: thinner or turpentine decanted into an unlabelled drink bottle; spraying or brushing solvent based finish in a closed shop with the door shut; loose screws, washers, and wall anchors on the floor at the height where a toddler or a dog finds them first.
- **Fall**: spilled screws on a concrete floor, which put you on your back holding something sharp.

## Kit (product TYPES only)
Protection first, because this room starts there: impact-rated safety glasses, a PPE wall station, hearing protection and dated dust masks, nitrile gloves, a fire extinguisher with a gauge, and a stocked first aid kit. Cleaning inputs: a compact vacuum with attachments (never a broom), a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a floor-material-compatible cleaner, a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner for the extinguisher gauge, a colour-coded microfiber cloth set, a small detail brush set, a plastic scraper set, light machine oil, a resin and pitch remover for the blades, mineral spirits or a matching brush cleaner, and a portable cleaning caddy. Storage and control: a small parts drawer cabinet, small clear lidded bins, a material rack, a battery charging rack, a flammable safety cabinet, a self-closing oily rag can, a cable management kit and cord label set, a drawer safety latch, anti-tip anchors and hardware, floor boundary tape, a shelf location label set, removable write-on labels, water-resistant permanent labels, a portable label maker, FIFO date markers, a surge protector with overload protection, a moisture absorber or humidity monitor, and a keep, relocate, donate, recycle, trash container set.
