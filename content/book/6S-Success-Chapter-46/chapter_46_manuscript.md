## Chapter Opening

We have come a long way around to arrive here, and it is worth saying plainly: this is the room 6S came from.

Everything you have applied to a pantry and a nursery and a laundry room started on a shop floor, where a tool out of place was not untidy but expensive, and where an outline traced on a board was not a decoration but a way of knowing that a chisel was loose somewhere in the room. So this chapter is a homecoming, and it is also the one chapter where the stakes are genuinely different.

Every other room in this book fails into inconvenience. A drifted pantry costs you a duplicate jar. A drifted laundry room costs you a second tumble. This room fails into injury. A guard that has stopped springing shut, a blade stored flat in a drawer where your hand goes in first, a rag balled in a bin: those are not tidiness problems wearing a safety costume. They are the reason the discipline exists.

Which is why the order of work here is unlike anywhere else in the book. You start at the smallest zone in the room, the PPE station, because everything else in here involves a blade, a solvent, or a cloud of fine dust, and you want your glasses findable before you spend a Saturday handling any of it.

And there is one more thing to name before you begin, because it will decide whether this room actually changes. The workshop's mess is not about storage. It is three verdicts you have been postponing: on offcuts, on half-finished projects, and on paint. You already know which ones you avoid, because you will sort the fasteners twice before you touch the offcut pile once. Tipping out a jar of screws feels like progress. Putting good wood in the fire bucket feels like waste.

## Six Zones, Not One Room

You do not clean a workshop. You do the PPE station, then the bench, then the power tools, then the fasteners, then the material rack, then the finishes. Each is a job with a clear finish, and the first one is deliberately the smallest.

> The workshop is six small zones, not one big job: the safety and PPE station, the main workbench, the power tool storage, the fastener and hardware zone, the material rack, and the finishing and chemical zone. Do them one at a time, and start at the safety and PPE station even though it is the smallest zone in the room, because it sets the terms for the other five: once the glasses hang by the door and the extinguisher gauge reads green, you can work through the blades and the solvents without improvising.

One note on the order, because it is a deliberate departure. The Micro Zone Manual lists the main workbench first and the PPE station last, but its own guidance says to take the PPE station first, and that is the order this chapter follows. It is the only room in the book where the smallest zone leads, and the reason is that the other five all involve something that can hurt you.

> A finished workshop is one you can walk into and start cutting in inside a minute: safety glasses and muffs on labelled hooks at the door you come in through, an extinguisher with its gauge in the green and clear floor in front of it, a bare bench holding only the vise and the task lamp, every chisel and square back on its traced outline, each tool stored guarded with the accessories that make it usable, one fastener size per compartment with a sample glued to the face, boards flat and supported with the heaviest at the bottom, and every finish in its original labelled container inside a closed metal cabinet.

## The Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

Sort here is three postponed verdicts wearing a disguise, and the honest test is which zone you keep walking past. Straighten asks how far your hand travels mid-cut, so the marking knife and the combination square live within arm's reach of the vise while the things you use once a project go on the wall behind. Safety is not a pass in this room, it is the point of the room, and it runs through every zone. Standardize is where the workshop gave the rest of the book its vocabulary: a traced outline, a sample screw glued to a drawer face, a minimum line inside a bin, a photograph of the cleared bench taped where you stand. And Sustain hangs on the two moments that already end a session: the light going off, and the glasses coming off your face.

## How to Shine the Workshop: The Method

The nine rules of Shine hold, and this is the room they came from, so they need no defending here. What changes is that one of them is overridden by a rule specific to this space, and three others become genuinely serious rather than merely good practice.

> This is the room 6S came from, and the only one in the house where a bad standard can cost you a finger rather than an afternoon. Everywhere else in this book, a standard that slips costs you time, or a smell, or a morning putting things right. Here it costs you a hand. So the rules in this room are not preferences and they are not tidiness: a traced outline is how you know a chisel is loose in the room, a guard that springs back is the difference between a cut and an injury, and the glasses on the hook by the door are the reason you will still be doing this in twenty years.

The first part is the order of work, and it is the only room in the book where the smallest zone leads.

> The smallest zone in this room goes first, and it is the only room in the book where that is true. Everything else in here involves a blade, a solvent, or a cloud of fine dust, so you want your glasses findable and your extinguisher gauge green before you spend a Saturday handling any of it. Do it the other way round and you spend the whole day improvising: working without muffs because they are on the far wall, cutting with hazed glasses pushed up on your forehead, telling yourself you will find the mask before the sanding starts. The PPE station is not the reward at the end of the clean. It is the permission to begin.

The second part overrides a habit almost everyone brings into this room.

> There is one cleaning instruction in this room that overrides habit, and it is that you vacuum rather than sweep. A broom does not remove the finest sawdust, it launches it: back into the air, where it hangs, then settles onto wet finish and into your lungs. And the fines are the fraction that actually burns, which is why a shop full of airborne dust is a different kind of room from a shop with dust on the floor. So the vacuum comes out instead of the broom, the dog holes get sucked rather than brushed, and the extractor gets emptied at the end of the session rather than the start of the next one.

And the third part is the diagnosis. It is the reason a workshop that gets tidied every few months still never actually improves.

> The workshop's mess is never really about storage. It is three verdicts you keep postponing: on offcuts, on half-finished projects, and on paint. And you already know which ones you avoid, because you will sort the fasteners twice before you touch the offcut pile once. Tipping out a jar of screws feels like progress and putting good wood in the fire bucket feels like waste, so the drawers get neat while the pile that is actually holding the room back stays exactly where it was. The zones you keep skipping are the zones holding the room back, and that is the whole diagnosis.

> **Quick Win**
> Do the PPE station first. It is the smallest zone in the room and it takes under an hour: wash the lenses in soap and water, wipe the muff cushions, check the extinguisher gauge reads green with clear floor in front of it, and hang everything on labelled hooks at the door you actually walk in through.

> **6S Tip**
> Vacuum, never sweep. A broom launches the finest sawdust back into the air, where it settles onto wet finish and into your lungs, and the fines are the part that actually burns. Empty the extractor at the end of every session rather than the start of the next one.

## Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the heart of the chapter. For each of the six zones you get the inputs to gather before you start and the exact order of steps to clean and shine it. Work top to bottom within every zone, and reach for the vacuum rather than the broom every time. Every zone finishes the same way, with a look for the things to flag for the Safety pass.

### Zone 1 · The Safety and PPE Station

This makes protective gear so easy to reach that putting it on is faster than skipping it, and it goes first because it sets the terms for the other five zones. Clean the gear so it still protects: wash the lenses in soap and water rather than a dusty shop rag, which is what puts the scratches there in the first place.

**Inputs:**

- Mild dish soap and warm water, and a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, kept clean and away from the shop rags.
- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a compact vacuum with attachments.
- A fire extinguisher with a gauge, a stocked first aid kit, and a PPE wall station with labelled hooks.

**Steps:**

1. Dust the hooks and the board the gear hangs on, and vacuum the ledge above it where sawdust drifts down onto clean glasses.
2. Wash each pair of lenses in mild dish soap and warm water and dry them with a clean microfiber cloth, never a shop rag.
3. Bin any pair still scratched or hazed after washing, because hazed glasses are the reason people stop wearing safety glasses.
4. Wipe the sweat and sawdust off the earmuff cushions and the headband so the cushions still seal against your head, and wipe out the earplug case.
5. Wipe the outside of the dust mask box and check the masks are still boxed and dated rather than sitting loose and dusty.
6. Wipe the extinguisher body and bracket, then clean the gauge face with the glass cleaner so you can read the needle at a glance.
7. Lift the extinguisher, confirm the gauge sits in the green, and check nothing has crept into the floor space in front of it.
8. Wipe the outside of the first aid kit, open it, and clear any grit that has worked its way in among the dressings.
9. Vacuum and wipe the clear floor in front of the extinguisher and the door, so nothing creeps into the space you need to reach fast.
10. Hang everything back on the path you walk in on, one labelled set per regular user plus a visitor set, with a silhouette behind each item, and put your hand on the shop's power cutoff to confirm you could reach it with sawdust in your eyes and the lights off.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** earmuff cushions gone hard, cracked, or split so they no longer seal; lenses so scratched or hazed that you would be tempted to lift them off to see the cut line; a first aid kit low on burn dressings or down to its last few plasters, or dressings past their date; a dust mask box empty, or masks that have sat open and loaded with the dust they are meant to stop; and corrosion on the extinguisher body, or a bent, missing, or unsealed safety pin. Flag each.

### Zone 2 · The Main Workbench

This is the one surface where a project actually gets built, cut, glued, and repaired. Clean the bench to read it: vacuum the top and the dog holes rather than brushing dust into the next joint, scrape the glue drips before they telegraph into a workpiece, and finish at the vise with a wipe and one drop of oil.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner.
- A plastic scraper set, and light machine oil.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a compact vacuum with the crevice tool.
- A portable cleaning caddy, and a permanent marker for the outlines.

**Steps:**

1. Clear the bench top and the shelf beneath it completely, giving every half-finished project a verdict before anything goes back.
2. Start high and at the back: brush the tool wall and each hook downward, then run the vacuum crevice nozzle along the top of every tool outline where sawdust settles unseen.
3. With the bulb cool, wipe the task lamp shade, head, and arm joints with a barely damp cloth, keeping the cloth off the bulb and switch, because dust drawn to a warm shade bakes on and dulls the light you cut by.
4. Vacuum the grit out of the vise jaws and slide ways, wind the vise fully open and closed to clear the screw, then wipe the screw and work one drop of light machine oil along the thread.
5. Vacuum the whole bench top, and suck the dog holes out with the crevice nozzle rather than brushing, which only moves the grit around.
6. Scrape dried glue drips and squeeze-out flat with the plastic scraper before they press into your next board, then wipe the sealed top with neutral pH cleaner on a damp cloth.
7. Wipe the front edge and apron where forearms rest and where the fine dust rolls off the top and clings.
8. Clear the offcuts and dropped hardware off the under-bench shelf, vacuum the shelf and around the leg feet, then wipe the shelf down.
9. Vacuum the shavings and offcuts from the strip of floor you stand on, working them out from under the bench, then damp-wipe a sealed floor.
10. Check the vise jaws still bite a test piece without slipping, move the lamp cord clear of the run a handsaw crosses, and set the bench back to the vise, the lamp, and one project tray.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a wobble or a racking sway when you lean your weight on the bench, which points to a leg joint or a stretcher bolt working loose; the lamp cord or a tool flex cracked, stiff, or frayed where it bends over the bench edge; rust bloom on the vise screw, the bench dogs, or any bare steel fitting, a sign the shop is holding damp; and a scorch mark or a heat-glazed patch on the top that says a tool or a workpiece has been running hot on this spot. Flag each.

### Zone 3 · The Power Tool Storage

This keeps every corded and cordless tool guarded, complete with its own accessories, and ready to pick up and use. The dust these tools breathe is the dust that shortens them, so the motor vents come first, and every guard gets worked by hand before the zone is finished.

**Inputs:**

- A resin and pitch remover for the blades, and a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a soft brush or blower for the vents.
- A compact vacuum with attachments, and your caddy.
- A battery charging rack, and a shelf location label set.

**Steps:**

1. Work from the top down: vacuum the sawdust off the highest shelves and the cabinet top first, so nothing you disturb falls onto tools you have already cleaned.
2. With each tool unplugged or its battery out, blow or brush the packed sawdust out of every motor vent and air intake, because dust in the vents is what turns a warm motor into a hot one.
3. Wipe each tool housing with a barely damp cloth kept clear of the vents.
4. Wipe the pitch and resin off each saw blade and cutter with the resin remover so the teeth cut clean instead of burning, then stand them back guarded rather than loose.
5. Pull the clogged sanding pads, clear the dust ports with the vacuum, and brush out the accessory bins.
6. Vacuum the sawdust off the battery shelf and around the chargers, then wipe each contact face with a dry cloth so grit does not sit on a live terminal, keeping all moisture away from the contacts.
7. Wipe each cord down its full length as you coil it, and vacuum the grit and filings out of the accessory drawers.
8. Vacuum the cabinet floor and the strip in front of it where the heaviest sawdust settles, then damp-wipe the sealed floor.
9. Work the retracting guard on every saw with your thumb and confirm it snaps back on its own, and flex each cord at the strain relief where the sheath cracks first.
10. Set each tool back in a labelled home with its guard closed and its blade wrench or chuck key attached to it, batteries off the chargers and resting on a shelf that is not wood, and nothing stored plugged in.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a battery pack warm to the touch, swollen, or with a split or bulging casing while it sits on the charger; a brown scorch stain or a hot electrical smell around a motor vent; blade teeth chipped or missing, or a blue heat-discoloured patch on the blade plate; and rust on a saw table, a chuck, or an arbor, or a chuck that no longer grips a bit square. Flag each.

### Zone 4 · The Fastener and Hardware Zone

This lets you put your hand on the right screw straight away, so a project never stops for a hardware run. Vacuum the drawers out and read the corners while they are empty, because metal filings jam the runners and a bloom of rust in a bottom corner is the cabinet telling you where it sits.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy.
- A small parts drawer cabinet, water-resistant permanent labels, and a marker for the minimum lines.

**Steps:**

1. Dust and vacuum the top of the small parts cabinet and any shelf above it first, since it gathers the fine grit that sifts down the moment you open a drawer.
2. Wipe each drawer face and the sample fastener glued to it with a barely damp cloth, so you can still match a screw by eye rather than by reading a smeared label.
3. Lift out any loose bins and vacuum the metal filings and grit from each compartment.
4. Run the small detail brush into the drawer corners where washers and swarf pack down.
5. Wipe the outside of each bin and jar, clean any thread or lid gone stiff with grime, and check the level against the minimum line before it goes back.
6. Wipe the wall and the mounting rail behind the cabinet while you can reach them, and clear the dust off the fixings.
7. Vacuum the floor under and in front of the cabinet, where a single dropped screw waits for a bare foot.
8. Confirm the cabinet is fixed into studs rather than plasterboard anchors, because a wall cabinet full of steel fasteners is far heavier than it looks.
9. Refill one size per compartment with no shared drawers, the sizes you drive weekly at hand height, and a minimum line marked inside so a low bin announces itself as you slide it open.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** rust bloom in the bottom corners of a drawer, which says the cabinet is against a cold outside wall and holding damp; the cabinet standing proud of the wall, or a mounting screw beginning to back out under the weight of steel; a drawer runner sagging or dragging, a sign the drawer is carrying more than it was built for; and condensation or a damp patch on the wall behind the cabinet. Flag each.

### Zone 5 · The Material Rack

This holds the wood and metal you will genuinely use, stored so it stays straight and does not fall on anyone. The mess here hides behind the rack rather than on it, so the gap nobody sees is where the cleaning actually happens.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and light machine oil for the steel stock.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a long-handled brush for the high rails.
- A compact vacuum with attachments, and your caddy.
- A material rack, anti-tip anchors and hardware, and a marker for the size floor and fill line.

**Steps:**

1. Start at the top: brush the cobwebs and settled dust off the top rail and the highest arms before you touch the stock below.
2. Sweep or vacuum the strip of floor behind the rack that never sees daylight, where sawdust, spider webs, and mouse droppings collect out of sight.
3. Wipe each rack arm and bracket, clearing the sawdust that packs into the angle where the arm meets the upright.
4. Wipe the dust off the faces and edges of the boards as you restack them, running a hand along each one to feel for grit before it goes back.
5. Wipe the surface rust off any steel stock and lay on a light film of oil before it goes back, since bare steel on an open rack rusts from the shop air alone.
6. Wipe down the faces of the sheet goods and clear the grit from the floor where their bottom edge stands.
7. Tip the offcut bin out, vacuum the sawdust from the bottom, and refill it only up to the size floor and fill line written on the front.
8. Vacuum or sweep the full width of the route from the rack to the door so nothing is left low to trip on.
9. Restack heaviest at the bottom, flat stock supported at intervals close enough that nothing sags between brackets, sheet goods on edge against the wall rather than leaning out into the room, and nothing stored directly on the floor.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** mouse droppings, gnawed edges, or fresh webs behind the rack, the signs something has moved in where you cannot see; surface rust spreading on steel stock, or damp staining and a bow in a board that says water is reaching the rack; a rack bracket bending, a weld cracking, or an upright pulling away from the wall under load; and a sheet leaning at too shallow an angle and starting to creep, which you often hear as a faint slide before you see it. Flag each.

### Zone 6 · The Finishing and Chemical Zone

This stores paint, glue, stain, and solvent so they stay usable and so they never start a fire. Clean it can by can: wipe every rim before the lid goes back so the can does not skin over, clean your brushes right through to the ferrule, and wipe down the spill tray.

**Inputs:**

- Mineral spirits or a matching brush cleaner, and a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and clean shop rags.
- A compact vacuum with attachments, and your caddy.
- A flammable safety cabinet, a self-closing oily rag can, a drip tray, and a marker for dating lids.

**Steps:**

1. Dust and wipe the top of the metal cabinet first, then the upper shelf where the water based paints stand.
2. Wipe the rim and the shoulder of every can before the lid goes back on, because a rim packed with dried finish is why a lid stops sealing and the can skins over.
3. Date every container with the marker as it goes back, and check each one against its date as you handle it.
4. Wipe the solvent shelf, then lift the drip tray out and clean it, so the next drip lands on bare metal you can see rather than into a crust of old spills.
5. Clean each brush right through to the ferrule with the matching thinner or brush cleaner, work it dry, and hang it bristle down.
6. Wipe the outside of the oily rag can and check that the self-closing lid falls shut on its own weight.
7. Put every oil finish rag into that can as you work, or spread it flat outdoors to dry, because a rag soaked in oil finish generates its own heat as it cures and can ignite with nothing touching it.
8. Vacuum and wipe the cabinet floor, then the strip of floor in front, watching for the sheen of a slow seep.
9. Set the zone back with solvents and thinners on their own shelf, water based finishes on another, hardeners away from the resins they cure, everything in its original labelled container behind a closed metal door, and the shelf layout written on the inside of that door.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a can rim skinned over, a lid rusting into the seam, or a container bulging or swollen out of shape; a ring or stain on the shelf under the solvents that says something is weeping through a seam or a tired gasket; an aerosol gone warm or bulging, or a label rubbed illegible so you can no longer tell water based from solvent; and liquid pooled and standing in the drip tray, or the smell of solvent in the air with every lid supposedly shut. Flag each.

## The Calls That Matter Here

Six decisions, and three of them are the postponed verdicts that have been holding this room back.

The project that has been on the bench since spring. You have a half-assembled something occupying your best surface, untouched for months because the next step is one you are unsure how to do. Say out loud what the next physical action is and what date this month you will do it. If you cannot name both in one breath, the project is finished. Take it apart, put the hardware back in the fastener drawers and the stock back on the rack, and let the bench go back to being a bench. Keeping it assembled is not keeping the project alive. It is only keeping the surface unusable.

The expensive tool you never use. Somewhere in this room is a tool that cost real money and has not cut anything in two years, and you keep it because selling it would be admitting the purchase was wrong. Two questions settle it. Has it done work in the last twenty-four months? And if the job came up tomorrow, would you happily hire one for a day instead? If the answer is no and yes, sell it now, while it still fetches something. A tool losing value on your shelf is not an asset you are holding. It is a decision you are storing.

The inherited jar of mixed hardware. There is a tin, probably from a relative's garage, holding a few kilograms of unsorted screws, nuts, and washers, and it has followed you through two houses because there is obviously good hardware in there. Price the alternative first: a full box of the screws you actually drive costs less than the evening you would spend sorting. So give it one sitting, pick out only the sizes you already have a compartment for, and whatever is left goes to metal recycling that same day. Do not tip the remainder back into a smaller jar. That is exactly how it survived the last two moves.

The offcut that is still good wood. This is the hardest decision in the workshop and you already know it. Every short piece in that bin is genuinely usable, which is exactly why the pile never shrinks. So set two limits and let them decide instead of you: a size floor, anything shorter than your forearm or narrower than your palm, and a bin cap of one bin with no overflow. When the bin is full, the shortest piece leaves to make room for the newest. You are not judging whether a piece is good wood. You are only judging whether it is better than the worst piece already in the bin.

The wall of half-used paint. You are storing a row of part cans against the possibility of a touch-up, including colours from a wall you painted over and a house you no longer live in, and almost none of it will still brush out matching in two years anyway. Keep only colours currently on a surface in this house, and keep them properly: one small sealed jar of each, labelled with the room and the finish, not the original half-empty tin sitting open to the air. Everything else goes to hazardous waste on the same day you sort it, because a box of condemned paint standing by the door for a month is just the same problem, relocated.

The visitor rule. This is the only call in the book that is about a person rather than an object, and it is the one people handle worst. Your brother-in-law or your fourteen year old wanders in while the saw is running and wants to help, and you have been deciding it case by case, which means you have been deciding it badly. Decide it once and hang it on the wall: anyone who works in this shop has their own labelled set of glasses and muffs, and there is one spare set on a hook marked for visitors. If a visitor will not wear it, they stay on the near side of the doorway and watch. Saying that once, in advance, is far easier than saying it while holding a running grinder.

> **The Skipped Zone Challenge**
> Name the zone you have been avoiding. It will be the offcuts, the half-finished project, or the paint, because those are verdicts rather than tidying. Do that one next, not the fasteners again.

## Watch for in the Workshop

This is the room where the Safety pass stops being a pass and becomes the reason for the whole exercise.

> Burn or fire is the question this room answers first, and its sharpest answer is a rag: a cloth soaked in oil finish generates its own heat as it cures and can ignite with nothing touching it, so it goes in a lidded metal can or gets spread flat outdoors to dry. Fall, cut, or crush is the guard that has stopped springing shut on its own, the chisel lying edge up in an open tray, the loose blade stored flat in a drawer where your hand goes in first, and the sheet of ply leaning at too shallow an angle. Water and electricity is cracked cord sheathing at the strain relief, used while you stand on damp concrete. And poison is thinner decanted into a drink bottle, or solvent finish brushed in a closed shop with the door shut.

One of those deserves a second sentence, because it is the single most common serious hazard in a home workshop and almost nobody treats it as one.

The blade guard. It is a spring-loaded part that lives in sawdust, and sawdust is exactly what makes a spring sticky. So it degrades slowly, it degrades silently, and the first time you notice is the time it does not come back. Work it with your thumb every time you clean this zone, and if it hesitates at all, the tool is out of service until it does not.

The rest follow the shape of the room. Blades stored guarded, never flat in a drawer. Cords flexed at the strain relief, where the sheath cracks first. Cabinets full of steel fixed into studs. Sheets on edge rather than on a shallow lean. And an extinguisher whose gauge you can actually read, with clear floor in front of it.

> **Common Mistake**
> Sorting the fasteners twice before touching the offcut pile once; sweeping a shop rather than vacuuming it; and leaving the PPE station until last, so the whole day is spent improvising around gear you cannot find.
