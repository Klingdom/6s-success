Chapter Twenty-Four

Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself. A standard actually runs when three things are true: someone knows exactly what to do, the work has a time, and someone owns it. This chapter gives every standard its three tools: a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and a name for the who.

Last chapter you wrote a standard: you took the best way you knew to do some recurring task and put it on a card where anyone could follow it. That was the crucial first move, and it is not, by itself, enough, as you may already have discovered if you wrote one and then watched it get ignored for a week.

The problem is simple and worth saying plainly. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself. Writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, but knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. The card can be perfect and still sit there, because a standard, to actually run, needs three things to be true that writing it down does not supply. Someone has to know exactly what to do, step by step, without holding it in their head. The work has to have a time, or it waits forever for a moment that never feels right. And someone has to own it, or everyone assumes someone else will.

Those three needs are the whole of this chapter, and each has a tool that answers it. A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who. Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it. That is the difference between a home that has good intentions written down and a home that actually keeps its shape.

In this chapter

  • See why a written standard, on its own, still does not get followed.
  • Turn a standard into a checklist, so nothing gets forgotten.
  • Give recurring work a schedule, so it happens on a rhythm instead of in a crisis.
  • Put a name on every standard, so someone owns it.
  • Combine the three tools into a simple home system that runs without you.

The gapWhy a Standard on a Card Is Not Enough

It helps to be precise about how a good standard fails, because each way it fails points straight at the tool that fixes it. Watch a written standard that nobody follows, and you will see it fall down in one of exactly three places.

The first is memory. The standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not because they are careless but because that is what human memory does under a normal amount of distraction, so two steps quietly get dropped every time. The second is timing. Everyone agrees the task matters and everyone means to do it, but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later", and "later" is where good intentions go to die. The third is ownership. The task belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it, and it sits undone in the exact center of everyone's blind spot.

Notice that none of these three failures is a failure of the standard itself. The card was fine. What was missing was a way to carry it: something to hold the steps so none are forgotten, something to attach it to a time so it happens, and something to pin it to a person so it is owned. Those three carriers are the checklist, the schedule, and the responsibility, and the rest of this chapter is simply one each.

To make a standard run without you

To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own.

The whatThe Checklist: So Nothing Is Forgotten

Start with memory, because it is the one people are most embarrassed by and least need to be. A checklist takes a standard and turns it into a list you run rather than a thing you recall, and the moment you have one, forgetting a step stops being possible in the ordinary way.

A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not.

That is worth dwelling on, because a lot of people resist checklists at home out of a quiet sense that needing one is admitting a weakness. It is the opposite. The people who fly planes and operate on hearts, who are about as capable and well-trained as humans get, do not trust their memory for the steps that matter, and their results got dramatically better the day they stopped. A checklist is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. The trick is only to keep it short and real: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words, not every conceivable detail. A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time.

The whenThe Schedule: So It Actually Happens

Now timing, which is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient. It waits to become urgent.

A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it.

This is the whole quiet economy of a schedule. The gutter you never clean on a rhythm is the gutter you eventually clean in the rain because it overflowed; the fridge nobody wipes on a schedule is the one you empty in a hurry when something has gone off. Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working, and the way you get boring, working upkeep instead of dramatic, expensive crises is to give recurring work a time before it demands one. The easiest way to do that is not to invent a new slot in an already full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing. Attached to an existing rhythm, a task stops depending on anyone remembering to schedule it, because the schedule is already running.

The whoThe Responsibility: So Someone Owns It

Last, ownership, which is the one most likely to cause a flicker of discomfort, because it sounds like assigning chores and standing over people. Held rightly, it is nothing of the sort. It is just making sure each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's, because a task that is everybody's is, reliably and always, nobody's.

A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled.

You met that principle when you divided up the cleaning, and it applies without change here: a standard with no name against it is a standard in freefall, because responsibility that is diffuse is responsibility that evaporates. The fix is a name, one name, per standard. But how you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace. You do not hand out the jobs; wherever you can, you let people choose them, because a job someone chose is a job they will actually do, and a job that was dropped on them is a job they will quietly resent and half-perform. Sit the household down, lay out the standards that need owners, and let people pick. The one who does not mind the bins takes the bins. What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses.

The worked examplePut the Three Together

None of this is heavy, and the fastest way to feel that is to take a single standard and give it all three tools at once, which takes about five minutes and changes the standard entirely.

Take the after-dinner reset, the light fifteen-minute pass you met back in Shine. As a standard, it is the best way you know to get the common rooms back to calm before bed. Now give it the tools. The checklist is four or five lines: clear the table, wipe the counters, start the dishwasher, ten minutes of everyone putting their own things away, lights and lock. The schedule is not a new appointment; it is a cue you already have, so the reset starts when the dinner plates are cleared, every night, no decision required. And the responsibilities are chosen, not handed down: one person has the kitchen, another has the living room, the kids each have their own things, and the youngest, who asked, gets the very important job of the light switches. Written on one small card and stuck to the side of the fridge, that is no longer a vague good intention. It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass.

Quick Win

Take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes: turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it. That is the difference between a standard that exists and a standard that happens.

6S Tip

Hang the checklist and the schedule where the work happens and where everyone passes, the fridge, the back of a door, and keep both short. A checklist nobody can find, or one so long nobody finishes it, is the same as no checklist at all.

The milestoneThe Needle Holds, and the Catch Gets Stronger

Go and look at the friction meter, and you will find it exactly where it was last chapter. It has not moved, and, as before, that is honest rather than disappointing. You did not clear any new friction this chapter; you built tools for the standards you already had. Nothing left the rooms, so the needle holds.

A standard is the best way. A checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen.

But look again at the small catch that appeared behind the needle last chapter, the one that keeps a gain from sliding back, because it is sturdier now. A written standard was the first thing that could hold a gain in place at all; a standard with a checklist, a schedule, and a name is a much stronger hold, because now the good way is not just recorded but carried, run on a rhythm, and owned by someone. Where last chapter's catch was a single tooth, this chapter has given it three, and the backslide it prevents is barred more firmly than before. The needle did not advance. The grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S. The goal is still ahead on the calm side, because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain still follows.

Family Challenge

Build one checklist together for a shared recurring job, the after-dinner reset, the Saturday tidy, and let each person choose the line they will own. A job you chose gets done; a job you were handed gets resented.

In closingStandardize, Nearly Whole

You have most of the fifth S now. Chapter 23 gave you the idea of a standard, the best way you know today, written down. This chapter gave you the three tools that turn a written standard into something that runs: a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it happens, and a name so it is owned. Between them, a concept and its tools, you can take almost any recurring task in the home and make it hold its shape without leaning on your memory or your patience.

There is one kind of standard left, and it is the kind where all the words in the world work less well than a single picture. Some things are far easier to show than to describe: what a "tidy" shelf actually looks like, how the cables should be coiled, where exactly each thing sits when the room is right. For those, the best standard is not a checklist but an image, and building that last kind, the visual standard, is the next chapter, and it completes Standardize. For now, you have turned good intentions into small machines, and that is most of the way to a home that keeps itself.

Common Mistake

Making the checklist so complete it becomes a chore in itself, or the schedule so ambitious nobody keeps it. The three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier. If a tool is adding work instead of removing worry, cut it back until it helps.

One Idea to Keep

A written standard is a good idea; a checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make it happen. Give every standard you care about a list so nothing is forgotten, a time so it gets done on a rhythm, and a name so someone owns it, and it will run without you.
