# Teleprompter Script: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

**Chapter 24, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** checklists-schedules-and-responsibilities
**Length:** about 760 words. Narration only. Short lines for easy reading.

---

[Read at a calm, even pace. Pause at each blank line.]

This is the second chapter of Standardize.

The fifth S, continued.

Last chapter you wrote a standard.

The best way you know, put on a card anyone can follow.

That was the crucial first move.

And, on its own, it is not enough.

You may already know this.

You wrote a lovely little standard. Stuck it up.

And watched it get ignored for a week.

That is not a mistake. That is the gap.

A standard is a recipe on a card.

And a recipe on a card does not cook itself.

Writing it down tells everyone what good looks like.

And knowing what good looks like is not the same as doing it.

Watch a standard nobody follows.

It fails in exactly three places.

Memory. The six steps get done as four.

Timing. It drifts to "later", where good intentions die.

Ownership. It belongs to everyone, so to no one.

None of those is a failure of the card.

What was missing was a way to carry it.

And each one has a tool.

A checklist answers what.

A schedule answers when.

A responsibility answers who.

Start with memory.

A checklist turns a standard into a list you run.

Not a thing you recall.

And needing one is not a weakness.

A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful.

It lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly.

Without holding all of it in their head.

Pilots and surgeons live by them.

Not because they are bad at their jobs.

But because everyone forgets, and a list does not.

Keep it short and real.

The few steps that actually change the result.

A checklist you will run beats a thorough one you will not.

Now timing. Where the most good standards die.

A standard can be clear, and owned, and still never happen.

Because it never has a particular time.

And a task with no time does not wait to be convenient.

It waits to become urgent.

The gutter you never clean is the one you clean in the rain.

A schedule trades the emergency for dull, ordinary upkeep.

And upkeep is boring precisely because it is working.

You do not need a rigid timetable.

Hang the task off something that already happens.

The bins go out the night the show you watch ends.

The plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing.

Now the who. A name, one name, per standard.

Because a task that is everybody's is nobody's.

A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.

You met that line back in Shine.

The fix is a name attached to each standard.

But this is where a home is not a workplace.

You do not hand out the jobs.

Wherever you can, you let people choose them.

Because a job someone chose is a job they will do.

What is chosen gets done.

What is imposed gets subverted, with excellent excuses.

Now put all three on one standard.

Take the after-dinner reset.

The checklist is five lines.

Clear the table. Wipe the counters. Start the dishwasher.

Ten minutes, everyone puts their own things away. Lights and lock.

The schedule is a cue you already have.

It starts when the dinner plates are cleared. Every night.

The names are chosen.

One has the kitchen. One the living room.

The youngest, who asked, gets the light switches.

On one card, stuck to the fridge.

That is no longer a vague good intention.

It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts.

And it runs on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered.

Now go look at the friction meter.

It has not moved. And that is honest.

You cleared no new friction. You built tools.

But the catch behind the needle is stronger now.

A standard alone could hold a gain.

A standard with a list, a time, and a name holds it far better.

Where last chapter's catch was one tooth.

This chapter has given it three.

The needle did not advance.

The grip on everything behind it got tighter.

So here is your job.

Take the standard you wrote last chapter.

Give it the three tools in five minutes.

A short checklist. A time or a cue. One chosen name.

A written standard is a good idea.

A checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make it happen.

Give every standard a list, a time, and a name.

And it will run without you.
