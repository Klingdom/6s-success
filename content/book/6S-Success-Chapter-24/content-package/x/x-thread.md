# X Thread: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

Chapter 24 of *6S Success: Home Edition*. One thread, 14 posts. Each post is at or under 280 characters, counting its number line, and stands on its own. Plain "1/" numbering on its own line.

1/
Four S's made your home sorted, arranged, clean, and safe, and Chapter 23 gave you a standard: the best way you know, written on a card. Chapter 24 opens with the catch. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself.

2/
Writing the best way down tells everyone what good looks like, but "knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it." The card can be perfect and still just sit there, because it needs three things writing it down does not supply.

(255 chars)

---

3/
Watch a standard nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places. Memory: steps get quietly dropped. Timing: it drifts to "later". Ownership: it belongs to everyone, so to no one. None is the card's fault. What was missing was a way to carry it.

(264 chars)

---

4/
Those three needs are the chapter's three tools, each the answer to one plain question. "A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who." Give a standard all three and the card becomes a small machine that runs without you.

(259 chars)

---

5/
Start with memory, the failure people are most embarrassed by and least need to be. A checklist turns a standard into a list you run rather than a thing you recall, and the moment you have one, forgetting a step stops being possible in the ordinary way.

(256 chars)

---

6/
"A checklist is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always." Keep it short: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words.

(247 chars)

---

7/
Now timing, where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never got a particular time. A task with no time does not wait to be convenient. It waits to become urgent.

(268 chars)

---

8/
"A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it."

(225 chars)

---

9/
The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week, but to hang the task off something that already happens: bins out the night your show ends, plants watered while the Sunday coffee brews. The schedule is already running.

(263 chars)

---

10/
"A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled."

(264 chars)

---

11/
How you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and a home is not a workplace: wherever you can, let people choose. "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses."

(231 chars)

12/
Put all three on the after-dinner reset. A short checklist, a schedule that is a cue you already have (it starts when the plates are cleared), and chosen names for each part. On one card on the fridge, it becomes a fifteen minute machine that runs on a tired Tuesday.

(271 chars)
