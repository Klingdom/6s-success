## You are here blurb
You are at Chapter 24, the second chapter of Part Seven, Standardize, and it continues the fifth of the six S's. Last chapter gave you the concept of a standard and one written card as proof, and this chapter starts exactly where that leaves off: a standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself. Writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, but knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it, so a perfect card can sit there and do nothing. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places: memory, so steps get quietly dropped; timing, so it drifts to later, where good intentions go to die; and ownership, so it belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular. None of the three is a failure of the standard itself; what was missing was a way to carry it, and this chapter delivers the three carriers, each the answer to one plain question. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. The checklist is reframed away from shame: it is not a sign that you are forgetful, it is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head, the way pilots and surgeons work, not a crutch for the incompetent but how the competent stay reliable when tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always, and kept short and real, because a checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not. The schedule is reframed too: a task with no time does not get done on a rhythm, it gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis, so you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it, and the easiest way to give recurring work a time is to hang it off a cue that already happens rather than invent a new slot in a full week. The responsibility carries a callback to Shine: a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, so put one name per standard, and because a home is not a workplace, let people choose their standards wherever you can, since what is chosen gets done and what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses. The chapter works one example, the after-dinner reset given all three tools at once, a short checklist, a schedule that is a cue you already have, and chosen names against each part, written on one small card and turned into a fifteen-minute machine that runs on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass. This chapter does not build the visual and pictorial standards (Chapter 25), or the long-term adherence and audit system and the daily 6S time ritual (Sustain), and it does not re-teach Sort, Straighten, Shine, or Safety. Because building tools clears no new friction, the friction meter holds exactly where it was and does not move; but the catch behind the needle from last chapter is reinforced, its single tooth now three, standing for the three tools, and the backslide is barred more firmly, because the good way is now not just recorded but carried, run on a rhythm, and owned. The needle did not advance; the grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S. This is a hold with the anti-backslide catch reinforced, not a move and not a milestone, and the goal, out on the calm side, stays ahead, because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain still follows. The rule to carry out: a standard is the best way; a checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen.
