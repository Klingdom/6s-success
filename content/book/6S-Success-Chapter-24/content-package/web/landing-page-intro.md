# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 24, Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

Last chapter you did the hard, quiet thing: you took the best way you knew and put it on a card where anyone could follow it. You wrote a standard. That was the crucial first move, and if you have tried it, you may already have found the catch, because a written standard is necessary and it is not, by itself, enough. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself. You can write the perfect card, stick it exactly where it belongs, and still walk past it a week later to find the thing it describes simply is not happening. That is not the standard failing. It is the standard sitting there, waiting for the three things that writing it down never supplied.

Because writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, and knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places. Memory: the card has six steps and the person remembers four, so two quietly get dropped. Timing: it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to later, where good intentions go to die. Ownership: it belongs to the household in general, which means, reliably and always, it belongs to no one in particular. None of those is a failure of the standard itself. What was missing was a way to carry it, and there are exactly three carriers, one for each way a standard falls down.

So this chapter gives every standard its three tools, and each is the answer to one plain question. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who. Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

Start with the checklist, and start by clearing away the shame that clings to the word. A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not. A checklist is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. Keep it short and real, the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words, because a checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time.

Then the schedule, which is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient. It waits to become urgent. A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm; it gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis, and a schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it. Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working. The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing. And then the name, because a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned. The fix is a name, one name, per standard, and because a home is not a workplace, let people choose their standards wherever you can, since what is chosen gets done and what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses.

None of this is heavy, and the fastest way to feel that is to take a single standard and give it all three tools at once, which takes about five minutes. Take the after-dinner reset, the light fifteen-minute pass you met back in Shine. The checklist is four or five lines. The schedule is not a new appointment; it is a cue you already have, so the reset starts when the dinner plates are cleared, every night, no decision required. And the responsibilities are chosen, not handed down: one person has the kitchen, another the living room, the youngest, who asked, gets the very important job of the light switches. Written on one small card stuck to the side of the fridge, that is no longer a vague good intention; it is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass.
