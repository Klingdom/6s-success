### Slide 2

**Knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it.**

The line the whole chapter turns on:

"A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself."

A written standard is necessary, and it is not enough on its own. To actually run, a standard needs three things that writing it down does not supply: someone has to know exactly what to do, the work has to have a time, and someone has to own it. Those three needs are the whole of this chapter, and each has a tool.

### Slide 3

**A good standard fails in exactly three places.**

Watch a written standard that nobody follows, and it falls down in one of three spots. Memory: the standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not from carelessness but because that is what memory does under distraction, so two steps quietly get dropped. Timing: everyone means to do it, but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later", and "later" is where good intentions go to die. Ownership: it belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it.

None of the three is a failure of the standard itself. The card was fine. What was missing was a way to carry it, and there are exactly three carriers.

### Slide 4

**Three questions. Three tools.**

The frozen definition box, the hero of the chapter:

"To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own."

A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who. Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

### Slide 5

**A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful.**

The frozen checklist reframe:

"A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not."

A checklist takes a standard and turns it into a list you run rather than a thing you recall, and the moment you have one, forgetting a step stops being possible in the ordinary way. It is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. Keep it short and real: "A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time."

### Slide 6

**A task with no time does not wait to be convenient. It waits to become urgent.**

The frozen schedule reframe:

"A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it."

Timing is where more good standards die than anywhere else. The gutter you never clean on a rhythm is the one you clean in the rain because it overflowed. "Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working." The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week, but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing.

### Slide 7

**A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.**

The frozen responsibility reframe, a callback to Shine:

"A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled."

Ownership just means each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's. The fix is a name, one name, per standard. But how you attach it matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace: wherever you can, you let people choose. "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses."

### Slide 9

**Give one standard all three tools. It takes about five minutes.**

None of this is heavy. Take the after-dinner reset, the light fifteen-minute pass you met back in Shine, and give it all three tools at once. The checklist is four or five lines. The schedule is not a new appointment but a cue you already have, so it starts when the dinner plates are cleared, every night. And the responsibilities are chosen, not handed down.

Quick Win: "Take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes: turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it. That is the difference between a standard that exists and a standard that happens."

6S Tip: "Hang the checklist and the schedule where the work happens and where everyone passes, the fridge, the back of a door, and keep both short. A checklist nobody can find, or one so long nobody finishes it, is the same as no checklist at all."

### Slide 10

**A job you chose gets done. A job you were handed gets resented.**

There is a right way to hand out the names, and it is not to hand them out at all. Sit the household down, lay out the standards that need owners, and let people pick. A job someone chose is a job they will actually do; a job dropped on them is one they will quietly resent and half-perform.

Family Challenge: "Build one checklist together for a shared recurring job, the after-dinner reset, the Saturday tidy, and let each person choose the line they will own. A job you chose gets done; a job you were handed gets resented."

### Slide 11

**The needle still does not move. But the catch behind it just grew three teeth.**

Go and look at the friction meter, and you will find it exactly where it was last chapter. It has not moved, and that is honest rather than disappointing: you did not clear any new friction this chapter, you built tools for the standards you already had, so the needle holds.

But look again at the small catch that appeared behind the needle last chapter, the one that keeps a gain from sliding back, because it is sturdier now. A standard with a checklist, a schedule, and a name is a much stronger hold than a standard alone, because now the good way is not just recorded but carried, run on a rhythm, and owned. A concept last chapter, and its tools this one, and between them you can take almost any recurring task in the home and make it hold its shape without leaning on your memory or your patience.
