### Slide 2 · A recipe on a card does not cook itself

- Last chapter you wrote a standard: you took the best way you knew to do some recurring task and put it on a card where anyone could follow it. That was the crucial first move, and it is not, by itself, enough, as you may already have discovered if you wrote one and then watched it get ignored for a week. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself.
- Writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, but knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. The card can be perfect and still sit there, because a standard, to actually run, needs three things writing it down does not supply: someone has to know exactly what to do, the work has to have a time, and someone has to own it.
- Those three needs are the whole of this chapter, and each has a tool. Verbatim: "A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who." Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

### Slide 4 · Why a standard on a card is not enough

- Be precise about how a good standard fails, because each way it fails points straight at the tool that fixes it. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places. The first is memory: the standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not from carelessness but because that is what human memory does under distraction, so two steps quietly get dropped. The second is timing: everyone means to do it, but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later", and "later" is where good intentions go to die. The third is ownership: the task belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it, and it sits undone in the centre of everyone's blind spot.
- None of these three is a failure of the standard itself. The card was fine. What was missing was a way to carry it, and those three carriers are the checklist, the schedule, and the responsibility.
- The definition box, frozen: "To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own."

### Slide 5 · The checklist: so nothing is forgotten

- Start with memory, because it is the one people are most embarrassed by and least need to be. A checklist takes a standard and turns it into a list you run rather than a thing you recall, and the moment you have one, forgetting a step stops being possible in the ordinary way. A lot of people resist checklists at home out of a quiet sense that needing one is admitting a weakness; it is the opposite. The people who fly planes and operate on hearts do not trust their memory for the steps that matter, and their results got dramatically better the day they stopped.
- A checklist is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. Keep it short and real: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words. Verbatim: "A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time."
- The checklist reframe, frozen: "A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not."

### Slide 6 · The schedule: so it actually happens

- Now timing, which is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient; it waits to become urgent. This is the whole quiet economy of a schedule: the gutter you never clean on a rhythm is the one you clean in the rain because it overflowed; the fridge nobody wipes on a schedule is the one you empty in a hurry when something has gone off. Verbatim: "Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working."
- The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing. Attached to an existing rhythm, a task stops depending on anyone remembering to schedule it, because the schedule is already running.
- The schedule reframe, frozen: "A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it."

### Slide 7 · The responsibility: so someone owns it

- Last, ownership, the one most likely to cause a flicker of discomfort, because it sounds like assigning chores and standing over people. Held rightly, it is nothing of the sort. It is just making sure each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's, because a task that is everybody's is, reliably and always, nobody's. You met that principle when you divided up the cleaning, and it applies without change here: responsibility that is diffuse is responsibility that evaporates. The fix is a name, one name, per standard.
- But how you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace: you do not hand out the jobs; wherever you can, you let people choose them, because a job someone chose is a job they will actually do. Sit the household down, lay out the standards that need owners, and let people pick. Verbatim: "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses."
- The responsibility reframe, frozen, a callback to Shine: "A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled."

### Slide 8 · Put the three together

- None of this is heavy, and the fastest way to feel that is to take a single standard and give it all three tools at once, which takes about five minutes. Take the after-dinner reset, the light fifteen-minute pass you met back in Shine. The checklist is four or five lines: clear the table, wipe the counters, start the dishwasher, ten minutes of everyone putting their own things away, lights and lock. The schedule is not a new appointment; it is a cue you already have, so the reset starts when the dinner plates are cleared, every night, no decision required.
- And the responsibilities are chosen, not handed down: one person has the kitchen, another has the living room, the kids each have their own things, and the youngest, who asked, gets the very important job of the light switches. Written on one small card stuck to the side of the fridge, that is no longer a vague good intention. Verbatim: "It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass."
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes: turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it. That is the difference between a standard that exists and a standard that happens." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Hang the checklist and the schedule where the work happens and where everyone passes, the fridge, the back of a door, and keep both short. A checklist nobody can find, or one so long nobody finishes it, is the same as no checklist at all."

### Slide 9 · The rule

### Slide 12 · One kind of standard is left

- Be honest about what is done and what remains. You have most of the fifth S now: the concept and its three text-based tools, a list, a time, and a name. There is one kind of standard left, and it is the kind where all the words in the world work less well than a single picture: what a "tidy" shelf actually looks like, how the cables should be coiled, where exactly each thing sits when the room is right. For those, the best standard is not a checklist but an image, and building that last kind, the visual standard, is the next chapter, and it completes Standardize.
- The one thing to guard against is turning the tools against their own purpose. The three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier; if a tool is adding work instead of removing worry, cut it back until it helps. A checklist so complete it becomes a chore, or a schedule so ambitious nobody keeps it, is the tools working against you.
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Making the checklist so complete it becomes a chore in itself, or the schedule so ambitious nobody keeps it.
