# You Wrote the Standard. Then You Watched It Get Ignored for a Week, Because a Recipe on a Card Does Not Cook Itself.

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Last chapter you did the crucial thing. You took the best way you knew to do some recurring task and put it on a card where anyone could follow it. That was the first move, and it matters. It is also, on its own, not enough, as you may already have discovered if you wrote one and then watched it sit there, unread, while everyone did the task the old way.

Here is the uncomfortable little truth the fifth S has to face squarely: writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, but it does not make anyone do it.

**A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself.**

Knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. The card can be perfect and still lie there, because a standard, to actually run, needs three things writing it down does not supply: someone has to know exactly what to do, the work has to have a time, and someone has to own it. Those three needs are the whole of this chapter, and each one has a tool. A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who. Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

## Why a standard on a card is not enough

It is worth being precise about how a good standard fails, because each way it fails points straight at the tool that fixes it. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places. The first is memory: the standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not from carelessness but because that is what human memory does under distraction, so two steps quietly get dropped. The second is timing: everyone means to do it, but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later", and "later" is where good intentions go to die. The third is ownership: the task belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it, and it sits undone in the middle of everyone's blind spot.

None of these is a failure of the standard itself. The card was fine. What was missing was a way to carry it.

**To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own.**

## The checklist: so nothing is forgotten

Start with memory, because it is the one people are most embarrassed by and least need to be. A checklist takes a standard and turns it into a list you run rather than a thing you recall, and the moment you have one, forgetting a step stops being possible in the ordinary way. A lot of people resist checklists at home out of a quiet sense that needing one is admitting a weakness; it is the opposite. The people who fly planes and operate on hearts do not trust their memory for the steps that matter, and their results got dramatically better the day they stopped.

**A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not.**

Keep it short and real: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words. A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time.

## The schedule: so it actually happens

Now timing, which is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient; it waits to become urgent. This is the whole quiet economy of a schedule: the gutter you never clean on a rhythm is the one you clean in the rain because it overflowed; the fridge nobody wipes on a schedule is the one you empty in a hurry when something has gone off. Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working.

**A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it.**

The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing. Attached to an existing rhythm, a task stops depending on anyone remembering to schedule it.

## The responsibility: so someone owns it

Last, ownership, the one most likely to cause a flicker of discomfort, because it sounds like assigning chores and standing over people. Held rightly, it is nothing of the sort. It is just making sure each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's, because a task that is everybody's is, reliably and always, nobody's.

**A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled.**

But how you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace: you do not hand out the jobs; wherever you can, you let people choose them, because a job someone chose is a job they will actually do. Sit the household down, lay out the standards that need owners, and let people pick. What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses.

## The needle holds, and the catch gets stronger

Go and look at the friction meter, and you will find it exactly where it was last chapter. It has not moved, and, as before, that is honest rather than disappointing. You did not clear any new friction this chapter; you built tools for the standards you already had, so the needle holds. But look again at the small catch that appeared behind the needle last chapter, because it is sturdier now. A standard with a checklist, a schedule, and a name is a much stronger hold than a standard alone, because now the good way is not just recorded but carried, run on a rhythm, and owned by someone. Where last chapter's catch was a single tooth, this chapter has given it three.

**The needle did not advance. The grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S.**

The goal is still ahead on the calm side, because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain still follows.

## The assignment

Right now, before you do anything else, take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in about five minutes. Turn it into a short checklist, the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words. Hang it off a time or a cue you already have, so it happens on a rhythm rather than in a crisis. And put one name against it, chosen not handed down, so someone owns it. If it is a shared job like the after-dinner reset, build the checklist together and let each person choose the line they will own. Then stick the card where the work happens and everyone passes, the side of the fridge, the back of a door, and keep it short, because a checklist nobody can find, or one so long nobody finishes it, is the same as no checklist at all.

**A written standard is a good idea; a checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make it happen. Give every standard you care about a list so nothing is forgotten, a time so it gets done on a rhythm, and a name so someone owns it, and it will run without you.**
