# Chapter 24 Calls to Action: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

This is the second chapter of Part Seven, and it continues the fifth S. Its strongest CTA is the smallest possible proof of the whole idea: take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes, a short checklist, a time or an existing cue, and one name against it. The CTAs match the chapter's tone, warm and freeing, not corporate: the three tools are meant to take a load off you and share it, not impose a compliance system on the household, and responsibilities are chosen, not handed down. The move costs about five minutes, because the value is turning a good intention that only exists on a card into a small machine that runs on a tired Tuesday.

## In-chapter actions a reader can take (from the callouts)
- **Quick Win:** Take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes: turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it. That is the difference between a standard that exists and a standard that happens.
- **Family Challenge:** Build one checklist together for a shared recurring job, the after-dinner reset, the Saturday tidy, and let each person choose the line they will own. A job you chose gets done; a job you were handed gets resented.
- **6S Tip:** Hang the checklist and the schedule where the work happens and where everyone passes, the fridge, the back of a door, and keep both short. A checklist nobody can find, or one so long nobody finishes it, is the same as no checklist at all.

## CTA writing notes
- The action is a small, provable move that demonstrates the whole chapter: take a standard you already wrote and give it a checklist, a schedule, and a name. Lead with it, which is concrete and doable in about five minutes.
- Keep it non-prescriptive and anti-shame, in a register of warmth and relief. A standard that is written but never followed is the normal case; the fix is three small tools that share the load, not more discipline or nagging.
- The rule (a standard is the best way; a checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen), the recipe-on-a-card line, and the three reframes (checklist, schedule, responsibility) are the most memorable, shareable lines. Lead social with them.
- Avoid corporate framing, hype, and any force or control language (no enforce, comply, crack down, lock in, hold accountable in the punitive sense). The three tools take a load off you and free you; they are not a compliance regime imposed on the household, and responsibilities are chosen, not assigned. Avoid brand names (no named apps or chore-chart products).
- Scope note: this continues Standardize with the three text-based tools (lists, times, names); it does not build the visual and pictorial standards (Chapter 25), or the long-term adherence and audit system (Sustain, Chapters 26 to 30), and it does not re-teach Sort, Straighten, Shine, or Safety. The goal is still ahead because one piece of Standardize remains (Chapter 25 completes it) and Sustain follows.
