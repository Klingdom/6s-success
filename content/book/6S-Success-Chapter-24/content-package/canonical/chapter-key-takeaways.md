# Chapter 24 Key Takeaways: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **This chapter continues Standardize.** It is the second chapter of Part Seven and continues the fifth S. Chapter 23 gave the concept of a standard, the best way you know today, written down. This chapter gives that written standard the three tools that turn it from a good idea on a card into something the home actually runs.

2. **A standard on a card is not enough.** Writing down the best way was the crucial first move, and it is not, by itself, enough, as you may already have discovered if you wrote one and then watched it get ignored for a week. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself.

3. **Knowing is not doing.** Writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, but knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. The card can be perfect and still sit there, because a standard needs three things to be true that writing it down does not supply.

4. **A good standard fails in exactly three places.** Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of three spots: memory (steps get quietly dropped), timing (it drifts to "later", where good intentions go to die), and ownership (it belongs to the household in general, so to no one in particular). None of the three is a failure of the standard itself; what was missing was a way to carry it.

5. **The three tools, frozen (hero).** To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own.

6. **Each need has one tool.** A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who. Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

7. **A checklist is for everyone, not the forgetful.** A checklist takes a standard and turns it into a list you run rather than a thing you recall, and the moment you have one, forgetting a step stops being possible in the ordinary way.

8. **The checklist reframe, frozen.** A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not.

9. **A checklist is how the competent stay reliable.** It is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. Keep it short and real: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words. A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time.

10. **A task with no time waits to become urgent.** Timing is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient. It waits to become urgent.

11. **The schedule reframe, frozen.** A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it.

12. **Hang the task off a rhythm that already runs.** The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing. Attached to an existing rhythm, a task stops depending on anyone remembering to schedule it.

13. **A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.** Ownership just means each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's, because a task that is everybody's is, reliably and always, nobody's. Responsibility that is diffuse is responsibility that evaporates. The fix is a name, one name, per standard.

14. **The responsibility reframe, frozen (callback to Shine).** A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled.

15. **Chosen, not handed down.** How you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace. Wherever you can, let people choose their standards, because a job someone chose is a job they will actually do, and a job dropped on them is one they will quietly resent and half-perform. What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses.

16. **Put the three together on one standard.** Take a single standard and give it all three tools at once; it takes about five minutes and changes the standard entirely. The after-dinner reset gets a four or five line checklist, a schedule that is a cue you already have (it starts when the dinner plates are cleared), and chosen responsibilities (one person has the kitchen, another the living room, the youngest the light switches).

17. **The test is a tired Tuesday.** Written on one small card and stuck to the side of the fridge, that is no longer a vague good intention. It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass.

18. **The rule, frozen (hero).** A standard is the best way. A checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen.

19. **The needle holds, and the catch gets stronger.** You did not clear any new friction this chapter; you built tools for the standards you already had, so the needle holds. But the catch that appeared behind it last chapter is sturdier now: where it was a single tooth, this chapter has given it three, and the backslide it prevents is barred more firmly. The needle did not advance. The grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S.

20. **Standardize is nearly whole, and the goal is still ahead.** You have the concept (Chapter 23) and the tools (Chapter 24). One kind of standard is left, the kind a single picture carries better than any list, and building that visual standard is the next chapter, which completes the fifth S, with Sustain still to follow. One Idea to Keep: a written standard is a good idea; a checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make it happen. Give every standard you care about a list, a time, and a name, and it will run without you.
