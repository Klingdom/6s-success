# Infographic Spec: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

**Chapter 24, "Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Seven · Standardize**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: a written
standard is a good idea, but a standard runs only when three questions are answered, what, when, and who,
each with its own tool. The concept pairs the hero rule, **A standard is the best way. A checklist, a
schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen.**, with the picture at its heart: **the
three tools**, three even columns, a checklist for the what so nothing is forgotten, a schedule for the
when so it happens on a rhythm, and a responsibility for the who so someone owns it, each showing, faint
beneath, what goes wrong without it. So the viewer sees the whole move at once, that a standard falls down
in exactly one of three places and each place has its fix.

It stays consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_24_final.html`. Slate carries the
headings, the foot line, and the labels; one warm accent is held to a single column each, honey on the
checklist, terracotta on the clock, slate on the name-tag; the single small warm red is held back for
marks only and is barely used, if at all, on this calm image, because these are three light tools that
make a standard lighter, not a live hazard. "Chores" is not the point, and neither is "assignment"; a
home standard given its tools is warmer and simpler, a good intention turned into a small machine that
runs on a tired Tuesday. Anti-shame throughout: a written standard that gets ignored is the normal case,
not a failing, and a standard fails not because anyone is careless but because writing it down supplies
none of the three things it needs to run. The register is warm and freeing, never corporate, never a
chore chart; the three tools are a gift to the household.

## Layout notes

- The rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the whole chapter compressed: a checklist, a
  schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen. The eye should read the rule, then see
  the three tools make it run, the three even columns that answer what, when, and who.
- The three tools are the hero, so make them clear and even: three columns, none louder than the others,
  read left to right or top to bottom as what, when, who, with clear air between them, never a chore
  chart or a clinical table. Set the three headings, the three tool names, the three job lines, the three
  failure lines, and the foot line byte-for-byte as they read in the chapter; they are the frozen strings.
- Hold the color discipline: slate carries the headings, the foot line, and the labels; one warm accent is
  held to a single column each, honey on the checklist, terracotta on the clock, slate on the name-tag;
  the faint failure lines are muted and never alarming; the single small warm red is reserved for marks
  and is barely used, if at all, because this image is three light tools and a gift, not a live hazard.
  This image is calm, so no fill-line red anywhere.
- This image makes the concept, the three tools that make a standard run. Do NOT re-teach the earlier
  four S's, and do NOT re-explain the concept of a standard (Chapter 23, already given), nor build the
  visual and pictorial standards (Chapter 25, the piece that completes the fifth S, named only as still
  ahead), nor the long-term adherence and audit system (Sustain, a later S); this is the tools of
  Standardize, a checklist, a schedule, and a responsibility. And name no brand and draw no specific
  product; the tools are plain and generic, teaching the idea, not a shopping list.
- Anti-shame: a written standard that gets ignored is the normal case, not a failing, and a standard fails
  not because anyone is careless but because writing it down supplies none of the three things it needs to
  run. What you are building is not chores, it is a gift to the household. Keep the register warm and
  freeing, the three tools make a standard lighter, never heavier; no force or control language (no
  enforce, comply, lock down, no assigning chores), no clipboards or chore charts presented as virtue, no
  cage, no hype.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the columns and the tool
  icons drawn by hand rather than as a clinical chart, the checklist and the clock and the name-tag
  sketched rather than typeset, the whole image the calm, warm register of a good intention turned into a
  small machine that runs on a tired Tuesday rather than a chore chart.
