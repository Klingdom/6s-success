# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 24

**"Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Seven · Standardize*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to hold
across all five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`, terracotta
`#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B`, soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single warm red `#CB4B36`
reserved for the smallest marks only and used barely, if at all, on these calm images. Natural soft
daylight, shallow but not showy depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the finished home the reader has cleared, arranged, cleaned, made safe, and
begun to standardize. This chapter, the SECOND of Part Seven, CONTINUES Standardize, the fifth S, and
does one turn: it takes the written standard the last chapter delivered, a recipe on a card, names the
gap underneath it (a recipe on a card does not cook itself, so a written standard is necessary but not
enough, and it fails in exactly three places, memory, timing, and ownership) and delivers the tools that
answer it. It teaches the three failure modes and the tool that fixes each, delivers the hero (every
standard gets its three tools, a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and a name for the who,
carried by the three-tools line and the definition box), delivers the checklist reframe (not a sign you
are forgetful but how the competent stay reliable when tired, rushed, or interrupted, the way pilots and
surgeons work, kept short and real), the schedule reframe (a task with no time gets done in a panic once
it is a crisis, so hang it off a cue that already happens and trade the emergency for dull, working
upkeep), and the responsibility reframe (a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, the callback
to Shine, so one chosen name per standard because what is chosen gets done), walks the reader through
putting all three on one standard (the after-dinner reset becomes a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a
time, and named parts that runs on a tired Tuesday), and reads the friction meter as a HOLD with the
catch behind the needle reinforced. Nothing of the earlier four S's is re-taught, and the concept of a
standard from Chapter 23 is used, not re-explained. No visual or pictorial standards are built (Chapter
25, named only as still ahead, the piece that completes the fifth S), and no long-term adherence or audit
system (Sustain, a later S, named only as still to come). Keep the tone calm, warm, and freeing: a
written standard that gets ignored is the normal case, not a failing, and a standard fails not because
anyone is careless but because writing it down supplies none of the three things it needs to run. The
three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier, a gift and not a chore; the register is
relief, good intentions turned into small machines that run without you. Crucially, describe only the
calm, warm illustrated house style: no corporate office look, no clipboards or chore charts presented as
virtue, no one standing over a shoulder, no force or control framing (no enforce, comply, lock down, no
assigning chores), no cages, no photoreal polish; the standard is a warm card on a real surface and its
tools are light and human.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical object
in the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real sheet, a name on a real tag, or the
reading on a real gauge, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental hand-lettered
label: use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 24 · STANDARDIZE`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot
between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: standard-card, name-tag, after-dinner,
anti-backslide, best-way, launch-pad, cupboard-door, recipe-card, Safety-complete, Shine-complete,
Straighten-complete, Sort-complete, fifteen-minute, five-minute, tired-Tuesday.

## 1. The same standard, activated (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of a single hand-lettered standard card, the recipe from last
chapter, shown twice on a kitchen counter in soft daylight. To one side, in a faint greyed version, the
card just lies flat, ignored, a good idea nobody is acting on, with a small question mark beside it.
Beside it, in full warm color, the very same card stands upright and is set visibly in motion by three
small tools clipped to it: a little checklist with a few ticks springs from one corner in honey, the
what; a small round clock hangs from another corner on a short string in terracotta, the when; and a
slate name-tag on a short string is clipped to a third, the who. Around the activated card, faint motion
lines trail and a couple of family figures are actually doing the task, each with a small soft green
check, because the card is now being followed where the greyed one is not. Warm paper cream and honey
counter tones, ink-brown line work, slate for the card, honey checklist, terracotta clock, slate
name-tag, soft green for the checks of ease, one terracotta for the ignored-card question mark, no
warm-red anywhere. Editorial cookbook-style illustration. The same card, once ignored and once running.

- Mood: a written standard by itself does nothing, it just lies there; the same standard given three
  tools, a checklist, a schedule, and a name, becomes something the home actually does; writing the
  standard was the last chapter, making it happen is this one; the register is relief and momentum, a
  good intention set running, not blame.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, a corporate or office look, floating typeset text
  overlays, any clipboard or chore chart presented as the answer, anyone being scolded or standing over
  another, a cage or lock, any warm-red accent, brand names or recognizable products; keep the greyed
  card genuinely inert and low-contrast and the colored card the very same card, unchanged in its words,
  only now standing up and carried by its three tools with people actually following it.

## 2. The three tools a standard needs (the hero infographic)

A warm editorial illustration of three clean hand-drawn columns under a shared header the three tools a
standard needs, in soft daylight, read left to right as what, when, and who. The first column, what,
shows a small hand-drawn checklist with a few ticks in a warm honey accent, the tool a checklist, its job
so nothing is forgotten, and faint beneath a dashed line the failure mode without it, forgotten. The
second column, when, shows a small round clock in a terracotta accent, the tool a schedule, its job so it
happens on a rhythm, and faint without it, done in a panic. The third column, who, shows a small
name-tag on a string in slate, the tool a responsibility, its job so someone owns it, and faint without
it, orphaned. Across the foot, a small hand-lettered line reads a list, a time, and a name. Warm paper
cream background, ink-brown line work, slate headings, one warm accent held to a single column each,
honey on the checklist, terracotta on the clock, slate on the name-tag, the faint failure lines muted and
never alarming, no warm-red anywhere. A picture of one plain idea: a standard runs only when all three
questions are answered, what, when, and who.

- Mood: three questions, three tools; miss any one and the standard falls down in that exact spot,
  forgotten, delayed, or orphaned; the three tools are light and make a standard lighter, not heavier;
  calm, even, quietly practical, the three columns equal and none louder than the others.
- Avoid: a cold clinical chart, a corporate slide, gridlines everywhere, a chore chart, one column
  shouting over the others, alarming red failure warnings, cold clinical light, floating typeset text,
  any clipboard presented as virtue, any warm-red, brand names or recognizable products; keep the three
  columns hand-drawn and even, one warm accent each, the failure lines quiet and muted, the foot line a
  small hand-lettered a list, a time, and a name.

## 3. A standard with its three tools (the whole thing, once)

A warm editorial illustration of a single standard card, in the same style as the taped-up recipe card
from last chapter, now clearly carrying all three tools as a small running system, at a realistic size,
in soft daylight. A small curl of tape sits at the top-left corner. The plain title, in a warm honey
accent, reads The After-Dinner Reset. Below it is a short checklist of five ticked lines in plain slate
words with soft green ticks: clear the table; wipe the counters; start the dishwasher; ten minutes,
everyone puts their own things away; lights and lock. Below the list, a schedule line marked with a small
terracotta clock reads when, the moment the dinner plates are cleared, every night. Below that, a
responsibility block marked with small slate name-tags reads kitchen, Sam; living room, Alex; own things,
everyone; lights, Jo. Warm paper cream card, ink-brown line work, slate step text, soft green ticks, one
honey accent on the title, one terracotta accent on the clock, soft rule hairlines, no warm-red anywhere.
A picture of one plain idea: a standard with all three tools is a short checklist, a schedule tied to a
cue that already happens, and chosen names, all on one small card.

- Mood: this is the whole craft of the chapter done once, a good intention turned into a fifteen-minute
  machine that runs on a tired Tuesday; the card is small, warm, and doable; the checklist few and plain,
  the schedule hung off a cue you already have, the names chosen and human; warm, doable, unintimidating.
- Avoid: a formal form, a policy, a chore chart, a printed clinical document, a clipboard, cold clinical
  light, more than five checklist lines, legalistic or corporate wording, names that read as assigned
  duties rather than chosen ones, floating typeset overlays, any warm-red, brand names or recognizable
  products; keep the card small, warm, and hand-made, the curl of tape saying it lives on a real surface,
  the register a fifteen-minute machine stuck to the side of the fridge.

## 4. Three ways a standard falls down (the reframe)

A calm, warm illustration of the chapter's central turn, drawn as three gentle rows read top to bottom,
each a place a written standard falls down paired with the tool that answers it, in soft daylight. The
first row, memory: on the left, a standard card with one step quietly faded out, drawn dim and
low-contrast, a couple of steps dropped; on the right, warm and lit, a honey checklist that holds every
step, joined by a gentle arrow. The second row, timing: on the left, a dim standard drifting to later
with no time attached; on the right, warm and lit, a terracotta clock hung off a cue that already
happens. The third row, ownership: on the left, a dim standard belonging to everyone and so to no one,
with a few onlookers each assuming another has it; on the right, warm and lit, a slate name-tag that owns
it. Between or beneath the rows, a quiet hand-written line carries the reframe: none of these is a
failure of the standard itself, only of the way it was carried. To one side, a quieter, dimmer, unlit
panel stands for the chapter where a standard is a picture, kept low-contrast and clearly ahead. Warm
paper cream background, ink-brown line work, each failure dim and a little tired, each tool warm and lit,
honey for the checklist, terracotta for the clock, slate for the name-tag, the right group dim and unlit,
no warm-red anywhere. The image reads as one plain idea: each way a standard fails points straight at the
tool that fixes it, a list for memory, a time for timing, a name for ownership.

- Mood: a written standard is necessary but not enough, and it fails in exactly three places, memory,
  timing, and ownership, each of which names its own fix; the card was fine, what was missing was a way
  to carry it; the register is warmth and relief, not correction, a standard is not failing, it is only
  waiting for its tools.
- Avoid: drawing the standard as villainous or the tools as flimsy, any scolding or shaming tone, a cage
  or lock or someone standing over a shoulder, a corporate office look, a chore chart, teaching any
  next-chapter content in the dim side panel, any warm-red, floating typeset text, hype; keep each
  failure dim and a little tired and each tool visibly warm and lit and light, the register a fix that
  each failure asks for rather than a rebuke.

## 5. The needle holds, and the catch gets three teeth (the friction meter)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge dialing
from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right. This chapter the
dark live needle does not move: it holds exactly where Chapters 22 and 23 left it, at the Safety-complete
position, resting on the fourth slate milestone tick, with Sort complete, Straighten complete, and Shine
complete sitting further back as small passed slate markers. What has changed since last chapter is the
catch behind the needle: where last chapter drew a single small catch, this chapter draws a firmer,
stronger ratchet whose three teeth stand for the three tools, a checklist, a schedule, and a
responsibility, or a list, a time, and a name, holding the gains far more securely against the pull back
toward friction, and the faint dashed backslide arrow toward the friction side is barred more solidly
than before, with a double bar and a small red block, hand-marked no backslide. A slate crosshair labeled
the goal, carrying its purpose flag, still sits further out on the green calm side, clearly ahead of the
live needle, with visible room left between them, because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain
still follows. Warm paper cream background, ink line work, consistent stroke weight, friendly but not
childish.

- Mood: the needle does not move because nothing in the rooms changed, you built tools for the standards
  you already had, so it would be dishonest to show a forward step; but the catch that appeared last
  chapter is stronger now, its single tooth grown to three, one for each tool, and the gains are held far
  more firmly than a lone written standard held them; the needle did not advance, its grip on everything
  behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S; the goal is still ahead,
  because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain follows.
- Avoid: the live needle advancing, moving forward, or reaching the goal crosshair, drawing this as a new
  milestone or a forward step, a completion glow or celebratory confetti, the passed markers or the
  dashed backslide arrow drawn solid or bright, no room left between the needle and the goal, hype, neon
  color, any sense of the journey being finished. The needle holds exactly where Safety left it, the
  catch behind it grows from one tooth to three, the backslide is barred more solidly, and clear room
  remains between the needle and the goal.
