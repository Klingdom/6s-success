# Diagram Ideas: Chapter 24

**"Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Seven · Standardize*

Five diagrams that carry the chapter's logic. All use the warm book palette and the two chapter
fonts (Fraunces display, Newsreader body, a clean sans for labels). These are working specs, not
finished art. They stay consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_24_final.html`: the
opener, one slate standard-card shown twice, on one side a faint greyed version lying flat and ignored
while beside it the very same card stands upright and colored, set running by three small tools clipped
to it, a ticked checklist in honey (the what), a clock on a short string in terracotta (the when), and a
slate name-tag on a string (the who), with faint motion lines and two family figures walking after it;
the three-tools infographic, three clean columns under the header the three tools a standard needs, What
with a honey checklist, When with a terracotta clock, Who with a slate name-tag, each with its one-line
job and, faint beneath, the failure mode without it, and a foot line reading a list, a time, and a name;
the standard-with-its-three-tools card, a warm card titled The After-Dinner Reset carrying a short ticked
checklist, a schedule line marked with a small clock, a responsibility block with small name-tags, and a
curl of tape; and the friction meter, which this chapter HOLDS, the live needle unmoved at the
Safety-complete position with the catch behind it REINFORCED to three teeth and the barred backslide
arrow strengthened, the goal still ahead.

Scope fence: this chapter is the SECOND chapter of Part Seven, and it CONTINUES Standardize, the fifth of
the six S's. It does one turn: it takes the written standard the last chapter delivered, a recipe on a
card, names the gap underneath it (a recipe on a card does not cook itself, so a written standard is
necessary but not enough and fails in exactly three places, memory, timing, and ownership), and delivers
the tools that answer it, the three tools that make a standard actually run. It teaches the three
failure modes and the tool that fixes each (memory answered by a checklist, timing answered by a
schedule, ownership answered by a responsibility), delivers the hero (every standard gets its three
tools, a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and a name for the who, carried by the
three-tools line and the definition box), delivers the checklist reframe (not a sign you are forgetful
but how the competent stay reliable, the way pilots and surgeons work, kept short and real), the
schedule reframe (a task with no time gets done in a panic once it is a crisis, so hang it off a cue
that already happens and trade the emergency for dull, working upkeep), and the responsibility reframe
(a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, the callback to Shine, so one chosen name per
standard because what is chosen gets done), walks the reader through putting all three on one standard
(the after-dinner reset becomes a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts that runs
on a tired Tuesday), and reads the friction meter as a HOLD with the catch behind the needle reinforced.
It USES the written standard from Chapter 23 and does NOT re-teach it. CRITICAL fence: this is the tools
that make a standard run, the checklist, schedule, and responsibility system, NOT the concept of a
standard (Chapter 23, already given), NOT the visual and pictorial standards (Chapter 25, named only as
still ahead, the piece that completes Standardize), and NOT the long-term adherence and audit system
(Sustain, a later S, named only as still to come). Because building tools for standards you already had
clears no new friction from the rooms, the friction needle does NOT move this chapter; it HOLDS exactly
where Safety left it and where Chapter 23 held it, and the catch that appeared last chapter is
REINFORCED, its single tooth now three, one for each tool, the backslide barred more firmly, so the
meter is a HOLD with a stronger anti-backslide catch, NOT a move and NOT a milestone. Separator
discipline: labels use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 24 · STANDARDIZE`, never a floating hyphen or a
floating dot between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: standard-card, name-tag,
after-dinner, anti-backslide, best-way, launch-pad, cupboard-door, recipe-card, Safety-complete,
Shine-complete, Straighten-complete, Sort-complete, fifteen-minute, five-minute, tired-Tuesday. Anti-shame
throughout: a written standard that gets ignored is the normal case, not a failing; a standard fails not
because anyone is careless but because writing down the best way supplies none of the three things a
standard needs to run, memory, a time, and an owner. The three tools are meant to make a standard
lighter, not heavier, a gift and not a chore; a checklist is not a crutch for the incompetent and a
responsibility is not a chore handed down. No force or control framing anywhere (no enforce, comply, lock
down, no chores standing over people, no clipboards presented as virtue); no corporate or office
register; no hype; no brand names. The register is calm, warm, human, and illustrated, the relief of
turning good intentions into small machines that run without you.

## 1. The Same Standard, Activated (the opener)

**Purpose:** Show the chapter's whole idea in one image. A written standard by itself does nothing; the
same standard given three tools, a checklist, a schedule, and a name, becomes something the home actually
does. Nothing about the standard changes; what changes is that it is now carried, timed, and owned.
Mirrors Figure 1 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** One slate standard-card, the recipe from last chapter, drawn twice on a kitchen
counter. To one side, in a faint greyed version, the card just lies flat, ignored, a good idea nobody is
acting on, with a small terracotta question mark beside it. Beside it, in full color, the very same card
stands upright and set visibly in motion by three small tools clipped to it: a little ticked checklist
springs from one corner in honey (the what), a small round clock hangs from another on a short string in
terracotta (the when), and a slate name-tag on a string is clipped to a third (the who). Around the
activated card, faint motion lines trail and two small family figures walk after it, each with a small
green check, because the card is now being followed where the greyed one is not.

**Rough layout:**
- A warm counter-top wash on cream `#FBF7EF`. A faint Newsreader italic line, muted: `the same card,
  once ignored and once running`.
- LEFT, `A GOOD IDEA NOBODY IS ACTING ON` in soft gray sans caps at the foot: the standard-card lying
  flat, drawn low-contrast in greyed wood-and-paper tones `#ECE6DA` and `#9C9284`, a small terracotta
  `?` beside it, no tools attached.
- RIGHT, `THE SAME CARD, RUNNING` in slate sans caps at the foot: the very same card standing upright in
  slate with a cream face `#FCF8F0`, three tools clipped on, a honey ticked checklist (the what), a
  terracotta clock on a string (the when), a slate name-tag on a string with a card-slate `#EEF3F6` face
  (the who), faint motion lines behind it, and two family figures in green walking after it each with a
  small green check.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter): "The same standard, twice. On its own, greyed and ignored, it is a
  good idea nobody is acting on. Given three tools, a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and
  a name for the who, the very same card is suddenly in motion, being followed. Writing the standard was
  the last chapter. Making it happen is this one."

**Note for the illustrator:** the point is the shift from a standard that only exists to a standard that
happens, so draw the greyed card genuinely inert, flat and quiet and low-contrast, and the colored card
the same card, unchanged in its words, only now standing up and carried by its three tools with people
actually following it. Hold the color discipline: slate for the card, one warm accent per tool, honey for
the checklist, terracotta for the clock, slate for the name-tag, soft green for the checks of ease and
the following figures, one terracotta for the ignored-card question mark; spark-red does not appear. The
ignored card is the normal case, never a scolding; a written standard that gets ignored is not a failing
but exactly what happens before it has its tools. Warm line-and-flat-color style, slate card, the
register of a good intention turned into a small machine.

## 2. The Three Tools a Standard Needs (the hero infographic)

**Purpose:** Give the reader the whole hero device on one page: the three questions a standard must
answer to run, each with its tool, its job, and what goes wrong without it. This is the single strongest
image of the chapter and the subject of the infographic spec. Mirrors Figure 2 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** Three clean columns under a shared header, `THE THREE TOOLS A STANDARD NEEDS`. Each
column names one question, shows its tool as a small warm icon, gives the tool's one-line job, and, faint
beneath a dashed rule, the failure mode if the tool is missing. A small line across the foot reads a
list, a time, and a name.

**Rough layout:**
- A calm panel background `#FBF7EF`. Top, a small sans caps label, letter-spaced, soft gray: `THE THREE
  TOOLS A STANDARD NEEDS`.
- Three cream `#FCF8F0` columns with rule `#E2D8C4` borders, each with a slate question heading, a warm
  tool icon, a Fraunces tool name, a Newsreader italic job line, a dashed hairline, and a faint sans
  failure line in `#B0A692`:
  - Column one, `WHAT` (honey accent): a honey checklist icon, `A checklist`, `so nothing is forgotten`,
    faint `without it: forgotten`.
  - Column two, `WHEN` (terracotta accent): a terracotta clock icon, `A schedule`, `so it happens on a
    rhythm`, faint `without it: done in a panic`.
  - Column three, `WHO` (slate accent): a slate name-tag icon on a string with a card-slate `#EEF3F6`
    face, `A responsibility`, `so someone owns it`, faint `without it: orphaned`.
- Centre foot, a short rule to each side and a Fraunces slate line between: `a list, a time, and a name`.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter): "Three questions, three tools. What needs a checklist, so nothing
  is forgotten. When needs a schedule, so it happens on a rhythm rather than in a crisis. Who needs a
  name, so someone owns it. Miss any one and the standard falls down in that exact spot: forgotten,
  delayed, or orphaned."

**Note for the illustrator:** the message is that a standard runs only when all three questions are
answered, so keep the three columns even and equal, none louder than the others, read left to right as
what, when, who. Set the headings, tool names, job lines, failure lines, and the foot line exactly as
they read; they are frozen wording and must not be trimmed or reworded. Hold the color discipline: slate
for the headings and the foot line, one warm accent held to a single column each, honey on the checklist,
terracotta on the clock, slate on the name-tag; the faint failure lines in muted `#B0A692`, never
alarming; no spark-red anywhere. Draw no clipboard-as-virtue, no chore chart; these are three light tools
that make a standard lighter. Warm line-and-flat-color style, the register of a good idea given the three
things it needs to run.

## 4. Three Ways a Standard Fails, Three Tools That Fix Them (the reframe, no direct HTML twin)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's central turn as a picture: a written standard is necessary but not
enough, and it fails in exactly three places, memory, timing, and ownership, each of which points
straight at the tool that fixes it. This diagram has no direct HTML figure twin; it renders the failure
reframe as a calm before-and-after, so the reader feels that none of the three failures is a failure of
the standard itself, only of the way it was carried.

**What it shows:** Three quiet rows, read top to bottom, each a place a standard falls down paired with
the tool that answers it. Row one, memory: a standard with a step quietly dropped, drawn dim, answered by
a honey checklist that holds every step. Row two, timing: a standard drifting to "later" with no time
attached, drawn dim, answered by a terracotta schedule that hangs it off a cue. Row three, ownership: a
standard belonging to everyone and so to no one, drawn dim, answered by a slate name that owns it. Each
failure sits on the left, faint and low-contrast; each tool sits on the right, warm and lit; a gentle
arrow crosses from the failure to the fix.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: sans caps label, soft gray, `THREE WAYS A STANDARD FALLS DOWN`, with a Fraunces ink line beneath
  carrying the section's spine: "None of these three failures is a failure of the standard itself; what
  was missing was a way to carry it."
- Three cream `#FCF8F0` rows with ink outlines, each split into a dim `WHERE IT FALLS` cell on the left
  and a warm `THE TOOL THAT FIXES IT` cell on the right, joined by a gentle slate arrow:
  - `MEMORY`: left, a card with a step faded out, muted note `two steps quietly get dropped`; right, a
    honey checklist, note `a checklist, so nothing is forgotten`.
  - `TIMING`: left, a card drifting with a muted note `it drifts to later, where good intentions go to
    die`; right, a terracotta clock hung off a cue, note `a schedule, so it happens on a rhythm`.
  - `OWNERSHIP`: left, a card with no name and three onlookers each assuming another has it, muted note
    `it belongs to everyone, so to no one`; right, a slate name-tag, note `a name, so one person owns
    it`.
- Beneath, a small slate-bordered strip carrying the reframe in ink: `The card was fine. What was missing
  was something to hold the steps, something to attach a time, and something to pin a person.`
- To the right or below, a dimmer honey-outlined panel, unlit, tagged `PICTURES · VISUAL STANDARDS ·
  NEXT` in muted honey sans caps, a muted italic note beneath, `the last kind of standard is a picture,
  and it completes the fifth S`, held quiet and clearly ahead, not yet reached.
- Footer, Newsreader italic, slate: "Each way a standard fails points straight at the tool that fixes it:
  a list for memory, a time for timing, a name for ownership."

**Note for the illustrator:** this is the chapter's central turn, so keep it calm and plain, never
scolding; the point is not that the standard was wrong but that a standard needs carrying, and each
failure names its own fix. Draw the three failures dim and low-contrast and a little tired, and each tool
warm and lit and light. Hold the color discipline: honey for the checklist, terracotta for the clock,
slate for the name-tag and the sub-strip, one muted honey for the dim next-chapter panel; no spark-red,
no cage, no chore chart, no one standing over a shoulder. Keep the next panel dim, unlit, and clearly
ahead; you may name the visual standard as next and as the piece that completes the fifth S, but teach
nothing of it. The register is warmth and relief, not correction: a standard is not failing, it is only
waiting for its tools. Warm line-and-flat-color style.

## 5. The Needle Holds, and the Catch Gets Three Teeth (the friction meter)

**Purpose:** Deliver the honest HOLD beat and close the chapter. The needle does NOT move this chapter,
because nothing new was cleared; you built tools for the standards you already had. What is new is that
the catch that appeared last chapter is stronger now: where Chapter 23 drew a single small catch, this
chapter draws a firmer ratchet with three teeth, one for each tool, and the barred backslide arrow is
strengthened, so a standard with a checklist, a schedule, and a name is far harder to let slide than a
standard alone. It must read as a true HOLD with a reinforced catch, NOT as a move and NOT as a new
milestone, and the goal is still ahead. Mirrors Figure 4 in the HTML, and drives the live data-driven
gauge.

**What it shows:** The recurring half-circle gauge with the live needle unmoved at the Safety-complete
position it reached two chapters ago and held last chapter, resting on the fourth marked tick. Behind the

BACKSLIDE. The Safety-complete, Shine-complete, Straighten-complete, and Sort-complete milestones sit as
small passed slate markers further back. The GOAL crosshair, tagged purpose, still sits ahead on the
green calm side, because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain still follows.
