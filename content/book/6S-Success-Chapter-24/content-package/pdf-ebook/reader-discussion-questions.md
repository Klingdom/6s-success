# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

*Chapter 24, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter names the gap left by the last one: "A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself." Have you ever written down or agreed on a good way of doing something at home and then watched it get ignored for a week? What do you think was actually missing?

2. The chapter draws a hard line between knowing and doing: "knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it." Where in your home is the best way already known and written down, and yet still not what happens?

3. A written standard, the chapter says, fails in one of exactly three places: memory, timing, and ownership. Think of one household task that keeps not getting done. Which of the three is really at fault for it, and how do you know?

4. The hero of the chapter is three questions: "A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who." Pick a recurring task that matters to you and answer all three for it out loud. Which of the three answers is currently missing?

5. The definition box says giving a standard all three tools makes it "something the home does on its own." What is one standard in your home you would most like to run on its own, without leaning on your memory or your patience?

6. The checklist reframe turns the whole idea on its head: "A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them." Have you ever resisted a checklist because using one felt like admitting weakness? Does this change that?

7. The chapter insists a checklist should be short: "A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time." Think of a task you would list. Which few steps actually change the result, and which ones could you cut without anyone noticing?

8. The schedule reframe is blunt about timing: "A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis." What is one job in your home you only ever do once it has already become an emergency, and what small rhythm would have prevented it?

9. The chapter's trick for timing is to hang a task off something that already happens (the bins go out the night your show ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing). What existing rhythm in your week could you attach a recurring task to, so nobody has to remember to schedule it?

10. The responsibility reframe is a callback to Shine: "A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one." Where in your home is a task quietly nobody's, because everyone assumes someone else has it handled?

11. The chapter says how you attach the name matters: "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses." Where have you seen a chore that was handed down get quietly resented and half-done? What might letting the person choose have changed?

12. The worked example turns the after-dinner reset into "a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered." What is your household's version of the after-dinner reset, and what would its list, its time, and its named parts be?

13. The Quick Win is the whole chapter in five minutes: "Take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools... turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it." Which standard will you pick, and what are the three tools you would give it?

14. The Family Challenge points at choosing, not assigning: "Build one checklist together for a shared recurring job... and let each person choose the line they will own. A job you chose gets done; a job you were handed gets resented." What shared job could your household build together, and how would you let each person pick their part?

15. The Common Mistake is a warning against overbuilding: "The three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier. If a tool is adding work instead of removing worry, cut it back until it helps." Where might you be tempted to make a checklist too complete or a schedule too ambitious, and how would you keep it light?

16. The chapter reads the friction meter honestly: "The needle did not advance. The grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S." What does it mean to you that this chapter holds your gains more firmly rather than making a new one? As the book turns to Chapter 25 and the visual standard, which of your standards would you most like to make impossible to let slide?
