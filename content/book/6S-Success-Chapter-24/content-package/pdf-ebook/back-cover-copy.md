# Back Cover Copy: Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

## Back-cover text

Last chapter you did the crucial thing: you took the best way you knew to do a recurring task and wrote it on a card where anyone could follow it. Then, if you are honest, you watched it get ignored for a week. That is not a failure of the card. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself; knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. A written standard, on its own, is necessary and not enough, and it falls down in one of exactly three places: someone forgets a step, the work never gets a time and drifts to "later", or the task belongs to everyone and so to no one. Each of those three gaps has a tool that closes it. "A standard is the best way. A checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen."

Chapter 24 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the second chapter of Part Seven, and it continues Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept; this chapter gives every standard the three tools that turn it from a good idea on a card into something the home runs on its own. "To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own." And each tool is warmer than its reputation. A checklist is not an admission of weakness: "A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not." A schedule is not one more thing on a full week: "A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it." And a responsibility is not chores handed down: a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, so put one name per standard, chosen not imposed, because "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses."

Then the chapter puts all three in your hands at once, on a single standard, in about five minutes. Take the after-dinner reset: give it a four or five line checklist, hang it off a cue you already have (it starts the moment the dinner plates are cleared), and let each person choose the part they own. "It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass." Then go and look at the friction meter, and notice what it does not do: it does not move, because you cleared no new friction, you built tools for the standards you already had. But the small catch that appeared behind the needle last chapter is sturdier now, its single tooth become three. "The needle did not advance. The grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S." Keep every standard runnable with **The What-When-Who card**, and turn your standards into a running system with **The Home Checklist and Schedule template**. Next, Chapter 24 hands off to Chapter 25 · Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards, which builds the last kind of standard and completes the fifth S.
