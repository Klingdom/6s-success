## You wrote the standard. Then you watched it get ignored.

Last chapter you did the crucial thing: you took the best way you knew to do some recurring task and put it on a card where anyone could follow it. That was the right first move. And then, if you are honest, you watched it get ignored for a week. The card was fine. The problem is that a card is not enough.

A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself. Writing down the best way tells everyone what good looks like, but knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it. This chapter is the fix, and the fix is not more discipline; it is three small tools that share the load.

> A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself.

## Welcome back to Standardize, the fifth S.

Chapter 24 is the second chapter of Part Seven, and it continues Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. ## Why a good standard still does not get followed.

Be precise about how a good standard fails, because each way it fails points straight at the tool that fixes it. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places. The first is memory: the standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not from carelessness but because that is what human memory does under distraction, so two steps quietly get dropped. The second is timing: everyone means to do it, but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later", and "later" is where good intentions go to die. The third is ownership: the task belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it.

None of these three is a failure of the standard itself. The card was fine. What was missing was a way to carry it, and those three carriers are the checklist, the schedule, and the responsibility.

> A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who.

## Three questions, three tools.

The whole chapter fits in a box, and it is three plain questions with three plain answers.

> To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own.

Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

## The checklist: so nothing is forgotten.

Start with memory, because it is the one people are most embarrassed by and least need to be. A lot of us resist checklists at home out of a quiet sense that needing one is admitting a weakness. It is the opposite. The people who fly planes and operate on hearts do not trust their memory for the steps that matter, and their results got dramatically better the day they stopped.

> A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not.

A checklist is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. Keep it short and real: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words. "A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time."

## The schedule: so it actually happens.

Now timing, which is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient; it waits to become urgent. The gutter you never clean on a rhythm is the one you clean in the rain because it overflowed. "Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working."

> A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it.

The easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing. Attached to an existing rhythm, a task stops depending on anyone remembering to schedule it, because the schedule is already running.

## The responsibility: so someone owns it.

Last, ownership, the one most likely to cause a flicker of discomfort, because it sounds like assigning chores and standing over people. Held rightly, it is nothing of the sort. It is just making sure each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's, because a task that is everybody's is, reliably and always, nobody's.

> A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled.

But how you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace: you do not hand out the jobs, you let people choose them, because a job someone chose is a job they will actually do. Sit the household down, lay out the standards that need owners, and let people pick. "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses."

## Put the three together, in five minutes.

None of this is heavy, and the fastest way to feel that is to take a single standard and give it all three tools at once.

> Take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes: turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it. That is the difference between a standard that exists and a standard that happens.

Take the after-dinner reset, the light fifteen-minute pass you met back in Shine. The checklist is four or five lines: clear the table, wipe the counters, start the dishwasher, ten minutes of everyone putting their own things away, lights and lock. The schedule is a cue you already have, so the reset starts when the dinner plates are cleared, every night, no decision required. And the responsibilities are chosen, not handed down: one person has the kitchen, another the living room, the kids each have their own things, and the youngest, who asked, gets the very important job of the light switches. Written on one small card stuck to the side of the fridge:

> It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass.

## Not corporate. Lighter, not heavier.

One word about how this is meant to feel, because the subject usually sounds like rules and clipboards and it does not have to.

The three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier, to take the load off the one person who has been carrying it and share it around. A written standard that never got followed is the normal case, not a failing, and the fix is not nagging or discipline; it is a list, a time, and a name. Responsibilities are chosen, not handed down. And there is a simple test for whether you have gone too far: if a tool is adding work instead of removing worry, cut it back until it helps. The three tools free you; they are not a compliance system imposed on the household.

## Keep the tools light, not a chore.

One caution, so the three tools stay a relief and not one more burden.

> Making the checklist so complete it becomes a chore in itself, or the schedule so ambitious nobody keeps it. The three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier. If a tool is adding work instead of removing worry, cut it back until it helps.

The whole point is to take a load off, not to add one. Keep the checklist to the few steps that change the result, keep the schedule to a rhythm you can actually hold, and cut anything that is adding work rather than removing worry.
