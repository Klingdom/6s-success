# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

Last chapter you did the crucial thing: you took the best way you knew to do some recurring task and wrote it on a card where anyone could follow it. Then, if you are honest, you watched it get ignored for a week. That is not a failure of the card. Chapter 24 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where you fix it, and the fix is not more discipline or nagging; it is three small tools that share the load. As the chapter puts it, "A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself," because "knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it."

"Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities" is the second chapter of Part Seven, and it continues Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept, the best way you know today, written down; this chapter gives that written standard the three tools that turn it from a good idea on a card into something the home runs on its own. It uses the concept without re-teaching it, and it does one thing: it gives every standard its three tools, a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and a name for the who.

The chapter starts by being precise about how a good standard fails, because each way it fails points straight at the tool that fixes it. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places. The first is memory: the standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not from carelessness but because that is what human memory does under distraction, so two steps quietly get dropped. The second is timing: everyone means to do it, but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later", and "later" is where good intentions go to die. The third is ownership: the task belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it, and it sits undone in the center of everyone's blind spot. None of these three is a failure of the standard itself; the card was fine. What was missing was a way to carry it, and those three carriers are the checklist, the schedule, and the responsibility.

That is the hero, and it fits in a box: "To make a standard run without you, answer three questions. What: a checklist, so nothing is forgotten. When: a schedule, so the work has a time and happens on a rhythm. Who: a responsibility, so one name owns it. A standard with all three stops being a good idea on a card and becomes something the home does on its own." A checklist answers what. A schedule answers when. A responsibility answers who. Give a standard all three and it stops being a hopeful card on the fridge and becomes a small piece of machinery that runs whether or not you are thinking about it.

Then the chapter takes each tool in turn and takes the cold out of it. Start with the checklist, because it is the one people are most embarrassed by and least need to be. A lot of us resist checklists at home out of a quiet sense that needing one is admitting a weakness; it is the opposite. "A checklist is not a sign that you are forgetful. It is what lets a capable person do a complicated thing perfectly without holding all of it in their head. Pilots and surgeons live by them, and not because they are bad at their jobs, but because everyone forgets, and a list does not." It is not a crutch for the incompetent; it is how the competent stay reliable when they are tired, rushed, or interrupted, which at home is roughly always. Keep it short and real: the few steps that actually change the result, in plain words, because "A checklist you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not, every time."

Then the schedule, which is where more good standards die than anywhere else. A standard can be perfectly clear and perfectly owned and still never happen, simply because it never has a particular time, and a task with no time does not sit patiently waiting to be convenient; it waits to become urgent. The gutter you never clean on a rhythm is the one you clean in the rain because it overflowed; the fridge nobody wipes on a schedule is the one you empty in a hurry when something has gone off. "Upkeep is boring precisely because it is working." As the reframe has it, "A task with no time does not get done on a rhythm. It gets done in a panic, once it has become a crisis. A schedule is how you trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it." And the easiest way to give recurring work a time is not to invent a new slot in a full week but to hang the task off something that already happens: the bins go out the night the show you watch ends, the plants get watered when the Sunday coffee is brewing.

Then responsibility, the one most likely to cause a flicker of discomfort, because it sounds like assigning chores and standing over people. Held rightly, it is nothing of the sort; it is just making sure each standard has a name attached, so it is somebody's, because a task that is everybody's is, reliably and always, nobody's. "A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. You met that line back in Shine, and it is even truer of a standard: the fastest way to make sure something never happens is to leave it unowned, so that every person assumes some other person has it handled." But how you attach the name matters as much as that you do, and this is where a home is not a workplace: you do not hand out the jobs, you let people choose them, because a job someone chose is a job they will actually do. "What is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses."

Enough concept: the fastest way to feel how light this is, is to take a single standard and give it all three tools at once, which takes about five minutes. Take the after-dinner reset, the light fifteen-minute pass you met back in Shine. The checklist is four or five lines: clear the table, wipe the counters, start the dishwasher, ten minutes of everyone putting their own things away, lights and lock. The schedule is not a new appointment; it is a cue you already have, so the reset starts when the dinner plates are cleared, every night, no decision required. And the responsibilities are chosen, not handed down: one person has the kitchen, another the living room, the kids each have their own things, and the youngest, who asked, gets the very important job of the light switches. Written on one small card stuck to the side of the fridge, that is no longer a vague good intention. "It is a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts, and it will run on a tired Tuesday when nobody can be bothered, which is the only real test any of this has to pass."

Then go and look at the friction meter, and notice what it does not do: it does not move. That is honest rather than disappointing. You did not clear any new friction this chapter; you built tools for the standards you already had, so nothing left the rooms and the needle holds exactly where Chapter 23 left it. But look again at the small catch that appeared behind the needle last chapter, the one that keeps a gain from sliding back, because it is sturdier now. A written standard was the first thing that could hold a gain in place at all; a standard with a checklist, a schedule, and a name is a much stronger hold, because now the good way is not just recorded but carried, run on a rhythm, and owned by someone. Where last chapter's catch was a single tooth, this chapter has given it three. As the rule of the chapter has it, "A standard is the best way. A checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen." And on the meter: "The needle did not advance. The grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and that is precisely the work of this S."

That is Standardize nearly whole. Chapter 23 gave you the idea of a standard; this chapter gave you the three tools that turn a written standard into something that runs, and between them, a concept and its tools, you can take almost any recurring task in the home and make it hold its shape without leaning on your memory or your patience. There is one kind of standard left, the kind where all the words in the world work less well than a single picture, and building that visual standard is the next chapter, which completes the fifth S, with Sustain still to follow. One Idea to Keep: "A written standard is a good idea; a checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make it happen. Give every standard you care about a list so nothing is forgotten, a time so it gets done on a rhythm, and a name so someone owns it, and it will run without you."
