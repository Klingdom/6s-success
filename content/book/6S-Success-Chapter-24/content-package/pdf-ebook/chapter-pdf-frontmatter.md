# Checklists, Schedules, and Responsibilities

**Part Seven, Standardize**
Chapter 24 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

Subtitle · A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself. A standard actually runs when three things are true: someone knows exactly what to do, the work has a time, and someone owns it. This chapter gives every standard its three tools: a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and a name for the who.

---

## Chapter descriptor

The second chapter of Part Seven, and Standardize continues here, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept, the best way you know today written on a card; this chapter names the uncomfortable next thing, that the card, on its own, does not get followed. A standard is a recipe on a card, and a recipe on a card does not cook itself, because knowing what good looks like has never once been the same as doing it, and a written standard fails in one of exactly three places: memory, where steps get quietly dropped; timing, where the work has no particular moment and drifts to "later", where good intentions go to die; and ownership, where the task belongs to the household in general and so to no one in particular. None of those is a failure of the standard itself; what was missing was a way to carry it, and there are three carriers. This chapter delivers the hero, the three tools a standard needs to run without you, each the answer to one plain question. A checklist answers what, so nothing is forgotten, and it is not a crutch for the incompetent but how the competent stay reliable when tired, rushed, or interrupted, the way pilots and surgeons work, kept short and real. A schedule answers when, because a task with no time does not wait patiently to be convenient, it waits to become urgent, so you hang the work off a cue that already happens and trade the occasional emergency for the small, dull, ordinary upkeep that quietly prevents it. A responsibility answers who, because a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, the callback to Shine, so you put one name per standard, chosen not handed down, because what is chosen gets done and what is imposed gets subverted. Then the chapter puts all three on one standard at once, the after-dinner reset, which becomes a fifteen-minute machine with a list, a time, and named parts that runs on a tired Tuesday, and reads the friction meter as a hold that does not move because no new friction was cleared, while the catch behind the needle from last chapter is reinforced, its single tooth now three, before the hand-off to Chapter 25 and the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list.

## How to read this chapter

This is the second chapter of Part Seven, Standardize, and it continues the fifth S. It picks up exactly where Chapter 23 left off: you have grasped the concept of a standard and, ideally, written one, put the best way you know onto a card where anyone could follow it. This chapter uses that concept without re-teaching it, and answers the question the card raises the moment you tape it up, which is why, so often, nobody follows it. Read it through once for the idea, that a standard needs three tools to run without you, then do the one small, concrete thing it asks: take a standard you already wrote and give it a checklist, a schedule, and a name.

The chapter rests on a plain and slightly humbling observation. Writing the best way down was the crucial first move, and it is not, by itself, enough, as you may already have discovered if you wrote one standard and then watched it get ignored for a week. A recipe on a card does not cook itself. Watch a written standard that nobody follows and it falls down in one of exactly three places: memory, where the standard has six steps and the person doing it remembers four, not from carelessness but because that is what memory does under distraction; timing, where everyone means to do it but it has no particular moment attached, so it drifts to "later"; and ownership, where the task belongs to the household in general, which means it belongs to no one in particular, so each person assumes another has it. Each of those three needs has a tool that closes it, and the chapter is those three tools and how to put them together. The word to keep in mind throughout is light: the three tools are meant to make a standard lighter, not heavier, to take a load off the one person carrying it and share it, and a tool that adds work instead of removing worry should be cut back until it helps. Responsibilities are chosen, not handed down, because what is chosen gets done; what is imposed gets subverted, slowly, and usually with excellent excuses.

A word on the scope fence, so the chapter stays the thing that carries a standard into motion rather than drifting into the rest of the part or the S that follows. It works entirely in text-based tools, lists, times, and names, and it uses Chapter 23's concept of a standard without re-teaching it. It deliberately does not build the visual and pictorial standards, the photo of the ideal state, the simple storyboard, the picture that shows the right result at a glance, which are Chapter 25, the chapter that completes the fifth S. It does not build the long-term adherence, audit, and habit-as-a-system work, or the daily 6S-time ritual, that keeps everyone doing all of it over years, which is Sustain, Chapters 26 to 30; it may note that a standard becomes habit through repetition, but it does not build that system. And it does not re-open Sort, Straighten, Shine, or Safety; a one-line callback is fine, and the responsibility reframe deliberately echoes the "a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one" line you met in Shine, but the earlier S's are not re-taught here. Because building tools for standards you already had clears nothing new from the rooms, the friction meter holds exactly where Chapter 23 left it and does not move, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. But the small catch that appeared behind the needle last chapter, the one that keeps a gain from sliding back, is sturdier now, because a standard that is carried, run on a rhythm, and owned by someone is far harder to let slide than a standard alone; where last chapter's catch was a single tooth, this chapter gives it three. The needle did not advance; the grip on everything behind it got noticeably tighter, and the goal is still ahead on the calm side, because one piece of Standardize remains and Sustain still follows. The register throughout is warm, human, and freeing, never corporate: the three tools are a way to set down the weight of being the only one who knows how the home works, not a compliance system imposed on the household, and there is no shame in a written standard that never got followed, because that is the normal case, not a failing.

If you only do one thing: take the one standard you wrote last chapter and give it the three tools in five minutes, turn it into a short checklist, hang it off a time or an existing cue, and put one name against it. That is the difference between a standard that exists and a standard that happens, and it is the smallest possible proof of the whole chapter. A standard is the best way. A checklist, a schedule, and a name are what make the best way actually happen.
