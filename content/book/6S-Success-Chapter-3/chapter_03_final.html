Part One · Discovering 6S

Chapter Three

The Six Steps That Transform Any Space

One slow walk around the whole loop, from the first decision to the last habit, on a single small cabinet under a sink.

The cabinet under your kitchen sink did not become a disaster on purpose. Nobody decided one morning to store the dish soap next to a dead flashlight, a bottle of something blue from a previous decade, and a sponge that has clearly given up on life.

It happened the way these things always happen. One item at a time, each one perfectly reasonable, none of them ever leaving. You reach in for a clean sponge and your hand finds a damp bag of rags instead. A bottle tips. Something rolls to the back, into the dark, where the spare bottles go to be forgotten. And somewhere under all of it, though you do not know this yet, a slow drip has been quietly warping the wood for months.

Here is what I want you to notice. That cabinet is not dirty, exactly, and it is not even that full. The problem is not the amount of stuff. The problem is that nothing in there has a job, a home, or a reason. The cabinet stopped being storage and became a place where things land. Most of the trouble spots in a home are like this. Not filthy. Not overflowing. Just feral. Quietly running themselves, badly, while you are busy living your life.

In the last two chapters you learned what 6S is and where it came from. Now we use it. But before we slow down and spend a whole part of this book on each step, I want to walk you through all six at once, in order, on a single small space. We are going to fix this cabinet together, from the first decision to the last habit, so you can see the shape of the whole method before you practice any one piece of it.

Think of it as a lap around the track before the real training begins. One slow, complete lap, so your hands learn where the corners are.

6S Tip

The order is not bureaucracy. It is closer to physics. You cannot give something a home until you know it is staying, and you cannot see what a space is hiding until it is empty and clean.

In this chapter

  • How to walk one small space of your own through all six steps in fifteen minutes.

The whole shapeOne Loop, Walked Once

People new to 6S often treat the six S's like a menu. They like the sound of Sort, they enjoy a good Shine, and they quietly skip the ones that sound like homework. This never works, and the reason is simple. The steps are not six separate chores that happen to start with the same letter. They are a chain. Each one sets up the next.

You cannot Straighten a space until you have Sorted it, because giving a home to something you are about to throw away is wasted effort. You cannot truly Shine until you have Straightened, because you cannot clean around a pile. You will miss half the hazards in a room until you have Shined it, because the dangerous things hide behind the clutter and the grime. And there is no point standardizing an arrangement you have not yet made good. Do the steps out of order and you feel like you are working hard while the space fights you the whole way.

Do them in order and something better happens. Each step makes the next one easier. The work compounds. By the time you reach the end, the space almost finishes itself.

That is the secret this chapter is really about. Not the six words, which you already know, but the handoffs between them. The quiet moment where Sort passes Straighten an empty space to work in. The moment where Shine hands Safety a clear view of the danger. Learn to feel those handoffs and you will never again wonder which step comes next or why. So here is the whole thing on one page, arranged as a loop, because the last step feeds back into the first.

The loop in one line

Sort makes room. Straighten gives things a home. Shine reveals the truth. Safety protects the people. Standardize makes the good way the easy way. Sustain keeps it alive. Then it goes around again.

Step oneSort: Make Room

Open both cabinet doors. Now take everything out. Not some of it. All of it, onto a towel on the floor where you can see every single item in daylight.

This is Sort, and the towel is the whole trick. Inside the cabinet, surrounded by its neighbors, a crusty spray bottle looks like it belongs. Out on the towel, alone, it has to answer one honest question: do I actually use this? Most of what comes out of an under-sink cabinet cannot pass that test. The cleaner you bought for a stain you no longer remember. The second, third, and fourth partially used bottles of nearly the same thing. The bucket with a crack. The rag bag that has tripled in size through some process nobody witnessed.

Sort splits everything into three piles, and only three. Things you keep, because you really use them. Things that belong somewhere else, like the screwdriver that migrated here from the garage. And things that are simply done, headed for the trash, the recycling, or a donation box. You are not organizing yet. You are deciding. Keep, relocate, or release.

Here is why Sort has to come first. Every later step costs effort, and you do not want to spend that effort on things that should not be here at all. There is no sense cleaning under a bottle you are about to throw out, or buying a nice container to hold junk. Sort is the step that makes every other step smaller. An hour spent sorting can save you three hours of straightening, shining, and storing things you never needed to keep. And notice what Sort just handed the next step: an empty cabinet. Space. Room to think.

Three baskets, three outcomes, and no fourth option. The pile-for-later is not a decision, it is a decision postponed, and it is how the cabinet got this way in the first place.

Common Mistake

Buying bins before you sort. The container aisle is step two's reward, not step one's shortcut. Bins bought too early just give your clutter a nicer place to live, and you end up storing things you should have let go.

Step twoStraighten: Give It a Home

Look at what survived Sort. For a typical under-sink cabinet it is surprisingly little. The dish soap you use daily. One all-purpose cleaner. A sponge or two. A box of trash bags. Maybe a brush and a pair of gloves. That is often the whole keep pile, and it used to be buried under three times its volume in things that did not matter.

Straighten gives each survivor a deliberate home, and the rule that decides those homes is frequency of use. The things you reach for every day go in the easiest spot, right at the front where your hand lands without looking. The things you touch once a month move toward the back. This is the difference between a space that is merely tidy and a space that is genuinely easy. Tidy means everything looks lined up. Easy means the thing you need most is the thing you reach first.

A small caddy or a shallow bin earns its place now, and only now, because now you know exactly what it has to hold. Put the daily soap and the daily cleaner in the caddy at the front. Stand the trash bags where you can pull one without excavating. And leave the back corner clear, which matters more than you might guess, because that clear corner is where the next step is about to find something.

If you want a single rule for where things land, use this one. Your cabinet has prime real estate and cheap real estate, and the only question is what deserves the good spots.

Step threeShine: Clean Until It Talks

Now wipe it out. The cabinet is nearly empty, so this takes two minutes. A cloth, a little cleaner, along the floor of the cabinet and up the sides. If Shine were only about cleanliness, that would be the end of it, and it would be the least interesting step in the book. But Shine is not really about clean. Shine is about inspection.

The act of cleaning a surface is also the act of running your eyes and hands across every inch of it, slowly, paying attention. That is when a space finally tells you the truth it was hiding. Watch what happens as the cloth reaches that clear back corner. The wood there is darker than the rest. A little soft. A faint ring stains the board, and when you press it, it gives. That is the slow drip you never knew about, the one the clutter was politely concealing for months. You did not find it because you went looking for leaks. You found it because you were cleaning, slowly, with your hands already there. A plumber would have charged you to discover this. Your sponge found it for free.

This is the heart of Shine, and it is the part almost everyone misses. We are trained to clean fast, to make the surface look acceptable and move on. But cleaning done at speed tells you nothing. Cleaning done slowly, with attention, is an inspection you happen to be performing while also wiping up crumbs. The loose hinge, the rust starting on the pipe, the crack spreading along the back: a space hands all of this to the person who cleans it carefully.

Go slowly here, and look at what a slow wipe turns up.

Common Mistake

Cleaning fast to get it over with. Clean slowly enough to notice. The leak, the crumb trail, the loose hinge, the rust ring: Shine is the moment a space finally tells you the truth.

Step fourSafety: Now Look Low

Stay crouched in front of the cabinet for a moment, because you are at exactly the right height to see the thing you have been walking past for years. That bottle of drain cleaner, the one with the warnings all over it, sits on the cabinet floor at the front. At your eye level, kneeling, it is unremarkable. But you are kneeling, which means you are looking at the cabinet from roughly the height of a two-year-old. From down here, the drain cleaner is not in storage. It is on display, at grabbing height, behind a door that does not lock, a few feet from where small people play.

This is Safety, our fourth S, and notice that you could not have seen this clearly until right now. When the cabinet was full, the dangerous bottle was just one more thing in the chaos. It took Sort to clear the clutter, Straighten to bring order, and Shine to give you a clean, open, honest view before the hazard could announce itself. Safety rides on the back of the first three steps. That is why it sits in the middle of the loop and not tacked on at the end.

So you fix what you found. The drain cleaner and anything else that can burn, poison, or cut goes up high, out of a child's reach, ideally behind a latch. A simple cabinet latch costs about a dollar and takes a minute to install. The slow leak you found during Shine goes on a short list to repair before it rots the cabinet floor through. None of this is dramatic. It is a bottle moved up and a latch clicked shut. But a moved bottle is a real safety improvement, and you only made it because the first three steps set it up. At work, somebody is paid to spot these hazards. At home, the inspector is you.

Quick Win

Open one low cabinet and crouch down to a child's height. Anything down there that could burn, poison, or cut belongs up high. Moving one bottle is a real Safety step, done in under a minute.

Step fiveStandardize: Make the Good Way the Easy Way

The cabinet looks great. Here is the uncomfortable truth: it will not stay that way on its own, and willpower is not the answer. The arrangement you just built lives only in your head right now, and your head is busy. The moment you are tired, or a houseguest puts something away, or three weeks pass, the cabinet starts drifting back toward feral.

Standardize is how you stop the drift, and it is not about discipline. It is about making the good arrangement so obvious that doing it right is easier than doing it wrong. The caddy you placed at the front already does some of this work, because now there is a clear, single answer to the question "where does the dish soap go." You can do more with about thirty seconds of effort. Take a quick photo of the finished cabinet, print it small, and tape it inside the door. Now the standard is not a memory. It is a picture anyone can match, including future you at the end of a long day, and including the family member who has genuinely never known where anything goes.

That photo turns a private decision into a visible standard. Before, the right arrangement existed only when you were paying attention. Now it lives on the door, in plain sight, where it can correct a drifting cabinet without you saying a word. This is the visual workplace idea from Chapter 2, shrunk down to one cabinet door. Make normal visible, so abnormal becomes obvious, so problems get caught early.

Step sixSustain: Keep It Alive

One step left, and it is the one that decides whether any of this lasted or whether you just did a satisfying thing once. Sustain is keeping the gain, and the mistake people make is imagining it requires a big recurring effort. It does not. A standard that needs a weekly overhaul to survive was a bad standard. A good one needs only tiny, almost invisible maintenance.

For the cabinet, Sustain might be a single habit: every time you pull out the spray bottle, you take ten seconds to put back anything that wandered before you close the door. That is it. Ten seconds, attached to something you already do, so you never have to remember it as a separate task. Add one slightly bigger check on a slow rhythm. Once a month, when you are already under there, glance at the back corner where the leak was. Dry? Good. Damp again? Then the repair did not hold, and you would much rather learn that from a thirty-second glance than from a rotted cabinet floor a year from now.

Here is the part that makes 6S a loop instead of a line. Sustain does not lead to a tidy ending. It leads back to Sort. Over months, new things drift into even the best cabinet, needs change, the daily cleaner gets replaced by a different one. So every so often the ten-second resets are not enough, and the space quietly asks for a fresh, full lap. Not because you failed, but because a home is alive and its contents keep changing. Sustain hands you back to Sort, a little wiser, and the loop goes around again.

Before we talk about keeping it, look at what there is to keep.

And this is what keeping it actually costs.

Family Challenge

Everyone picks one small space and walks it through all six steps this week. At dinner, each person reports the single thing Shine revealed: the hidden leak, the expired jar, the long-lost remote. Best discovery wins.

The real lessonHow Each Step Hands Off the Next

Step back and look at what actually happened, because the magic was never in any single step. It was in the chain. Sort cleared the cabinet, which gave Straighten the empty space it needed. Straighten arranged what stayed and opened up clear surfaces, which gave Shine a space it could actually clean and inspect. Shine wiped everything down and revealed the hidden leak and the open view, which gave Safety a clear sight line to every danger. Safety moved the poison up and latched the door, which made the arrangement genuinely sound, which made it worth locking in. Standardize turned that sound arrangement into an obvious default, which finally gave Sustain something simple and stable to protect. And Sustain kept the whole thing alive until the day it quietly hands you back to Sort.

Pull any link out of that chain and the next one struggles. Skip Sort and you straighten junk. Skip Shine and you miss the leak and the hazard both. Skip Standardize and the space drifts back the first busy week. The order is not a preference. It is the reason the work compounds instead of fighting you.

This is also the secret to fixing a space that will not stay fixed. When a drawer keeps sliding back into chaos no matter how many times you redo it, you have not failed at willpower. You skipped a step. A drawer that will not stay neat was usually never truly sorted, so it is still full of things with no reason to be there. Find the skipped step, and the space stops fighting you.

6S Tip

If a space keeps sliding back, look for the step you skipped. A drawer that will not stay neat was usually never truly sorted. A standard that nobody follows usually skipped the part where you made the right way the easy way.

Your turnYour First Lap

You have now watched the entire method work on a cabinet. The fastest way to make it yours is to stop watching and take one lap yourself, today, on a space small enough that you cannot lose. Do not start with the garage. Do not start with the closet that haunts you. Start with one drawer, one shelf, or, if you want the full experience, the cabinet under your own kitchen sink.

Pick something you can finish in fifteen minutes, because the goal of your first lap is not a transformed home. The goal is to feel all six handoffs once, in your own hands, on your own stuff. That feeling is what turns six words you understand into a method you can use. It will not be perfect, and it does not need to be. One full, imperfect lap teaches your hands the whole shape of the method, and the next space will go faster because of it. Perfect Sorting and nothing else is just a very tidy pile of unfinished work.

One small space, six steps

  • Empty it. Keep, relocate, or remove each item. (Sort)
  • Put back only what stays, easy things in front. (Straighten)
  • Wipe it out, and notice what the mess was hiding. (Shine)
  • Crouch low. Move anything dangerous up and away. (Safety)
  • Make the good arrangement obvious for next time. (Standardize)
  • Pick one tiny habit that protects it. (Sustain)
Quick Win

Set a fifteen-minute timer and point it at one drawer, one shelf, or the cabinet under your own sink. Do not aim for perfect. Aim for one full lap. The first lap is the one that teaches your hands the whole method.

In closingThe Needle Moves

You came into this chapter with six words you understood and a cabinet you were quietly avoiding. You leave it having watched those words do real work, in order, each one handing off to the next, until a feral little space under a sink became calm, clean, safe, and easy to keep.

That is the whole method. Everything else in this book is detail, depth, and practice. The room-by-room playbooks later on are not new methods. They are this same loop, walked through a kitchen, a garage, a closet, a kid's room, with tips for the quirks of each. Learn the lap once, and the rest of the book becomes a set of friendly suggestions rather than a stack of new rules.

So if you have not already, go take your first lap. One small space, fifteen minutes, all six steps. The needle on our meter is about to move for the first time, just a little, because you finally moved some actual objects. It is a small space, so it is a small move. The bigger moves start in the next chapter, when we choose a target worth winning.

One Idea to Keep

Each step makes the next one possible. That is why 6S is an order, not a menu. You do not pick your favorite three. You walk the loop, and the loop does the work.
