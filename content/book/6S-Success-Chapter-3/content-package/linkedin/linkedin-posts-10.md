## 1. The feral cabinet

The cabinet under your sink did not become a disaster on purpose.

Nobody decided to store dish soap next to a dead flashlight and a sponge that gave up on life. It happened one item at a time, each one reasonable, none of them ever leaving.

Here is the thing. That cabinet is not even that full. The problem is not the amount of stuff. The problem is that nothing in there has a job, a home, or a reason. It stopped being storage and became a place where things land.

Not filthy. Not overflowing. Just feral, quietly running itself badly while you live your life.

Most trouble spots at home are exactly this.

## 2. A loop, not a menu

Most people treat the six steps of 6S like a menu.

They like the sound of Sort. They enjoy a good Shine. They quietly skip the ones that sound like homework.

It never works, and the reason is simple. The steps are not six separate chores that happen to start with the same letter. They are a chain. Each one sets up the next.

You cannot give something a home until you know it is staying. You cannot see what a space is hiding until it is empty and clean.

The order is not bureaucracy. It is closer to physics.

So you do not pick your favorite three. You walk the loop, and the loop does the work. That is the heart of Chapter 3, free in the online book.

## 3. The handoffs are the real lesson

The secret of 6S is not the six words. You already know those.

It is the handoffs between them.

Sort clears the cabinet, which gives Straighten an empty space to work in. Straighten opens clear surfaces, which gives Shine something it can actually inspect. Shine reveals the hidden danger, which gives Safety a clear view of it.

Each step hands the next one something it could not have done alone.

Do them in order and the work compounds. By the end, the space almost finishes itself.

Learn to feel those handoffs and you will never wonder which step comes next.

## 4. Sort makes everything smaller

Here is why Sort has to come first.

Every later step costs effort. You do not want to spend that effort on things that should not be there at all. There is no sense cleaning under a bottle you are about to throw out, or buying a nice container to hold junk.

Sort is the step that makes every other step smaller.

An hour spent sorting can save you three hours of straightening, shining, and storing things you never needed to keep.

You are not organizing yet. You are deciding. Keep, relocate, or release.

## 5. Buy the bins after, not before

The most common mistake in organizing: buying bins before you sort.

The container aisle is a reward for step two, not a shortcut for step one. Bins bought too early just give your clutter a nicer place to live. You end up storing things you should have let go.

Sort first. Decide what actually stays. Then, and only then, buy a container to fit a decision you already made.

A caddy earns its place once you know exactly what it has to hold. Not a minute before.

## 6. Tidy is not the same as easy

There is a difference between a space that is tidy and a space that is easy.

Tidy means everything looks lined up.

Easy means the thing you need most is the thing you reach first.

That is what Straighten is really for. You give each thing a home based on how often you use it. Daily things go to the front, where your hand lands without looking. Monthly things move toward the back.

A lined-up cabinet looks nice in a photo. An easy cabinet quietly saves you a small fight every single day.

Aim for easy.

## 7. Shine is inspection, not cleaning

Shine is not really about clean. Shine is about inspection.

Here is what I mean. I once watched someone wipe out a nearly empty cabinet under a sink. Two minute job. As the cloth reached the back corner, the wood was darker. A little soft. A faint ring, and when they pressed it, it gave.

A slow leak. Quietly warping the wood for months, hidden the whole time by the clutter.

They did not find it by looking for leaks. They found it because they were cleaning slowly, with their hands already there.

A plumber would have charged to discover that. Their sponge found it for free.

## 8. When you do Safety, look low

When you check a space for safety, get down low.

Crouch in front of a cabinet and you are suddenly at the height of a two-year-old. From down there, the bottle of drain cleaner is not in storage. It is on display, at grabbing height, behind a door that does not lock.

You walk past it standing up every day and never see it. You only see it kneeling.

The fix is small and real. Move anything that can burn, poison, or cut up high. A cabinet latch costs about a dollar and clicks shut in a minute.

At work, somebody is paid to spot these hazards. At home, the inspector is you.

## 9. Put the standard on the door

Your newly organized cabinet will not stay organized on its own. Willpower is not the answer.

So here is a thirty-second trick. Take a photo of the finished cabinet, print it small, and tape it inside the door.

Now the right arrangement is not living in your head, where your head is busy. It is a picture anyone can match. Future you at the end of a long day. The family member who has genuinely never known where anything goes.

The photo turns a private decision into a visible standard. It corrects a drifting cabinet without you saying a word.

Make normal visible, so abnormal becomes obvious.

## 10. Sustain loops back to Sort

People imagine the last step of 6S is the heavy one. A weekly overhaul to keep everything perfect.

It is the opposite. A standard that needs a weekly overhaul was a bad standard. A good one needs almost nothing.

Ten seconds when you close the cabinet to put back anything that wandered. A monthly glance at the corner where the leak was.

And here is the part that makes 6S a loop instead of a line. Sustain does not lead to a tidy ending. It leads back to Sort. A home is alive. Its contents keep changing. Every so often the space quietly asks for a fresh lap.

Not because you failed. Because you live there.
