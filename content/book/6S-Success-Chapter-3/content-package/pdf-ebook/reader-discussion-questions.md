# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions

*Chapter 3, The Six Steps That Transform Any Space. Ten open-ended questions grounded in the chapter.*

---

1. The chapter describes a cabinet that "went feral" one reasonable item at a time, and notes it is not dirty or even that full. The real problem is that nothing in it has a job, a home, or a reason. Which space in your own home fits that description, and what makes it feel feral rather than just full?

2. The chapter argues that the order of the six steps is "closer to physics" than bureaucracy. Which of the six steps would be hardest for you to do in order, and which one are you most tempted to skip ahead to or skip entirely?

3. The book says the real subject of the chapter is not the six words but the handoffs between them. Walk through one handoff in your own words. What does one step give the next that it could not have done alone?

4. Sort splits everything into only three piles: keep, relocate, release. The chapter insists you are "deciding, not organizing yet." Why do you think people find deciding harder than organizing, and where do you tend to get stuck?

5. Shine is described as inspection, not just cleaning. A slow wipe finds a hidden leak, and the chapter says "your sponge found it for free." When has a slow, careful clean ever shown you something you would have missed by cleaning fast?

6. The Safety step has the reader crouch to a toddler's height and notice the drain cleaner at grabbing level. What changes when you look at a familiar space from a completely different height or point of view, and what might you find if you tried it at home?

7. Standardize uses a photo taped inside the cabinet door so the right arrangement is visible instead of remembered. Where in your home would a simple visible standard help, and who else would it help besides you?

8. Sustain is described as a ten-second reset and a monthly glance, not a weekly overhaul. The chapter says a standard that needs a big recurring effort to survive was a bad standard. What tiny habit could you attach to something you already do, so it sticks without willpower?

9. The chapter offers a diagnosis for any space that will not stay fixed: find the step you skipped. Think of a space you have organized more than once that drifted back. Which step do you suspect you skipped, and what would doing it properly look like?

10. The closing idea is that 6S is "an order, not a menu," and the first lap is meant to be one full, imperfect pass rather than a perfect Sort. Why might a complete imperfect lap teach you more than perfecting a single step, and how does that change the way you would start?
