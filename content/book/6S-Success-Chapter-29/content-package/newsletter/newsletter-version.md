# Newsletter Edition: Daily 6S Times

**Subject line (placeholder):** Keeping fails when it depends on remembering. Give it a fixed time in the day instead, anchored to a moment that already happens, and it runs on the rhythm of the day rather than on your memory.

**Preview text (placeholder):** Stop asking your memory to keep the home, and hang the small upkeep on moments that already happen in the day.

---

Hi friend,

The last three issues handed you real ways to keep a home: the mindset that a home is kept and not finished, the friendly audit that catches drift while it is small, and the shared way to solve the problems that keep coming back. Held together, they are most of what keeping takes. But this issue is about the one weakness they all share, and it is a big one: every single one of them still depends on somebody remembering to do it. **And remembering, it turns out, is the least reliable tool in the whole box.** It works beautifully on the calm, well-slept days a home barely needs keeping, and it fails, predictably, on the tired, overwhelmed days a home most wants to slide. This chapter removes the remembering.

**The reason your good intentions keep failing is not that you are forgetful. It is that you are asking your memory to do a job the day should be doing. A keeping-habit tied to a fixed time in the day happens on the day you would have forgotten, which is the only day it ever really mattered.**

That is the whole turn. You have not been failing to keep your home; you have been running a keeping-plan with a single point of failure, your memory. So you stop hanging keeping on your memory and start hanging it on the day.

Your day is already full of things that happen without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the plates being cleared, the lights going off at night. Each one can serve as an anchor. **The easiest new habit is one you never have to start, because it is bolted to an old one that already runs without fail. You do not need to remember to reset the kitchen after dinner if the reset simply is part of after dinner, the way clearing the plates already is.** You bolt a small act of keeping onto one of these moments, and the reliable old habit drags the small new one along behind it. The keeping is no longer a separate thing you might forget; it is a passenger on a trip the day was already taking.

So the practice has a name, and here is the definition to hold on to.

**A daily 6S time is a short, shared moment of keeping, anchored to something that already happens in the day, so the small upkeep of the home runs on the day's rhythm rather than on anyone's memory. A couple of them, a few minutes each, are enough to keep a home that is already good.**

It is genuinely modest, and that is the point: not a schedule, not a rota, not a wall planner covered in coloured blocks, just a couple of short moments hooked to things that were going to happen anyway. Three qualities decide whether one survives contact with real life, and if you drop any one of them the plan quietly dies.

**What makes a daily time work: keep it short, a few minutes with a hard stop; anchor it to a moment that already happens, so no one has to remember; and do it together, so it is quick and shared rather than one person's chore.**

Short, because length breeds avoidance: a two-minute reset gets done on a bad night, a twenty-minute one gets postponed to a better night that never comes. Anchored, so it needs no memory. Together, because a shared time is faster and lighter and belongs to the whole household rather than one weary pair of hands. And these small times are quietly the most important of the three keeping-practices, because they are the ones that run every single day.

**The audit catches drift and problem-solving removes what keeps returning, but the daily times are the quiet hum underneath both: the small, constant upkeep that means the home never drifts far enough to need catching or solving in the first place. This is the rhythm that finally makes keeping automatic.**

Here is what a whole day of it looks like, folded in. On a normal weekday, one keeping-time is bolted to the morning leaving: for two minutes before the door shuts, everyone clears the launch pad and the breakfast surface, and then they go, and the day runs untouched. In the evening, one more is bolted to clearing up after dinner: a short shared reset of the common rooms, then it stops and the evening is free. Two small times, a handful of minutes, each riding a moment that happened anyway. Nobody had to remember either one, because the day remembered them. **That is a home keeping itself, and it looks like almost nothing, which is exactly how you know it will last.**

**To try this week:** Pick one thing that already happens in your day without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the lights going off at night, and bolt one two-minute keeping-act to it, starting today. You will never have to remember it again, because the day will remember it for you. Then, when you are ready, choose your household's daily times together, and do them together, everyone at once for a few minutes rather than one person for a long time. Four people resetting for three minutes is a whole reset, and it belongs to the family instead of resting on one pair of hands. And if a time keeps getting skipped, it is too long or badly anchored, not a sign that you lack discipline.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, and this time it comes to rest very close to the goal.

**The needle takes its third Sustain step, and it is nearly at the goal now. A home whose keeping runs on the rhythm of the day, rather than on anyone remembering, is a home that mostly keeps itself, which is very nearly the whole of what this book has been walking toward. Only a sliver of the gap remains, and the final chapter, the return to the start of the loop, closes it.**

It is a small step in distance and a large one in meaning. Small, because you have only bolted a couple of tiny times to the day; large, because those times are the engine that lets the whole of keeping run without effort. The mindset held the needle steady and revealed the gap; the audit took the first Sustain step; solving the recurring problems took the second; this chapter takes the third. It is a small move along the last-gap path, not a milestone. The catch that Standardize locked behind the needle still holds it against sliding back, and the goal is reached only at the very end, when all six S's are done.

You now have the mindset, the audit, the way to solve recurring problems, and now the rhythm that carries all of it, so the home holds its shape without anyone having to remember, decide, or nag. That is very nearly a home that keeps itself, which is the thing this whole book set out to build. One piece remains, and it is the next chapter. For now, hold on to the one idea this whole practice rests on.

**Stop asking your memory to keep the home. Bolt a couple of short, shared keeping-times to moments that already happen in your day, and the upkeep runs on the rhythm of the day itself. Do that, and keeping a home stops being something you remember to do and becomes simply part of how the day goes.**

Which is the same thing said more simply: **do not ask your memory to keep the home. Hand the job to the day.**
