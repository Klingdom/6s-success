## SHORT 1: Stop asking your memory to keep the home
**Topic: the hero fix, keeping fails when it depends on remembering. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** If you keep meaning to stay on top of the house and keep falling behind, it is not that you are forgetful. You are asking your memory to do a job the day should be doing.

**Script:**
You have the mindset now, the friendly audit that catches drift while it is small, and the shared way to solve the problems that keep coming back. Held together, that is most of what keeping takes. But they all share one weakness: every one of them still depends on somebody remembering to do it. And remembering is the least reliable tool in the whole box. It works on the calm, well-slept days a home barely needs keeping, and it fails, completely and predictably, on the tired, overwhelmed days a home most wants to slide. Here is the fix, and it is the whole chapter. You stop hanging keeping on your memory and start hanging it on the day. Keeping fails when it depends on remembering. Give it a fixed time in the day instead, anchored to a moment that already happens, and it runs on the rhythm of the day rather than on your memory.

## SHORT 2: Bolt it to something that already runs
**Topic: the anchor reframe and the definition. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The easiest new habit is one you never have to start, because it is bolted to an old one that already runs without fail.

**Script:**
Here is how you build a keeping-plan that needs no remembering, and it rests on one small trick. Your day is already full of things that happen without anyone deciding to make them happen: the kettle boils, the school run leaves, the dishes get cleared, the lights go off. Each can be an anchor. You take a small act of keeping and bolt it onto one of these moments, so the reliable old habit drags the small new one along behind it. You do not have to remember to wipe the counter, because wiping the counter is now just the last thing that happens while the coffee brews. The keeping is no longer a separate thing you might forget; it is a passenger on a trip the day was already taking. A daily 6S time is a short, shared moment of keeping, anchored to something that already happens in the day, so the small upkeep of the home runs on the day's rhythm rather than on anyone's memory. A couple of them, a few minutes each, are enough to keep a home that is already good.

## SHORT 3: Short, anchored, together
**Topic: the three qualities that make a daily time survive. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Three small things decide whether a daily keeping-time survives real life. Drop any one of them, and the plan quietly dies.

**Script:**
So what makes a daily time actually stick? Three qualities, and you need all three. Short, because length breeds avoidance: a two-minute reset gets done on a bad night, a twenty-minute one gets postponed to a better night that never comes. So keep it genuinely tiny, a few minutes with a hard stop, and resist every urge to make it thorough. Anchored, bolted to a moment that already happens, so it needs no memory and survives the tired days. And together, because a shared time is faster and lighter: four people tidying for three minutes is a full reset that costs each person almost nothing and belongs to the whole household rather than one weary pair of hands. Get all three right and the daily time becomes so light and so automatic that skipping it would feel odder than doing it. What makes a daily time work: keep it short, a few minutes with a hard stop; anchor it to a moment that already happens, so no one has to remember; and do it together, so it is quick and shared rather than one person's chore.

- 30s: "Anchored. So no one has to remember."
- 44s: "Together. Quick and shared, not one person's chore."

## SHORT 4: A whole day of keeping, folded in
**Topic: the worked day, two short anchored times. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Here is what a whole day of keeping looks like when it rides on the day itself. It looks like almost nothing.

**Script:**
Let me show you the whole thing in an ordinary weekday, because the point is how little it disturbs one. In the morning, in the flurry of everyone getting out of the door, one keeping-time is bolted to the leaving: for the two minutes before the door shuts, everyone puts their own things back at the launch pad and clears the breakfast surface, and then they go, and the home is left calm rather than strewn. Then the day runs, untouched, with nobody thinking about the house at all. And in the evening, one more time is bolted to clearing up after dinner: while the plates are already being cleared, the common rooms get a short shared reset, everyone to a zone for a few minutes, then it stops and the evening is free. Two small times, a handful of minutes total, each riding a moment that happened anyway. Nobody had to remember either one, because the day remembered them. That is a home keeping itself, and it looks like almost nothing, which is exactly how you know it will last.
