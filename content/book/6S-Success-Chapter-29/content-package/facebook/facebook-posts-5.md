## Post 1 · Stop asking your memory to keep the house

If you keep promising yourself you will stay on top of the house and it keeps sliding anyway, this is for you. You are not failing. You are running a keeping-plan with a single point of failure, your memory.

"And remembering, it turns out, is the least reliable tool in the whole box."

Memory works beautifully on the calm, well-slept days, which are exactly the days a home does not need much keeping. And it fails on the tired, late, overwhelmed days, which are exactly the days a home most wants to slide.

"The reason your good intentions keep failing is not that you are forgetful. It is that you are asking your memory to do a job the day should be doing. A keeping-habit tied to a fixed time in the day happens on the day you would have forgotten, which is the only day it ever really mattered."

It was never your character. It was your method. This is from Chapter 29 of 6S Success: Home Edition. Read it free online. 🙂

## Post 2 · Bolt the new habit to an old one

Here is the trick the whole thing lives on: do not try to start a new habit. Bolt a tiny one onto a habit you already have without even thinking about it.

"The easiest new habit is one you never have to start, because it is bolted to an old one that already runs without fail. You do not need to remember to reset the kitchen after dinner if the reset simply is part of after dinner, the way clearing the plates already is."

Your day is already full of things that happen without fail. The kettle boils. The school run leaves. The plates get cleared. The lights go off. Each one can be an anchor. You hang a small keeping-act on it, and the reliable old habit drags the small new one along behind it.

"The keeping is no longer a separate thing you might forget; it is a passenger on a trip the day was already taking."

## Post 3 · A rhythm, not a schedule

If "daily times" makes you picture a wall planner covered in colored blocks, breathe out. It is the opposite of that.

"A daily 6S time is a short, shared moment of keeping, anchored to something that already happens in the day, so the small upkeep of the home runs on the day's rhythm rather than on anyone's memory. A couple of them, a few minutes each, are enough to keep a home that is already good."

Three things decide whether one survives real life:

"What makes a daily time work: keep it short, a few minutes with a hard stop; anchor it to a moment that already happens, so no one has to remember; and do it together, so it is quick and shared rather than one person's chore."

Short, anchored, together. Drop any one and it quietly dies. Keep all three and it runs on a bad night. More in Chapter 29 of 6S Success: Home Edition, free to read online.

## Post 4 · The quiet hum underneath everything

Of the three ways to keep a home, the daily times are quietly the most important, and the easiest to overlook, because they barely feel like anything.

"The audit catches drift and problem-solving removes what keeps returning, but the daily times are the quiet hum underneath both: the small, constant upkeep that means the home never drifts far enough to need catching or solving in the first place. This is the rhythm that finally makes keeping automatic."

The audit and the family huddle are things you do occasionally, when something has slipped. The daily times run every single day, in the background, so gently you barely notice them, and because they run constantly they stop most drift before it ever becomes something to catch or solve.

"That is a home keeping itself, and it looks like almost nothing, which is exactly how you know it will last."

## Post 5 · The third Sustain step, and what comes next

One quiet note to close on. Go and look at the friction meter. The needle moves again, and it is nearly there.

"The needle takes its third Sustain step, and it is nearly at the goal now. A home whose keeping runs on the rhythm of the day, rather than on anyone remembering, is a home that mostly keeps itself, which is very nearly the whole of what this book has been walking toward. Only a sliver of the gap remains, and the final chapter, the return to the start of the loop, closes it."

The mindset held the needle steady, the audit caught drift faster, and solving ended the problems that kept returning. This adds the thing that makes keeping automatic. You now have all of it, running without anyone having to remember, decide, or nag.

"do not ask your memory to keep the home. Hand the job to the day."
