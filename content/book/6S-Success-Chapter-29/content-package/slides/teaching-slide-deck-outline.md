### Slide 4 · The trouble with remembering

- Be honest about why the sensible plan of "just remember to keep on top of things" fails so reliably, because the failure is not the moral one you probably assume. When the home slides despite their best intentions, most people quietly conclude the problem is them, that they are disorganized or undisciplined, which is both wrong and expensive, because it aims the fix at your character, which you cannot change much, instead of at your method, which you can change completely.
- The truth is kinder and more useful: a task that depends on being remembered has a hidden, unreliable dependency, and the more tired and busy you are, the more that dependency fails. You have not been failing to keep your home; you have been running a keeping-plan with a single point of failure, your memory, and asking it to hold up on exactly the days it is most overloaded. The answer is not to try to remember harder; it is to build a plan that does not need remembering at all.
- The memory-versus-rhythm reframe, frozen: "The reason your good intentions keep failing is not that you are forgetful. It is that you are asking your memory to do a job the day should be doing. A keeping-habit tied to a fixed time in the day happens on the day you would have forgotten, which is the only day it ever really mattered."

### Slide 5 · Anchor it to the day

- Here is how you build a keeping-plan that needs no remembering, and it rests on one small, almost sneaky trick. Your day is already full of things that happen reliably, every single day, without anyone deciding to make them happen: the kettle boils, the school run leaves, the dishes get cleared after dinner, the lights go off at night. Each is a moment that arrives on its own, carried by the rhythm of the day, and each can serve as an anchor. You take a small act of keeping and bolt it onto one of these moments, so the reliable old habit drags the small new one along behind it.
- You do not have to remember to wipe the counter, because wiping the counter is now simply the last thing that happens while the coffee brews; you do not have to remember the evening reset, because it has become part of clearing up after dinner. The keeping is no longer a separate thing you might forget; it is a passenger on a trip the day was already taking. Notice how modest that is: not a schedule, not a rota, not a wall planner covered in coloured blocks, just a couple of short moments hooked to things that were going to happen anyway, because your home is already good and a tiny drift needs only a tiny, regular correction.
- The anchor reframe, frozen (hero): "The easiest new habit is one you never have to start, because it is bolted to an old one that already runs without fail. You do not need to remember to reset the kitchen after dinner if the reset simply is part of after dinner, the way clearing the plates already is." The definition box, frozen: "A daily 6S time is a short, shared moment of keeping, anchored to something that already happens in the day, so the small upkeep of the home runs on the day's rhythm rather than on anyone's memory. A couple of them, a few minutes each, are enough to keep a home that is already good."

### Slide 6 · Short, anchored, together

- Anchoring is the core of it, but three small qualities decide whether a daily time actually survives contact with real life, and it is worth holding all three, because dropping any one of them is how these plans quietly die. Short matters because length breeds avoidance: a two-minute reset gets done on a bad night, a twenty-minute one gets postponed to a better night that never comes, so keep the times genuinely tiny, a few minutes with a clear stopping point, and resist every urge to make them thorough. Anchored matters because, bolted to an existing moment, the time needs no memory, so it survives the tired days.
- And together matters because a shared time is both faster and lighter: four people tidying for three minutes is a full reset that costs each person almost nothing and belongs to the whole household rather than one weary pair of hands, which is the same "a little, often, together" that made the fifteen-minute reset work back in Shine, now applied to the whole of keeping. Get all three right and the daily time becomes so light and so automatic that skipping it would feel odder than doing it. And it is worth seeing what these daily times are in the larger picture: they are quietly the most important of the three keeping-practices, because the audit and the family huddle are things you do occasionally, when something has slipped, while the daily times run every single day in the background, so gently you barely notice them, and because they run constantly they stop most drift before it ever becomes something to catch or solve.
- The three qualities, frozen: "What makes a daily time work: keep it short, a few minutes with a hard stop; anchor it to a moment that already happens, so no one has to remember; and do it together, so it is quick and shared rather than one person's chore." The daily-hum reframe, frozen (the engine): "The audit catches drift and problem-solving removes what keeps returning, but the daily times are the quiet hum underneath both: the small, constant upkeep that means the home never drifts far enough to need catching or solving in the first place. This is the rhythm that finally makes keeping automatic."

### Slide 7 · A day with its times

- Put the whole thing on an ordinary day, because the whole point is how little it disturbs one, and seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. Take a normal weekday. In the morning, in the small flurry of everyone getting out of the door, one keeping-time is bolted to the leaving: for the two minutes before the door shuts, everyone puts their own things back at the launch pad and clears the breakfast surface, and then they go, and the home is left calm rather than strewn. That is the whole morning time, two minutes, anchored to a departure that was always going to happen.
- Then the day runs, untouched, with nobody thinking about the house at all. And in the evening, one more time is bolted to the clearing-up after dinner: while the plates are already being cleared, the common rooms get a short shared reset, everyone to a zone for a few minutes, and then it stops, and the evening is free. Two small times, a handful of minutes total, each riding on a moment that happened anyway, and between them they hold the whole home steady day after day. Nobody had to remember either one, because the day remembered them. That is a home keeping itself, and it looks like almost nothing, which is exactly how you know it will last.
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Pick one thing that already happens in your day without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the lights going off at night, and bolt one two-minute keeping-act to it, starting today. You will never have to remember it again, because the day will remember it for you." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Keep the daily times few and short enough that they survive your worst day. Two anchored, three-minute times you actually do every day beat an ambitious routine you keep for a week. If a time keeps getting skipped, it is too long or badly anchored, not a sign that you lack discipline."

### Slide 8 · The rule

- That is the whole chapter, and it fits in a single line. Keeping fails when it depends on remembering, so you do not try to remember harder; you give the keeping a fixed time in the day, anchored to a moment that already happens, and it runs on the rhythm of the day rather than on your memory. Point the small upkeep at moments that arrive without fail, keep it short and shared, and the home holds itself steady whether or not anyone is thinking about it.
- The lightness is not softness; it is the whole point. This is a rhythm and not a timetable, a habit and not willpower. Forgetting is normal and expected, because memory is unreliable, which is the entire reason the method exists. A skipped time is never a sign that you lack discipline; it just means the time is wrong, too long or badly anchored, and wants adjusting, not more resolve.
- The rule, frozen, the hero: "Keeping fails when it depends on remembering. Give it a fixed time in the day instead, anchored to a moment that already happens, and it runs on the rhythm of the day rather than on your memory."
