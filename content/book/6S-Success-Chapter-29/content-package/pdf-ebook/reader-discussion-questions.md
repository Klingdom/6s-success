# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Daily 6S Times

*Chapter 29, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter turns on a single fix: "Keeping fails when it depends on remembering. Give it a fixed time in the day instead, anchored to a moment that already happens, and it runs on the rhythm of the day rather than on your memory." Which keeping-habit in your home has quietly failed because it relied on you remembering, and what fixed moment in the day could you hang it on instead?

2. The chapter calls memory "the least reliable tool in the whole box," working on the calm days a home barely needs keeping and failing on the tired days it most wants to slide. When does keeping fall apart in your home, on the good days or the hard ones, and what does that tell you about what you have been relying on?

3. The chapter reframes the failure as a method problem, not a character one: "The reason your good intentions keep failing is not that you are forgetful. It is that you are asking your memory to do a job the day should be doing." How does it change the feeling to hear that you have not been failing to keep your home, only running a keeping-plan with a single point of failure, your memory?

4. The hero is the anchor: "The easiest new habit is one you never have to start, because it is bolted to an old one that already runs without fail." What is one thing that already happens in your day without fail, and what small keeping-act could you bolt to it so you never have to remember it again?

5. The definition box names the practice: "A daily 6S time is a short, shared moment of keeping, anchored to something that already happens in the day, so the small upkeep of the home runs on the day's rhythm rather than on anyone's memory. A couple of them, a few minutes each, are enough to keep a home that is already good." What would a couple of daily times look like in your household, and where would you anchor each one?

6. The chapter insists this is modest by design, not a schedule, not a rota, not a wall planner covered in coloured blocks, only a tiny correction for a tiny drift, because your home is already good. Where have you made keeping harder than it needed to be, and what would it feel like to nudge the home back the small amount it drifts rather than rebuild it every day?

7. Three qualities decide whether a daily time survives: "keep it short, a few minutes with a hard stop; anchor it to a moment that already happens, so no one has to remember; and do it together." Which of the three, short, anchored, or together, does your household find hardest, and what happens to the plan when that one is missing?

8. The chapter says length breeds avoidance: a two-minute reset gets done on a bad night, a twenty-minute one gets postponed to a better night that never comes. Where have you set the bar too high and lost the habit entirely, and what would the two-minute version look like instead?

9. The Family Challenge is to choose and do the daily times together: "Four people resetting for three minutes is a whole reset, and it belongs to the family instead of resting on one pair of hands." Who in your home currently carries the keeping alone, and how might it change to have everyone reset together for a few minutes rather than one person for a long time?

10. The chapter names the daily times as the engine underneath everything: "the daily times are the quiet hum underneath both: the small, constant upkeep that means the home never drifts far enough to need catching or solving in the first place." How does it change your sense of the whole of Part Eight to see the daily rhythm, not the audit or the huddle, as the thing that finally makes keeping automatic?

11. In the worked example, one keeping-time is bolted to the morning leaving and one to clearing up after dinner, two small times a handful of minutes each, and "nobody had to remember either one, because the day remembered them." Walk through one of your own ordinary days: where are the natural anchors already sitting, waiting to carry a small act of keeping?

12. The chapter offers a vivid image for anchoring: "The keeping is no longer a separate thing you might forget; it is a passenger on a trip the day was already taking." What is one keeping-act you could turn from a separate errand into a passenger on something that already happens, and which trip would carry it?

13. The Quick Win is the smallest possible proof: "Pick one thing that already happens in your day without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the lights going off at night, and bolt one two-minute keeping-act to it, starting today." Which anchor would you pick, and could you bolt the first keeping-act to it before this discussion ends?

14. The Common Mistake names the exact way it goes wrong: "Building a long, detailed daily cleaning schedule and calling it a rhythm. A schedule you have to consult and remember is just one more thing to fail at." Where have you built a schedule that quietly became one more thing to fail at, and how would a couple of small anchored acts be different?

15. The friction meter takes its third Sustain step: "A home whose keeping runs on the rhythm of the day, rather than on anyone remembering, is a home that mostly keeps itself." With five of the six S's done and only a sliver of the gap remaining, how does it feel that this move is small in distance but large in meaning, the engine that lets the whole of keeping run without effort?

16. The One Idea to Keep closes the chapter: "Stop asking your memory to keep the home. Bolt a couple of short, shared keeping-times to moments that already happen in your day, and the upkeep runs on the rhythm of the day itself." What would it take for your household to hand the job to the day, so keeping a home stops being something you remember to do and becomes simply part of how the day goes?
