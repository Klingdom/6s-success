# Daily 6S Times

**Part Eight, Sustain**
Chapter 29 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

Subtitle · Everything you have built to keep the home still depends on someone remembering to do it, and remembering is exactly what fails on a tired day. The fix is to stop relying on memory and hang the small acts of keeping on moments that already happen in the day, so a couple of short, shared upkeep times run on the rhythm of the day itself, and keeping the home stops being a decision and becomes simply part of how the day goes.

---

## Chapter descriptor

The fourth chapter of Part Eight, and it is the piece that finally makes all the rest run on its own. Chapter 26 gave the mindset, that a home is kept and not finished; Chapter 27 gave the friendly audit that catches drift while it is small; Chapter 28 gave the shared way to solve the recurring problems, at the cause and once. Held together they are most of what keeping takes, but they share one big weakness: every one of them still depends on somebody remembering to do it, and remembering is the least reliable tool in the whole box, because it works on the calm, well-slept days a home barely needs keeping and fails, predictably, on the tired, overwhelmed days a home most wants to slide. This chapter removes the remembering, and that is the whole of it: you stop hanging keeping on your memory and start hanging it on the day. It names the trouble first, that your good intentions keep failing not because you are forgetful but because you are asking your memory to do a job the day should be doing, and a keeping-habit tied to a fixed time in the day happens on the day you would have forgotten, which is the only day it ever really mattered. Then it delivers the way out, the anchor: the easiest new habit is one you never have to start, because it is bolted to an old one that already runs without fail, so you do not need to remember to reset the kitchen after dinner if the reset simply is part of after dinner, the way clearing the plates already is. Your day is already full of things that happen without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the plates being cleared, the lights going off, and you bolt a small keeping-act onto one of them, so the reliable old habit drags the small new one along behind it, and the keeping is no longer a separate thing you might forget but a passenger on a trip the day was already taking. It fits the hero in a plain definition box, that a daily 6S time is a short, shared moment of keeping, anchored to something that already happens in the day, so the small upkeep of the home runs on the day's rhythm rather than on anyone's memory, and a couple of them, a few minutes each, are enough to keep a home that is already good. Then it gives the three qualities that decide whether a daily time survives contact with real life, short, anchored, and together: keep it short, a few minutes with a hard stop, because length breeds avoidance; anchor it to a moment that already happens, so no one has to remember; and do it together, so it is quick and shared rather than one person's chore. It names these daily times as the quiet hum underneath the whole of Part Eight, because the audit catches drift and problem-solving removes what keeps returning, but the daily times mean the home never drifts far enough to need catching or solving in the first place, and this is the rhythm that finally makes keeping automatic. It walks one ordinary weekday with a couple of keeping-times folded in, a two-minute morning tidy of the launch pad and the breakfast surface bolted to the moment of leaving, and a short shared reset of the common rooms bolted to clearing up after dinner, and nobody has to remember either one because the day remembers them, which is a home keeping itself and looks like almost nothing, exactly how you know it will last. Then it reads the friction meter as a small third Sustain step, not a milestone: the needle moves a little further along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26, from Chapter 28's position toward the goal, and comes to rest very close to it, because a home whose keeping runs on the rhythm of the day, rather than on anyone remembering, is a home that mostly keeps itself, which is very nearly the whole of what the goal has always represented. The step is small in distance and large in meaning, small because you have only bolted a couple of tiny times to the day and large because those times are the engine that lets the whole of keeping run without effort. Only a sliver of the gap remains, and there is one thing left to add, in the final chapter, that closes it: the periodic renewal that carries the household around the full six-S loop again. Five of the six S's were complete going in, Sustain is nearly complete, and the goal, its dashed path down to a sliver now, is reached only at the very end of the book, when all six S's are done.

## How to read this chapter

This is the fourth chapter of Part Eight, Sustain, and it gives the household the daily rhythm that finally makes keeping run on its own. It picks up exactly where the last three chapters left off: you have the keeping mindset from Chapter 26, that a home is kept and not finished; the friendly audit from Chapter 27, that catches drift while it is still small; and the shared way to solve the recurring problems from Chapter 28, at the cause and once. What all three still shared was a single point of failure, your memory, because every one of them depends on somebody remembering to do it, and remembering fails exactly on the tired, busy days a home most wants to slide. This chapter hands you the thing that removes the remembering: you attach the small acts of upkeep to fixed moments that already happen every day without fail, so a couple of short, shared keeping-times run on the rhythm of the day itself, whether or not anyone is thinking about the house. Read it through once for the move, that you stop asking your memory to keep the home and hand the job to the day, then do the one small, concrete thing it asks: pick one thing that already happens in your day without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the lights going off at night, and bolt one two-minute keeping-act to it, starting today.

The chapter rests on one move and three qualities. The move is to anchor keeping to the day rather than to memory: instead of trying to remember the small upkeep, you bolt it to something reliable that already happens, so the old habit drags the new one along and the keeping becomes a passenger on a trip the day was already taking. The three qualities are what make a daily time survive contact with real life, and dropping any one of them quietly kills the plan. Short, because length breeds avoidance: a two-minute reset gets done on a bad night, a twenty-minute one gets postponed to a better night that never comes. Anchored, so it needs no memory at all. And together, because a shared time is faster and lighter and belongs to the whole household rather than one weary pair of hands. The register throughout is warm, practical, light, and above all freeing: this is a rhythm, not a chore schedule; a habit, not willpower; and forgetting is entirely normal, because memory is unreliable, which is the whole reason the method exists. The emotional heart is relief, the relief of no longer having to remember, decide, or nag, because the day now carries the keeping for you.

A word on the scope fence, so the chapter stays the household's whole-home daily keeping-rhythm, and the anchor that makes it automatic, rather than drifting into the neighbouring tools or back into the earlier S's. It is distinct from the fifteen-minute reset of Chapter 19, which is Shine's cleaning rhythm; that reset is just one such daily time, and this chapter references it in a single light line as one example without re-teaching it. It is distinct from the schedule tool of Chapter 24, which sets a time for each standard; this chapter is the whole-home daily keeping-rhythm, not a per-standard timetable, and it touches the schedule only lightly. It uses the mindset from Chapter 26, the audit from Chapter 27, and the problem-solving from Chapter 28 without re-teaching any of them. It does not build the recurring, periodic event that renews the whole loop and completes the book, which is Chapter 30, Your Next 6S Event. It does not re-open Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, or Standardize; a one-line callback is fine, but the earlier S's are not re-taught here. And the idea of anchoring a new habit to an existing one is referenced lightly, as a plain observation about how habits stick, and is deliberately not dressed up as any named habit system. Because a home whose upkeep runs on the rhythm of the day, rather than on anyone remembering, is a home that mostly keeps itself, the friction meter takes its third Sustain step, a small move further along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26, from Chapter 28's position toward the goal, coming to rest very close to it. This is a small move, not a milestone, and honestly so, because you have only bolted a couple of tiny times to the day; but it is large in meaning, because those times are the engine that lets the whole of keeping run without effort. The goal is reached not in this chapter but at the very end of the book, at Chapter 30, when all six S's are done.

If you only do one thing: pick one thing that already happens in your day without fail, the kettle boiling, the school run leaving, the lights going off at night, and bolt one two-minute keeping-act to it, starting today. You will never have to remember it again, because the day will remember it for you. That is the smallest possible proof of the whole chapter, one small keeping-act hung on the rhythm of the day instead of on your memory, and it is how the tiring loop of trying to remember it all quietly ends. Do not ask your memory to keep the home. Hand the job to the day.
