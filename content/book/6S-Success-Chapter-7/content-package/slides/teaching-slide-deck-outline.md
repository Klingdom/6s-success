### Slide 2: The last question before the work

- You chose the space. You audited it. You photographed it.
- One short question is left, and skipping it is why organizing falls apart later.
- What is this space actually for?

### Slide 3: Decide before you sort

- Sorting is a long run of keep-or-go decisions. Without a standard, each one is slow and inconsistent.
- A purpose turns every decision into one fast question: does this help the space do its job?
- Decide the job first, and the sorting has something to be right about.

[VISUAL] A yardstick or ruler graphic labeled "the purpose," with items being held up against it. One item passes (green calm-dot), one does not (red friction-spark).

---

### Slide 4: A space is messy because it has no one job

- Most troubled spaces were never assigned a job. They accumulated five by default, and the jobs compete.
- The space is not failing because it is messy. It is messy because it is failing to be any one thing.
- Tidy can still fail. A space optimized for nothing in particular ends up neat and wrong.

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF. The line "It is messy because it is failing to be any one thing" set in Fraunces, slate rules above and below.

### Slide 5: The everything-space

- The surface or drawer quietly assigned five jobs at once: mail, keys, charging, homework, decor.
- The mail buries the keys. Nothing has room to be done well.
- The fix is not a better system for five jobs. It is the one-job rule.

[VISUAL] A single console crowded with five competing job tags (MAIL, KEYS, CHARGING, HOMEWORK, DECOR) in white boxes with ink outlines, colliding. Two red friction-sparks where the jobs overlap.

### Slide 6: One Job Beats Five

- Five jobs at once: good at nothing.
- One clear job, a launch pad: good at one thing.
- Excellence at one thing requires not being mediocre at four others.

### Slide 7: The one-job rule

- A space cannot be good at everything. Give it one clear job, and at most one supporting job.
- Pick the job that matters most. Let the others find a better home elsewhere.
- For a drop zone, the real job is usually a launch pad.

[VISUAL] Definition box. Slate 2px border on panel background, uppercase slate label "THE ONE-JOB RULE," the rule itself set in Fraunces medium.

---

### Slide 8: The Purpose Statement

- A purpose in your head is a vague intention. On paper it is a tool.
- Fill two blanks: This [space] is for [one primary job], so that [an outcome you actually want].
- You are not writing poetry. You are writing a decision rule.

[VISUAL] The Purpose Statement template card. Slate-bordered card with the fill-in-the-blank sentence in Fraunces, the three blanks shown in terracotta brackets. Small note beneath: "Name one primary job, and one supporting job at most."

---

### Slide 9: The worked example

- This entryway is for getting everyone out the door fast and landing softly on the way back in, so that mornings start calm instead of frantic.
- Now hold any object against it. The keys serve it. The basket for each kid's bag serves it.
- The three weeks of mail does not. The sentence sorted the space before you touched a thing.

[VISUAL] The filled example card beneath the template. A green-tabbed "FILLED EXAMPLE" card in Newsreader italic. Beside it, three small objects: keys (pass, green calm-dot), a basket (pass, green), a stack of mail (fail, red friction-spark).

---

### Slide 10: Match it to your real life

- The trap is writing a purpose for the home you wish you had.
- A space defined for an imaginary, tidier family is wrong for the real one every single morning.
- Test it at the worst moment: the frantic Tuesday, the rainy game day. A good purpose works with your life. A vain one fights it.

[VISUAL] An aspirational-vs-real contrast. Left, a catalog-perfect entryway labeled "the home you wish you had" with a faint red friction-spark. Right, a lived-in entryway with backpacks and boots labeled "the home you actually have," steady green calm-dot. Honest, not unflattering.

---

### Slide 11: Purpose sets the limits

- Once a space has one job, it also has a natural capacity. You know exactly how much belongs.
- That is your defense against the slow re-cluttering that undoes most organizing.
- A defined purpose gives every stray object a bouncer at the door.

[VISUAL] A doorway with a bouncer-style gatekeeper line. Objects that serve the job pass through (green calm-dots), a stray object is turned away at the threshold (red friction-spark). Calm, almost diagram-like.

---

### Slide 12: Write yours

- Five minutes, mostly thinking. Find the one job that matters most and write the sentence.
- Keep it to one primary job. Aim it at a real feeling, not at tidiness.
- Pressure-test it on your worst day, then keep it with your audit number and your before photo.

[VISUAL] A short dashed checklist card on panel: name the primary job, one supporting job at most, write the sentence, match it to real life, keep it with the number and photo. Slate square checkboxes.
