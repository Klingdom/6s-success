# Chapter 7 Outline: Define the Purpose of the Area

## Opener: The first thing you decide

## 1. Chapter Opening: The Last Question Before the Work
You chose, audited, and photographed the space. One short question remains, and skipping it is why organizing falls apart later: what is this space for? Most troubled spaces were never assigned a job; they accumulated five by default and compete. The space is not failing because it is messy; it is messy because it is failing to be any one thing. We give the space a job: one sentence, five minutes.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- Why a space needs a defined purpose before you sort it
- How to spot the everything-space
- How to write a one-sentence purpose for any space
- How to match that purpose to the life you actually live
- How the purpose sets a space's limits and keeps it good

## 3. Why Purpose Comes Before Sorting
Sorting is many keep-or-go decisions; without a standard they are slow, tiring, and inconsistent. A purpose turns each into one fast question: does this help the space do its job? It is the yardstick. It also explains why a tidy space can still fail (optimized for nothing). Decide the job first; then sorting has something to be right about.
- Callout (6S Tip): Organizing without a purpose is just moving things around neatly.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Sorting first and asking the purpose later. Every item becomes a judgment call with no yardstick.

## 4. The Enemy Is the Everything Space
The everything-space: a surface asked to do five jobs at once (mail, keys, charging, homework, decor) and good at none. The fix is not a better system for five jobs; it is the one-job rule. Pick the job that matters most; let the others find homes. For a drop zone, the real job is usually a launch pad.
- Hero infographic: One Job Beats Five (five competing jobs, "good at nothing" vs one job, "good at one thing")
- Definition box: The one-job rule. A space cannot be good at everything.

## 5. Write the One Sentence
A purpose in your head is vague; on paper it is a tool. Write: "this space is for one job, so that an outcome you care about." A plain true sentence beats a vague pretty one. Worked example: the entryway is for getting everyone out the door and landing softly, so mornings start calm. Hold any object against it.
- Hero infographic: The Purpose Statement (fill-in-the-blank template + filled drop-zone example)
- Callout (6S Tip): If your sentence needs "and" three times, it is doing too much. One primary job.

## 6. Match It to Your Real Life
The trap: writing a purpose for the home you wish you had. A fantasy entryway fails every real morning. Define it for the people who actually live there, doing what they actually do. Test it at the worst moment (frantic Tuesday, rainy game day). This is also the kindest version: permission to stop apologizing for your real life.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Writing a purpose for the home you wish you had.

## 7. Purpose Sets the Limits
A clear purpose sets capacity: how much belongs is exactly what the job requires, no more. This defends against re-cluttering, the real enemy, which erodes spaces one stray object at a time (like the junk drawer in Chapter 1). The purpose is a bouncer at the door, turning away anything that does not serve the job. The number located it, the photo recorded it, the purpose keeps it good.
- Callout (Quick Win): Say the job out loud, then name one thing in the space that has nothing to do with it. Do not move it yet, just notice it.

## 8. Write Yours
Five minutes, mostly thinking. Find the one job that matters most; write the sentence; keep it to one primary job; aim at a real feeling, not tidiness. Pressure-test it (worst day, real home, truly one job). Keep it with the audit number and the before photo. You will not move anything yet.
- Checklist (Write your purpose): name the primary job, one supporting job at most, write the sentence, match it to real life, keep it with the number and photo.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Each person writes the purpose of their space, reads it aloud, and the family votes on whether it is one honest job or three jobs in a trench coat.

## 9. Chapter Close: Fully Prepared
The one sentence turns sorting from a thousand judgment calls into one easy question, tells you what to defend, and installs a rule that keeps the space good. Part Two is complete: a number, a picture, and a purpose. Next, Part Three: we stop preparing and start doing. The needle is finally about to move.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): A space cannot be good at everything. Decide its one job, in a single sentence, and you have just made every sorting decision that follows almost automatic.
