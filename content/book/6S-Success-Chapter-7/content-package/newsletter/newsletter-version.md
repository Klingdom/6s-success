# Newsletter Edition: Define the Purpose of the Area

**Subject line (placeholder):** Your space is not messy. It is confused.

**Preview text (placeholder):** Decide its one job in a sentence, and the rest gets easy.

---

Hi friend,

Quick question before you ever pick up a single thing: what is that messy spot in your home actually *for*?

I know, it sounds too simple to count as a step. But try to say it out loud, in one sentence, and something uncomfortable shows up. Most of the troubled spaces in a home were never given a job. They just collected one, or three, or five, by accident, as life threw things at them. The drop zone by your door was never decided to be anything. It became where the mail lands, and the keys land, and the chargers live, and the homework gets dumped, all at once, all competing.

Here is the line I keep coming back to: the space is not failing because it is messy. It is messy because it is failing to be any one thing.

That is the trap, and it has a name. The everything-space. One small surface quietly assigned five jobs at once, and therefore good at none of them. The mail buries the keys. The fix is not a cleverer system for all five jobs. The fix is to admit a space cannot do five jobs, pick the one that matters most, and let the other four go find homes of their own. A space is allowed to have one job. Honestly, that is the only way a space ever gets genuinely good, because being great at one thing means not being mediocre at four others.

Here is why we decide the purpose before we sort. Sorting is just a long string of keep-or-go decisions, and without a fixed standard every one of them is slow, tiring, and inconsistent. You end up with a space that is technically tidy but somehow still wrong. A clear purpose turns all of that into one fast question: does this thing help the space do its job? The keys help the entryway get you out the door, so they stay. The three weeks of mail does nothing for getting anyone out, so it has a better home elsewhere. You set the rule once, then check each item against it, calmly.

The tool is a single sentence. Write down what your space is for, in this shape: this space is for one main job, so that some outcome you actually care about. Here is the worked example for a drop zone. This entryway is for getting everyone out the door fast and landing softly on the way back in, so that mornings start calm instead of frantic. Notice how much quiet work it does. It names the job, and it admits the reason, calmer mornings, which is the thing you genuinely want. You have not moved a thing, and the sentence has already started sorting the space for you.

One more thing, and it is the kind one. Write the purpose for the home you actually live in, not the one you wish you had. It is easy, sitting with a pen, to define your entryway as a serene, minimal gallery with one elegant tray. Lovely. Also a complete fantasy for a household of four people, a dog, and a sports schedule, and a purpose built on that fantasy will be wrong every single morning. A good purpose works with your real life. A vain one fights it. Test yours on the frantic Tuesday and the rainy game day.

**To try this week:** Go to your space, or call it clearly to mind, and write its one-sentence purpose. Name the single job that matters most, add the outcome you truly want, and keep it to one primary job. Five minutes, most of it thinking. Then keep that sentence with your audit number and your before photo. Together those three things mean you are fully prepared to start.

That is the whole assignment. You will not move anything yet. You are just deciding, in advance, what this space is going to be.

If you want the full version, with the everything-space diagram and the Purpose Statement template, the chapter is free to read in the online book. Go give one space a job.

Warmly,
The 6S Success team
