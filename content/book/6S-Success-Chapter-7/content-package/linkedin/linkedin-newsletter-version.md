# Give Your Messiest Space One Job

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

I want to start with a small test, because it tends to land harder than any argument I could make.

Walk to the most troubled spot in your home. The drop zone by the door, the kitchen counter that collects everything, the chair that quietly wears your clothes. Now say out loud, in one sentence, what that space is for.

Most people stall. And that stall is the whole point.

We tend to think a space is in trouble because it is messy. I have come to see it the other way around. The space is not failing because it is messy. It is messy because it is failing to be any one thing.

That console by your door was never decided to be anything. It became the place where mail lands, and keys land, and chargers live, and homework gets dumped, all at once, none of it chosen. It did not collect a job. It collected five, by default, as life threw things at it. And a space asked to do five jobs at once cannot be good at any of them. The mail buries the keys. Nothing has room to be done well.

## Decide the job before you sort

Here is the part I find most organizing advice skips. Before you decide what stays and what goes, decide what the space is for. One sentence. Five minutes. It is the least physical step you can take, and one of the most useful, because it changes what sorting even is.

Sorting is a long chain of decisions. Keep this, relocate that, release the other thing, for every object in the space. With no fixed standard, each decision is slow and tiring, because you are re-deciding your whole philosophy of life every time you pick up a stray battery. And the decisions come out inconsistent, because tired-you at item ninety makes a different call than fresh-you made at item three. That is how you get a space that is technically sorted and still somehow wrong.

A defined purpose fixes both at once. It turns every keep-or-go choice into one fast question: does this thing help the space do its job? The umbrella helps the entryway get you out the door in the rain, so it stays. The read magazines do nothing for that, so they have a better home elsewhere. You stop debating each object on its own merits and start checking it against a standard you set in advance.

## Write the one sentence

A purpose in your head is a vague intention. A purpose on paper is a tool. So write it in this shape:

This space is for one primary job, so that an outcome you actually want.

The first blank is the job. The second is the reason it matters. You are not writing poetry. You are writing a decision rule, and a plain, slightly clumsy sentence that is true beats a beautiful one that is vague.

Here is the worked example. This entryway is for getting everyone out the door fast and landing softly on the way back in, so that mornings start calm instead of frantic. Hold any object against that. The keys serve it. The basket for each kid's bag serves it. The three weeks of mail does not. The sentence sorted the space for you, and you have not touched a thing.

One caution. Write the purpose for the home you actually live in, not the one you wish you had. It is easy to define your entryway as a serene minimal welcome space with one elegant tray. Lovely, and for four people and a dog and a sports schedule, a fantasy that will be wrong every single morning. Test your sentence at the worst moment, the frantic Tuesday, the rainy game day. If it only holds when everyone behaves like a catalog model, it is the wrong purpose.

## Why it lasts

There is one last gift hidden in a clear purpose. It sets the space's limits. Once a space has one job, you know exactly how much belongs there, as much as the job requires and not one thing more. Then the purpose works like a bouncer at the door. New clutter drifts in, the purpose asks, does this serve the job, and if not, it is gently turned away before it can settle.

A space cannot be good at everything. Decide its one job, in a single sentence, and you have just made every sorting decision that follows almost automatic.

That is the whole assignment this week. You will not move anything yet. Just go to your space, find the one job that matters most, and write the sentence. Keep it next to your before photo.
