## Signature Overview

**Length:** 16 pages (8 spreads)
**Part:** Part 2, Prepare (the closing chapter of Part 2; it follows Chapter 6's before photo and hands off to Part 3, Sort, beginning with Chapter 8)
**Reading time:** 9 to 12 minutes
**Text-to-visual ratio target:** 58% text, 42% visual
**Job of this chapter:** Have the reader decide, in one written sentence, what their chosen space is actually for, before any sorting begins. Pay off Chapter 6's closing promise ("you have measured it and pictured it; before you change a thing, one short, clarifying question: what is this space actually for?"). Establish purpose as the decision rule that every later Sort and Straighten choice is measured against. Close Part 2 cleanly: the reader now has a number, a picture, and a purpose, which is everything they need before the work starts. Do not sort yet. Removing things is Part 3, Chapter 8.

**Throughline space:** the entryway drop zone, carried through Chapters 4, 5, and 6, so the reader defines the purpose of the same space they chose, audited, and photographed. The worked purpose: the drop zone is a launch pad for leaving and arriving, not a permanent home for mail, decor, and homework.

**Design continuity:** Same system as Chapters 1 to 6. Warm paper, Fraunces display, Newsreader body, terracotta and honey accents, slate for data. Green calm-dot and red friction-spark vocabulary returns (a single-job space glows with calm-dots; an everything-space throws sparks). The friction meter appears at the close.

**New hero motif introduced:** **the Purpose Statement.** A clean, fill-in-the-blank sentence that names what a space is for, in plain language, with a worked example for the drop zone. A second device, **One Job Beats Five**, contrasts a space asked to do five competing jobs (chaotic, sparks, good at nothing) with a space given one clear primary job (calm, dots, good at that one thing). Together they make the chapter's argument visible: a space cannot be good at everything, and the one-sentence purpose is the criterion that makes every later decision easy.

**Friction meter call:** holds steady, fully aimed. No objects move in this chapter, so the needle holds (book rule). The meter has been gathering its bearings across Part 2: Chapter 4 added the GOAL crosshair on the calm side (we aimed), Chapter 5 added the NOW baseline marker on the friction side (we measured), Chapter 6 framed that NOW marker as a photo (we documented). Chapter 7 attaches a small label or flag to the GOAL crosshair reading "purpose," so the destination is now not just a direction but a defined meaning of success for this space. The needle still has not moved. Everything is now aimed, and Part 3 is where it finally travels.

## Page-by-Page Layout

### PAGE 1 (recto) | Chapter Opener
- **Spread objective:** Frame the last Prepare step as a single clarifying question, asked before any object moves.
- **Layout:** Full-bleed image. Title lower third. Kicker "Part 2 · Prepare."
- **Visual:** [VISUAL: Full-page photo] A hand setting a small handwritten card on the entryway console, near the cluttered drop zone, the card reading a few words like "the launch pad." Warm light. The space is still a mess; the purpose is the first thing being decided.
- **Caption:** "You have measured it and you have pictured it. One question remains before you touch a thing: what is this space actually for?"

### PAGE 2-3 (Spread A) | The Last Question Before the Work
- **Spread objective:** Name the step and the stakes. A space organized without a purpose is just neatly rearranged, and it will drift, because nothing decided what belonged there in the first place.
- **Layout:** Left page narrative. Right page a small spot illustration of the drop zone with a question mark, signaling "purpose, not yet sorting."
- **Body copy:** "Chapter Opening" plus the top of "Why Purpose Comes Before Sorting," roughly 480 words.
- **Callout:**
  > **6S Tip**
  > Organizing without a purpose is just moving things around neatly. The purpose is the decision rule that tells you what belongs, so every later choice gets easy.

### PAGE 4-5 (Spread B) | Why Purpose Comes Before Sorting
- **Spread objective:** Make the logical case. Purpose is the criterion you sort against. Without it, every single item is a fresh debate. With it, most items answer themselves: does this serve the purpose, yes or no.
- **Layout:** Text with a small spot of one item being held up against a written purpose, a clear yes/no.
- **Body copy:** "Why Purpose Comes Before Sorting," continued, roughly 420 words.
- **Callout:**
  > **Common Mistake**
  > Sorting first and asking what the space is for later. Then every item is a judgment call with no yardstick, the decisions exhaust you, and you quit halfway with a worse mess than you started.

### PAGE 6-7 (Spread C) | The Enemy Is the Everything Space
- **Spread objective:** Diagnose the most common failure. A space asked to do five jobs does none of them well. One space, one primary job, plus at most one supporting job.
- **Layout:** Short intro text, then the "One Job Beats Five" infographic dominating the spread.
- **Body copy:** "The Enemy Is the Everything Space," roughly 440 words.
- **Visual:** [INFOGRAPHIC] "One Job Beats Five." Left, the drop zone labeled with five competing jobs (mail sorting, key storage, charging station, homework drop, decor display), red friction-sparks, the verdict "good at nothing." Right, the same drop zone with one clear job (a launch pad for leaving and arriving), green calm-dots, the verdict "good at one thing."
- **Boxed definition:**
  > **The one-job rule**
  > A space cannot be good at everything. Give it one clear job, and at most one supporting job, and it will finally be good at that. Everything that does not serve the job has a better home elsewhere.

### PAGE 8-9 (Spread D) | Write the One Sentence
- **Spread objective:** Hand the reader the tool. A single fill-in-the-blank sentence that names the purpose, with a worked example, so they leave the spread with their own purpose written down.
- **Layout:** Text with the hero "Purpose Statement" infographic.
- **Body copy:** "Write the One Sentence," roughly 460 words.
- **Visual:** [INFOGRAPHIC] "The Purpose Statement." A clean template card with blanks: "This [space] is for [primary job], so that [outcome]." Below it, the worked drop-zone example filled in: "This entryway is for getting everyone out the door fast and landing softly on the way back in, so mornings start calm instead of frantic." A small note: name one primary job, and one supporting job at most.
- **Callout:**
  > **6S Tip**
  > If your purpose sentence needs the word "and" three times, the space is still trying to do too much. Cut it down to one primary job. The rest can live somewhere that suits them.

### PAGE 10-11 (Spread E) | Match It to Your Real Life
- **Spread objective:** Anchor the purpose in the life the reader actually lives, not an aspirational one. The entryway is for getting four real people out a real door, not for a magazine vignette. The purpose also sets the space's capacity: what belongs, and how much.
- **Layout:** Text with a small spot contrasting an aspirational empty vignette and a real, working, lived-in version of the same space.
- **Body copy:** "Match It to Your Real Life," roughly 420 words.
- **Callout:**
  > **Common Mistake**
  > Writing a purpose for the home you wish you had. A space defined for an imaginary, tidier family will be wrong for the real one every single morning. Define it for the people who actually live there.

### PAGE 12-13 (Spread F) | Purpose Sets the Limits
- **Spread objective:** Show the quiet superpower of a clear purpose: it defines capacity. Once a space has one job, you know exactly how much belongs there, which prevents the slow re-cluttering that undoes most organizing.
- **Layout:** Text with a small spot of a defined space holding only what its job needs, with clear empty room left over.
- **Body copy:** "Purpose Sets the Limits," roughly 380 words.
- **Callout:**
  > **Quick Win**
  > Say your space's job out loud, then name one thing currently in it that has nothing to do with that job. You just found the first thing that will leave when sorting begins. Do not move it yet. Just notice it.

### PAGE 14-15 (Spread G) | Write Yours
- **Spread objective:** Turn it into the single action. The reader writes the purpose sentence for their own space, keeps it with the audit number and the photo, and is now fully prepared.
- **Layout:** A short, encouraging section and a compact "Write your purpose" checklist.
- **Body copy:** "Write Yours," roughly 340 words.
- **Checklist:**
  > **Write your purpose**
  > - Name the one primary job this space exists to do.
  > - Add at most one supporting job, if it truly needs one.
  > - Write it as one sentence: this space is for ___, so that ___.
  > - Match it to the people who actually live here, not an ideal version.
  > - Keep it with your audit number and your before photo.
- **Callout:**
  > **Family Challenge**
  > Each person writes the purpose of the space they chose, reads it aloud, and the family votes on whether it is one honest job or three jobs in a trench coat. Clearest single purpose wins.

### PAGE 16 (verso) | Chapter Close and What's Next
- **Spread objective:** Close Part 2 and launch Part 3. The reader is fully prepared. The next chapter finally moves objects.
- **Layout:** Short close, "One Idea to Keep" box, friction-meter spot (holds, GOAL now labeled with purpose), "Next" strip.
- **Body copy:** "Chapter Close," roughly 240 words.
- **Boxed recap:**
  > **One Idea to Keep**
  > A space cannot be good at everything. Decide its one job, in a single sentence, and you have just made every sorting decision that follows almost automatic.
- **Visual:** [ILLUSTRATION: Friction-meter spot] The meter holding where it sat, with the GOAL crosshair on the calm side now carrying a small flag or tag reading "purpose," the NOW marker still framed as a photo on the friction side. The full picture: where you are, recorded, and exactly what better means. Everything is aimed.
- **Next teaser:** "Next: Part 3 begins. Chapter 8 · Sort: Remove What Does Not Belong. You have a number, a picture, and a purpose. Now, at last, we start moving things, and the needle starts to move with them."

## Production Notes / Review Flags
- This chapter decides; it does not sort. Keep the reader from removing anything here. The first real removal is Part 3, Chapter 8. Part 2 is Prepare: audit (Ch 5), photograph (Ch 6), purpose (Ch 7), and this chapter closes it.
- The Purpose Statement template is a new device. Confirm the template wording ("This [space] is for [primary job], so that [outcome]") and the one-primary-job rule. Both relabel easily if review prefers different phrasing.
- The friction meter completes its Part 2 arc here, with the GOAL crosshair gaining a purpose label. Confirm this read. If review prefers the meter unchanged from Chapter 6, the label drops easily. Note that Part 3, Chapter 8, should be where the needle finally moves, since that is the first chapter that removes real objects.
- Keep the tone non-prescriptive about what a space's purpose should be. The reader defines it for their life. The book supplies the method, not the answer.
- Reuse Chapters 1 to 6's exact type, palette, and callout styling. The Purpose Statement card should be drawn so it works as a standalone printable and social graphic.
