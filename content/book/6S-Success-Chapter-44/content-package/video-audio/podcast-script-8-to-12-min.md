# Chapter 44 Podcast Script: The Home Office

*Eight to twelve minutes. Conversational, unhurried.*

---

I want to start with something that sounds like a contradiction.

The home office is the easiest room in your house to clean, and it is almost certainly the one you have left the longest.

Think about it honestly. Nothing in there is heavy. Nothing in there is dirty. There is no grease like the kitchen, no lint like the laundry room, no damp like a bathroom, no grit walked in off the street like an entryway. By every measure that made the other rooms in this book genuinely hard work, the office should take you an afternoon.

And yet. Most people I talk to have started it at least once, got somewhere around the second drawer, and quietly stopped.

Here is why, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

This room is not made of objects. It is made of decisions nobody has taken.

Every single thing in there asks you a question before it will move. This cable: what does it power, and is that thing still in the house? This book: are you the person who reads this now? This sheet of paper: are you actually required to keep it, or does throwing it away just feel irresponsible? That project in the corner you have not touched in six weeks: is it live, or is it over?

None of those questions are hard to lift. All of them are hard to answer. And an unanswered question is the heaviest object a house can hold.

That is also why the ordinary advice fails here. Every article about tidying tells you to move things: put it away, find it a home, give everything a place. But nothing in this room is waiting to be moved. It is waiting to be judged. You cannot give a cable a home until you have decided whether it stays.

So what actually unlocks it?

Stop deciding one item at a time.

The cable drawer is the perfect illustration. Every home office has one, and it survives for years for a very specific reason: each individual cable is too cheap to think about and too specific to throw away. So you pick one up, you think "I am not sure what that goes to", and you put it back. And that happens every time, forever.

The fix is to refuse to do it that way. Empty the entire drawer onto a cleared desk in one heap. Then pick up each cable, name the device it powers out loud, and point at where that device physically is in this house. No device, no keep. Then keep exactly one spare of each connector type you still use daily, coil it, band it, and everything else goes to electronics recycling this week.

Made in one batch, that is a single sitting. Made one cable at a time, it never gets made at all.

And the same logic runs through the whole room. Take every book off the shelf in one go, not shelf by shelf. Line up the entire supply cabinet on the floor so you can see the true count, because a dozen packs of sticky notes look like nothing until they are standing side by side.

Now, two practical things.

The first is that this is the one room in this entire book where the damp cloth is the hazard rather than the remedy. Everywhere else, water is your ally. Here, everything is either electronics or paper, and both are ruined by it.

So the method inverts. Power the devices down and let them cool. Spray the cleaner onto the cloth and never onto a screen, a scanner glass, or a vent, because liquid sprayed at a machine runs down inside it and finds the electronics. Wring the cloth almost dry before it goes near a keyboard. Use a dry cloth on toner dust, never a damp one, because water turns loose toner into ink. And let a drawer bottom dry completely before the files go back, because paper wicks up whatever damp you leave behind.

The second is about sequence. The desk comes first, and not for the reason you would guess. It is not first because it is the most visible. It is first because it is the only zone in this book that is also a tool. Every other zone in this room gets emptied onto it: the drawer you tip out, the shelf you strip, the cabinet you count. Clear it completely before you open a single drawer, and then defend it, because the moment it refills you have lost the only workbench you had.

And then there is the thing nobody says out loud about this room.

Look at what is in your office that is not office work. The broken lamp. The unopened box from the last move. The bag of things with nowhere else to go. None of that arrived by accident, and none of it is there because the office needed it. It is there because this is the room with a door that closes.

A door that closes is the perfect place to put something you have not decided about, because shutting it makes the decision disappear from view without ever being made.

That is the room's real failure mode, and it is not a storage problem. Everything in there that is not office work walks out today, because anything that stays past today stops being temporary and becomes permanent furniture.

One last thing, about how this chapter ends.

Every room in Part Nine closes with a before-and-after photograph: the before kept as an honest record, the after held as the standard. This room does it differently. You print the after and tape it inside the top drawer, and you match it every evening when you close the laptop lid, not at the change of season.

Reset it before you stand up, rather than next morning. Because by the morning the pile has been sitting there since last night, and it has started to read as normal.

Do one zone this week, and make it the desk.
