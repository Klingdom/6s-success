## The room in one line
The home office is the room where nothing is heavy and nothing is dirty, which is exactly why it has sat untouched for years: every single item in it asks for a judgment rather than a lift, the wet cloth that rescued every other room is the one thing that can destroy this one, and because it has a door that closes it has quietly become the place the whole household puts the things it has not decided about.

## The home-office Shine layer (the nine rules from Ch 31 PLUS)
- **Every item asks for a judgment, not a lift**: the work in this room is decisions, not effort, which is why it stalls. The counter-move is to batch the decisions rather than take them one at a time.
- **Here the wet cloth is the risk**: everything in this room is either electronics or paper, so the tool that rescued every other room is the one that ruins this one. Power down, spray the cloth and never the surface, and use a dry cloth on toner because water turns loose toner into ink.
- **Clear one surface first, then defend it**: the desk is the only zone in the book that is also a tool. Every other zone gets emptied onto it, so it is the first thing finished and the last thing you let refill.
- **The door that closes**: this room became the household's holding pen because it can be shut. Anything that is not office work walks out today, or it becomes permanent furniture.

## Six zones (start at the Primary Desk, and finish it before you open a single drawer)
1. **Primary Desk** - one clear surface for the work you are actually doing now; the screen and the underside lip are the grubbiest things in the room.
2. **Desk Drawers and Pedestal** - the small tools you reach for without looking; the mess hides under the divider and in the runner track.
3. **File Storage** - the documents you are genuinely required to hold; cleaning and inspecting are the same job here.
4. **Bookshelf and Reference Zone** - the reference you actually reach for, at the height you reach from; dust falls, so start at the very top.
5. **Printer and Scanning Station** - paper into files and files into paper; cleaner onto the cloth, never onto the glass or into a vent.
6. **Supply Cabinet** - the consumables this office truly burns through in a year, and no more; the real work is looking while you wipe.

## FROZEN STRINGS (byte-verbatim in manuscript AND final.html)

### Six zones
The home office is six small zones, not one big job: the primary desk, the desk drawers and pedestal, the file storage, the bookshelf and reference zone, the printer and scanning station, and the supply cabinet. Do them one at a time, and start at the primary desk, because every other zone in this room gets emptied onto that surface at some point, so it has to be the first thing finished and the last thing you let refill.

### The rule (a room of judgments, not lifts)
The home office is the room where almost nothing is heavy and almost nothing is dirty, and that is precisely why it has sat untouched for years. Every other room asks you to lift, wipe, and carry. This one asks you to decide, of every object in it: this cable, this book, this sheet of paper, this half-finished thing in the corner. A room of decisions does not stall because the work is hard but because it is unresolved, and an unresolved decision is heavier than any box. So you clean it differently. You batch the decisions rather than take them one at a time. You treat the wet cloth as the risk, not the remedy, because everything here is electronics or paper. You clear one surface first and defend it, because it is the workbench every other zone empties onto. And you stop the room being where the household hides what it has not decided, because a door that closes is what turned it into that.

### The definition (defbox)
A finished home office is one you can sit down and start working in without moving anything first: the monitor, keyboard, and mouse in fixed positions with one notebook and one inbox tray beside them, no sticky notes fringing the screen, the power strip hooked up off the floor so the chair rolls over bare carpet, every pen in the drawer writing and the scissors point-down in their own slot, folders named for the question you would ask when hunting, the permanent originals in a closed fireproof box, working reference at seated eye level with a hand's width of empty shelf at the end of every row, and one open pack plus one spare of anything you use monthly.

### Judgment-not-lift reframe (why this room stalls)
The reason this room has defeated you is not that it is dirty or difficult. It is made almost entirely of postponed decisions, and postponed decisions are the heaviest objects in a house. A cable is not hard to lift, it is hard to rule on. A book from a job you left weighs nothing and costs an enormous amount to release. This is why the advice to just tidy up fails here: tidying moves things, and nothing in this room is waiting to be moved, it is waiting to be judged. So the move that unlocks it is to take the decisions in batches. Empty the entire cable drawer onto the desk at once and rule on all of it in one sitting. Take every book off the shelf in one go. Line up the whole supply cabinet so you can see the true count. Made in one batch at a cleared surface, these are an afternoon. Made one item at a time, they never get made at all.

### The-wet-cloth-is-the-risk reframe (the room where the method inverts)
In every other room in this book, water is your ally and the damp cloth does most of the work. In this one it is the hazard, because everything here is electronics or paper and both are ruined by it. So the method inverts. You power the devices down and let them cool. You spray the cleaner onto the cloth and never onto a screen, a scanner glass, or a vent, because liquid sprayed at a machine runs down inside it and finds the electronics. You wring the cloth almost dry before it goes near a keyboard. You use a dry cloth on toner dust, never a damp one, because water turns loose toner into ink. And you let a drawer bottom dry completely before the files return, because paper wicks up any damp you leave. Dry before wet was always one of the nine rules. In this room it is the rule that saves the equipment.

### Clear-one-surface-first reframe (the desk is a tool, not just a zone)
The desk is the only zone in this book that is also a piece of equipment. Every other zone gets emptied onto it: the drawer you tip out, the shelf you strip, the supply cabinet you count. So it is not the first zone because it is the most visible, it is first because nothing else in the room can be done properly without it. Clear it completely before you open a single drawer, then defend it, because the moment it refills you have lost the only workbench you had. That is why its standard is the strictest in the room: the desk holds the tools of the current task plus one inbox tray, and nothing else stays there overnight.

### The-door-that-closes reframe (the household's holding pen)
Look honestly at what is in this room that is not office work. The broken lamp. The unopened box from the last move. The bag of things with nowhere else to go. None of it is here because the office needed it. It is here because this is the room with a door that closes, and a door that closes is the perfect place to put something you have not decided about, since shutting it makes the decision disappear from view without ever being made. That is the room's real failure mode, and it is not a storage problem. Everything in here that is not office work walks out today, because anything that stays past today stops being temporary and becomes permanent furniture.

### Safety reframe (paper is the fuel, the chair is not a ladder)
The hazards in this room are quiet, and most of them come from treating light objects as though light meant safe. Paper is the fuel load here, so a loaded filing cabinet does not stand against a heater and the overflow boxes never sit on a power strip. Anything tall and loaded tips forward onto the person opening it, so the cabinet and the shelf unit get anchored to the wall, the heavy binders and reams live on the bottom shelves rather than above the chair you sit in, and you open one drawer at a time. The rolling chair is not a step, because it moves the instant you shift your weight, so a real step stool lives in this room. Then the wiring: the power strip comes up off the floor and out of the arc the chair rolls through, drinks sit on the far side of the mouse, nothing rests on the printer lid, and a laser printer gets clear air at its back and its own wall outlet rather than a strip chained off another, because the fuser runs genuinely hot. And the drawer you reach into blind holds its scissors point-down in a fixed slot, with no loose button batteries rolling in it.

### Before-and-after caption
This is the home office the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, the desk clear enough to start work on without moving anything, the power strip up off the floor, the drawers holding pens that all write, the folders named for the question you would ask, a hand's width of empty shelf at the end of every row, and nothing in the room that is not office work. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then do what this room asks that no other room in the book does: print the after and tape it inside the top drawer, because in here the standard is not checked at the change of season, it is checked every evening when you close the laptop lid.

### One Idea to Keep
A home office is not clear because you tidied it. It is clear because you stopped storing decisions in it: you ruled on the cables in one sitting, you let the wet cloth nowhere near the electronics, you cleared one surface and defended it, and you made everything that was not office work leave through the door it came in. Do one zone this week, and make it the desk, because every other zone gets emptied onto it. Then photograph it clear and tape the picture inside the top drawer.

## Four callouts (frozen)
- **Quick Win (win)**: Clear the desk surface completely, down to bare wood, before you open a single drawer. Take everything off onto the floor and put back only what the task in front of you needs, plus one inbox tray. It takes twenty minutes, it gives you the workbench every other zone is going to need, and it changes how the room reads from the doorway more than anything else you will do in here.
- **6S Tip (tip)**: Spray onto the cloth, never onto the thing. In a room of screens, scanner glass, keyboards, and paper, liquid sprayed at a surface runs down inside and finds the electronics. Power the device off, let it cool, mist a dry microfiber cloth, wipe in one direction, buff with a second dry cloth. On toner dust use a dry cloth only, because water turns loose toner into ink.
- **The Door That Closes Challenge (family)**: Walk into this room and name out loud every object that is not office work. The broken lamp, the unopened box from the last move, the bag of things with nowhere else to go. Every one is here because this room has a door that can be shut. All of it walks out today, because anything still here tomorrow has stopped being temporary and become permanent furniture.
- **Common Mistake (mistake)**: Doing this room one item at a time, so the cable drawer is still a cable drawer after three sessions; taking a damp cloth to a screen or keyboard because that is what worked everywhere else; and starting the filing cabinet at the end of a session, when it is hundreds of keep-or-shred calls in a row and needs a fresh morning.

## The calls that matter here
The half-finished thing (say the next physical action out loud in one sentence; if you can name it, write it on a card, clip it to the papers and put it in the inbox tray; if you cannot name one, it is not stalled but abandoned, so box it, date the lid, and if it is still unopened on that date next year it leaves unopened); the cable bag (the decision cannot be made one cable at a time, so empty the whole lot onto the cleared desk at once, pick up each cable, name the device it powers and point at where that device physically is in this house, no device no keep, then one spare of each connector type you still use daily and the rest to electronics recycling this week); what you are actually required to keep (permanent originals to one closed fireproof box and never the filing cabinet; tax paper one folder per year held for whatever your local retention period is, looked up once and written on the card; and everything else by the test that if the issuer will reprint it on request or it already lives in an online account, it is information, not a record, and information does not get a folder); the shelf that says who you used to be (ask when you last took it down since it arrived, not whether you might one day; if you cannot name a specific occasion and you could borrow or reorder it inside a week, it is not reference, it is display, and it must win a display slot in open competition; appliance manuals are simpler, model still in the house keep it, model gone it goes today); the backup printer (plug it in and print a test page right now, and if you will not plug it in today it is not a backup, it is a large object you have not decided about; the same blade settles the sealed cartridges, held against the model number on the machine you actually use, because cartridge value only falls); the deal you got (count the true stock and divide it by your honest annual use, out loud, because six letters a year against five hundred envelopes is a lifetime supply; cap it at one open plus one spare of anything used monthly and a single year of use for everything else, and the surplus goes to a school, a charity shop, or a neighbour this week, not to the top shelf and not to a box in the garage).

## Watch for in the home office (five safety questions)
- **Burn or fire**: a four-drawer cabinet of dense paper standing against a heater, and the overflow boxes stacked on top of a power strip, because paper is the fuel load of this room; a laser printer pushed tight against a wall or into a closed cupboard with no clearance behind it, and powered through a strip plugged into another strip, when the fuser needs air and the machine needs its own wall outlet.
- **Fall, cut, or crush**: a tall filing cabinet that tips forward onto you when the top drawer is loaded and pulled fully out; a pedestal that tips if the loaded file drawer is pulled right out while the top drawer is open; an unanchored shelf unit standing above the desk chair with the heaviest binders and box files loaded on its top shelf; reams of paper and boxes of envelopes stored above shoulder height, where a slipping box lands on your head and shoulders while your arms are up; loose scissors and a letter opener rolling free in a drawer you reach into without looking.
- **Fall**: charger cables and the power strip lead running across the floor where the chair moves and where you push back to stand; reaching the top shelf from a rolling office chair, which moves under you the moment you shift your weight.
- **Water and electricity**: a mug of coffee sitting behind the keyboard on the same surface as a power strip and a laptop charger; a plant or a water glass sitting on top of the printer lid, directly above the electronics and the paper path.
- **Poison, choke, or strangle**: loose button batteries rolling in a desk drawer a child can open, since a swallowed button battery is a medical emergency; a long charger or device cable left hanging in a loop within a small child's reach.
