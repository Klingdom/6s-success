# Chapter 44: The Home Office

Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks

**Subtitle:** The home office is the room where nothing is heavy and nothing is dirty, which is exactly why it has defeated you for years: every object in it asks for a judgment rather than a lift, the damp cloth that rescued every other room is the one thing that can ruin this one, and because it has a door that closes it has quietly become the place the whole household puts what it has not decided about. This playbook gives you, for each of the six zones, the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, so the decisions get made in batches, the electronics survive the clean, and you end with a surface you can sit down and start work on.

## Chapter Opening

The laundry room was a machine you live with. This one is a room you have to think in, and it fails in a way no room in this book has failed yet.

Walk into it and look honestly at what is stopping you. Nothing here is heavy. Nothing here is dirty. There is no grease, no lint, no damp, no grit walked in from outside. By every measure that made the other rooms hard, this should be the easiest room in the house. And yet it is almost certainly the one you have left the longest, and the one where a previous attempt ran out of steam somewhere around the second drawer.

The reason is that this room is not made of objects. It is made of decisions you have not taken. Every thing in here asks a question before it will move. This cable: what does it power, and is that thing still in the house? This book: are you the person who reads this now? This project you have not touched in six weeks: is it live or is it over? None of those is hard to lift. All of them are hard to answer, and an unanswered question is the heaviest object a house can hold.

Two other things make this room specific. The tool you have relied on all through Part Nine turns against you here: everything in this room is electronics or paper, and the damp cloth that rescued the kitchen is what destroys a keyboard or a drawer of files. And this is the room with a door that closes, which is why it has become where the household puts what it does not want to look at. Neither is a cleaning problem. Both change how you clean.

So the method inverts and the sequence matters. You batch the decisions instead of taking them one at a time. You spray the cloth and never the thing. You clear one surface first and then defend it, because it is the workbench every other zone will be emptied onto. And you make everything that is not office work leave through the door it came in.

## Six Zones, Not One Room

You do not clean a home office. You clear the desk, then the drawers under it, then the files, then the shelves, then the printer corner, then the supply cabinet. Each is a job with a clear finish. And the order here carries a particular weight, because the first zone is not just the most visible one, it is the one the other five depend on.

> The home office is six small zones, not one big job: the primary desk, the desk drawers and pedestal, the file storage, the bookshelf and reference zone, the printer and scanning station, and the supply cabinet. Do them one at a time, and start at the primary desk, because every other zone in this room gets emptied onto that surface at some point, so it has to be the first thing finished and the last thing you let refill.

One warning about pacing. Five of these zones take between half an hour and an hour and a quarter. The file storage does not: it is hundreds of keep-or-shred calls in a row and wants a fresh morning of its own, not the tail end of a session when your judgment is tired. The rest is an afternoon.

> A finished home office is one you can sit down and start working in without moving anything first: the monitor, keyboard, and mouse in fixed positions with one notebook and one inbox tray beside them, no sticky notes fringing the screen, the power strip hooked up off the floor so the chair rolls over bare carpet, every pen in the drawer writing and the scissors point-down in their own slot, folders named for the question you would ask when hunting, the permanent originals in a closed fireproof box, working reference at seated eye level with a hand's width of empty shelf at the end of every row, and one open pack plus one spare of anything you use monthly.

## The Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

The passes take on the character of a room made of judgments. Sort is where nearly all the work is, and its rule here is that you rule in batches: the whole cable drawer at once, every book off the shelf in one go, the entire supply cabinet lined up on the floor where you can see the true count. Straighten asks a question this room answers better than most, which is why you reach for a thing rather than what it is, so reference sits at seated eye level and daily hand tools never share a drawer with hanging files. Safety is quiet here and easy to miss precisely because everything looks light: paper is a fuel load, tall loaded things tip forward, and the rolling chair is not a step. Standardize is unusually literal in this room, because the ideal state is a photograph of a cleared desk taped where you will see it. And Sustain is one motion attached to the end of the working day: when the laptop lid closes, the surface goes back to match the photo before you stand up.

## How to Shine the Home Office: The Method

The nine rules of Shine hold here, but one of them moves to the front and does most of the work. You still go top to bottom, still clean back to front, still use two cloths, still match the cleaner to the surface, still clean in order to inspect and flag rather than to fix. What changes is that **dry before wet** stops being a sequencing tip and becomes the rule that saves your equipment.

On top of that, this room adds a layer of four, and it is the layer of a room that is made of decisions rather than dirt.

> The home office is the room where almost nothing is heavy and almost nothing is dirty, and that is precisely why it has sat untouched for years. Every other room asks you to lift, wipe, and carry. This one asks you to decide, of every object in it: this cable, this book, this sheet of paper, this half-finished thing in the corner. A room of decisions does not stall because the work is hard but because it is unresolved, and an unresolved decision is heavier than any box. So you clean it differently. You batch the decisions rather than take them one at a time. You treat the wet cloth as the risk, not the remedy, because everything here is electronics or paper. You clear one surface first and defend it, because it is the workbench every other zone empties onto. And you stop the room being where the household hides what it has not decided, because a door that closes is what turned it into that.

The first part explains why every previous attempt at this room ran out of steam.

> The reason this room has defeated you is not that it is dirty or difficult. It is made almost entirely of postponed decisions, and postponed decisions are the heaviest objects in a house. A cable is not hard to lift, it is hard to rule on. A book from a job you left weighs nothing and costs an enormous amount to release. This is why the advice to just tidy up fails here: tidying moves things, and nothing in this room is waiting to be moved, it is waiting to be judged. So the move that unlocks it is to take the decisions in batches. Empty the entire cable drawer onto the desk at once and rule on all of it in one sitting. Take every book off the shelf in one go. Line up the whole supply cabinet so you can see the true count. Made in one batch at a cleared surface, these are an afternoon. Made one item at a time, they never get made at all.

The second part is the one that protects several hundred pounds worth of equipment.

> In every other room in this book, water is your ally and the damp cloth does most of the work. In this one it is the hazard, because everything here is electronics or paper and both are ruined by it. So the method inverts. You power the devices down and let them cool. You spray the cleaner onto the cloth and never onto a screen, a scanner glass, or a vent, because liquid sprayed at a machine runs down inside it and finds the electronics. You wring the cloth almost dry before it goes near a keyboard. You use a dry cloth on toner dust, never a damp one, because water turns loose toner into ink. And you let a drawer bottom dry completely before the files return, because paper wicks up any damp you leave. Dry before wet was always one of the nine rules. In this room it is the rule that saves the equipment.

The third part is about sequence, and it is why the desk is not simply first.

> The desk is the only zone in this book that is also a piece of equipment. Every other zone gets emptied onto it: the drawer you tip out, the shelf you strip, the supply cabinet you count. So it is not the first zone because it is the most visible, it is first because nothing else in the room can be done properly without it. Clear it completely before you open a single drawer, then defend it, because the moment it refills you have lost the only workbench you had. That is why its standard is the strictest in the room: the desk holds the tools of the current task plus one inbox tray, and nothing else stays there overnight.

And the fourth part is not about office work at all.

> Look honestly at what is in this room that is not office work. The broken lamp. The unopened box from the last move. The bag of things with nowhere else to go. None of it is here because the office needed it. It is here because this is the room with a door that closes, and a door that closes is the perfect place to put something you have not decided about, since shutting it makes the decision disappear from view without ever being made. That is the room's real failure mode, and it is not a storage problem. Everything in here that is not office work walks out today, because anything that stays past today stops being temporary and becomes permanent furniture.

> **Quick Win**
> Clear the desk surface completely, down to bare wood, before you open a single drawer. Take everything off onto the floor and put back only what the task in front of you needs, plus one inbox tray. It takes twenty minutes, it gives you the workbench every other zone is going to need, and it changes how the room reads from the doorway more than anything else you will do in here.

> **6S Tip**
> Spray onto the cloth, never onto the thing. In a room of screens, scanner glass, keyboards, and paper, liquid sprayed at a surface runs down inside and finds the electronics. Power the device off, let it cool, mist a dry microfiber cloth, wipe in one direction, buff with a second dry cloth. On toner dust use a dry cloth only, because water turns loose toner into ink.

## Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the heart of the chapter. For each of the six zones you get the inputs to gather before you start and the exact order of steps to clean and shine it. Power down anything electrical before you touch it, work top to bottom, and remember that in this room the cloth is misted and the surface never is. Every zone finishes the same way, with a look for the things to flag for the Safety pass.

### Zone 1 · The Primary Desk

This is one clear surface for the work you are actually doing right now, and it is the workbench the other five zones will be emptied onto, so it gets finished first. Power everything down before you start, then work screen to floor. The two spots holding the most grime are the two you touch most: the screen and the underside lip where your forearms rest all day.

**Inputs:**

- A screen-safe electronics cleaner, and a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, several cloths, at least two of them bone dry.
- A compact vacuum with the crevice and upholstery tools, and your caddy.
- A cable management kit and a cord label set, and a hook for the power strip.

**Steps:**

1. Take everything off the surface and onto the floor, then power the machines down and let the screen and the lamp bulb cool before you touch either.
2. With the screen off and cool, spray the screen-safe cleaner onto a dry cloth, never onto the glass, and wipe in one direction so no drips run down into the seam, then buff with a second dry cloth and run a dry corner along the bezel groove where dust and skin flakes pack in.
3. Turn the keyboard upside down and shake it, run the vacuum crevice tool along the rows to lift crumbs and hair from between the keys, then wipe the key tops with a cloth wrung out almost dry so nothing runs under the keys.
4. Wipe the mouse body and wheel with a barely damp cloth, then flip it and clean the sensor window and the feet pads with a dry corner, because built-up fuzz there is what makes the pointer skip.
5. Once the bulb is cool, wipe the task lamp shade, arm, and base, lifting the baked-on dust off the top of the shade where the heat sets it, and keep the cloth clear of the bulb and the switch so no damp reaches the wiring.
6. Spray the neutral pH cleaner onto a cloth and wipe the whole desk top, then the front edge, then reach underneath to the lip where your forearms rest, which is the grubbiest part of the whole room, and give it a firm pass and a dry buff.
7. With everything still powered down, wipe the dust off the top of the power strip and run a dry cloth down each cable and along the cable tray, using a dry cloth only around anything electrical.
8. Vacuum the chair seat and back with the upholstery tool, wipe the armrests where hands and sleeves land, then tip the chair and pick the hair and thread out of each caster by hand so it rolls clean.
9. Move the chair aside and vacuum the whole arc it rolls through, under and around the desk and right into the corner where the strip hooks up, finishing with a floor-safe pass on sealed floor.
10. Hook the power strip up off the floor onto the desk leg, run the cables back through the management kit and label them, and check that nothing crosses the arc the chair rolls through or lies where your feet go.
11. Put back only what the task in front of you needs, plus one inbox tray, then photograph the cleared desk and tape the print inside the top drawer.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a charger lead that has frayed or split at the plug end, found as you handle each cable; a power strip whose body feels warm or whose sockets look browned or discoloured; any joint or bracket under the desk lip that has started to work loose when you push gently up and side to side; and a chair caster that no longer swivels or has cracked, worth catching before it seizes mid-roll. Flag each.

### Zone 2 · The Desk Drawers and Pedestal

These hold the small tools you reach for without looking, and the live files you touch weekly. The mess in a desk drawer hides under the divider and in the runner track rather than on top, so lift the tray right out. Clear the grit that jams the slide and the drawer will close with a fingertip again.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy.
- A drawer divider set, and removable write-on labels or a portable label maker.

**Steps:**

1. Tip the top drawer out onto the cleared desk and lift the divided tray right out, then tip the tray over the bin.
2. Run the detail brush along each slot of the tray to sweep out eraser crumbs and dried ink, wipe it with a barely damp cloth, and stand it aside to dry fully.
3. With the tray out, vacuum the drawer bottom and get the crevice tool right into the back corners where loose staples and paperclip fuzz collect, then wipe the base with cleaner on a wrung-out cloth and dry it.
4. Brush the grit out of both runner tracks with the detail brush, wipe each runner clean and dry, and slide the drawer a few times to confirm it moves without a shove before you refill it.
5. Lift the hanging folders out of the deep pedestal drawer in one block, vacuum the drawer floor, and wipe the two side rails the folders hang from.
6. Check that the pedestal base is completely dry before the files return, because paper wicks up any leftover damp.
7. Wipe the drawer fronts and the pulls where fingers grip, then run a cloth down the outside sides and across the top of the pedestal where dust settles unseen against the desk.
8. Roll the pedestal clear, vacuum the patch of floor it sat on, and clear the hair from its casters by hand so it tracks straight when you push it back.
9. Refill deliberately: daily hand tools in the divided tray up top, hanging files in the deep drawer, the two never mixed, scissors and the letter opener point-down in a fixed slot, and no loose button batteries anywhere in the drawer.
10. Label each drawer front with what it holds, in the words this house actually uses, so tape and stamps are found without pulling three drawers.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** any grinding, or a drawer that no longer closes flush, as it slides on its newly clean runner, which points to a bent runner or a loose fixing; rust bloom or a bent lip on the runner metal, flagged rather than forced past; a musty note in the deep file drawer while it is empty, which means damp is reaching the paper; and a drawer pull whose screw has backed out or that has gone loose in your hand. Flag each.

### Zone 3 · The File Storage

This holds the documents you are genuinely required to keep, and lets you put your hand on any one of them without opening a second drawer. Give it a fresh morning. This is the one zone where cleaning and inspecting are literally the same job, because you cannot check a drawer bottom for damp or a receipt for fading without emptying the drawer first.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy.
- A fireproof document box for the permanent originals, a cross-cut shredder, and a document file box.
- A moisture absorber or humidity monitor, and anti-tip anchors and hardware.

**Steps:**

1. Clear whatever has landed on top of the cabinet and wipe the top with cleaner on a cloth, because dust falls heaviest here and drifts down into the open drawers.
2. Working one drawer at a time and never more than one open at once, lift the folders out and set them on the cleared desk.
3. Vacuum the drawer floor and the back corners, then wipe the bottom with a wrung-out cloth and let it dry fully before any paper goes back.
4. Wipe both side rails the hanging folders ride on and run the cloth along the runner track, because a rail with grit on it drags every folder you pull.
5. Wipe the drawer faces and pull handles where hands mark them, then the sides of the cabinet, and give a painted steel front a dry buff so it does not streak.
6. Wipe the outer shell and lid seam of the fireproof box with a barely damp cloth, keeping water away from the seal, and clear the dust from the hinge so the lid still closes flush.
7. Ease the cabinet out if it moves, or use the crevice tool to reach behind and beneath it, and vacuum the dust and any silverfish sheltering in that dark gap.
8. Refile as you go by the question you would ask when hunting, so car, house, health, school, rather than by document type, with tabs all standing in one line.
9. Move the permanent originals out of any cardboard box on top of the cabinet and into the closed fireproof box at floor level, and check the paper mass is clear of the power strip and any heater.
10. Anchor the cabinet to the wall, then write your retention rule on a card and tape it inside the top drawer, naming which categories are permanent, which run by tax year, and which get scanned.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a damp patch or a cupped, softened drawer base that says water is getting in; silverfish or their droppings in the corners and behind the cabinet, noted as a pest sign; any thermal receipt whose print has begun to grey and fade, flagged so a copy can be made before it disappears entirely; and a rock or a lean when you give the empty cabinet a gentle push, meaning it is not sitting square on the floor. Flag each.

### Zone 4 · The Bookshelf and Reference Zone

This holds the reference you genuinely reach for, at the height you reach for it from. Dust falls, so this unit is cleaned strictly from the very top down, and the places it actually settles are the two nobody looks at: the top of the tallest shelf and the top edges of the books themselves.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, with dry cloths for the tops and the glass.
- A compact vacuum with the dusting brush and crevice tools, and your caddy.
- A stable household step stool, anti-tip anchors and hardware, and bookends.

**Steps:**

1. Take every book, binder, and manual off the shelves in one go rather than shelf by shelf, so the sorting is one batch decision rather than six small ones.
2. Standing on the step stool and never on the desk chair, reach up with a dry cloth and clear the whole top surface of the unit first, because this is where the room's dust really gathers and it drops onto everything below when disturbed.
3. Tilt each book and binder out slightly and run the vacuum dusting brush along its top edge, then wipe the group with a dry cloth, since this is the grit a shelf wipe alone always leaves behind.
4. Wipe each shelf board with cleaner on a wrung-out cloth, then the front edge where hands grab to pull a spine out, and dry each board before anything returns.
5. Lift each box file and wipe its lid and sides, and clean the glass of any framed award with a dry cloth so no wet residue runs behind the frame.
6. Wipe the bracket or strap that ties the unit to the wall and clear the dust from around the fixing so you can actually see it while you work.
7. Vacuum along the bottom shelf, the skirting, and as far behind the unit as the crevice tool reaches, since this gap is where dust bunnies and dropped papers hide.
8. Reload by why you reach: working reference at seated eye level from the desk chair, occasional reading at standing height, archive boxes highest, and the heavy binders and box files on the bottom two shelves.
9. Close each group with a bookend and leave a hand's width of empty shelf at the end of every row, then check the unit is screwed into the wall studs and that nothing heavy or glass sits on the shelf above where your head is when you work.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** any bow or sag in a shelf board when you sight along it under the weight of the binders; a wall anchor strap gone slack or a screw head standing proud of the wall; a wobble or lean away from the wall when you nudge the unit with a shelf empty; and a damp or cold patch on the wall behind the top shelf that could be softening the boards over time. Flag each.

### Zone 5 · The Printer and Scanning Station

This turns paper into files and files into paper without hunting for either. Power off, then work from the glass down to the floor. The rule that saves the machine is the same one that governs the room: the cleaner goes onto the cloth, never onto the glass or into a vent, where it runs inside and finds the electronics.

**Inputs:**

- A streak-free glass and mirror cleaner, and a screen-safe electronics cleaner.
- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, keeping one cloth dry and reserved for toner.
- A compact vacuum with the crevice and brush tools, and your caddy.
- Min-max inventory labels for the paper bin, and a labelled box for the spare cartridge.

**Steps:**

1. Power the machine off, and take anything sitting on the lid down and out of the zone, plant and drink included.
2. Lift the lid and clean the scanner glass with the glass cleaner sprayed onto the cloth, never onto the glass itself, wiping in one direction and buffing dry.
3. Wipe the underside of the lid, where marks transfer onto every scan you make.
4. Wipe the closed lid, the top, and the outer body panels with neutral pH cleaner on a wrung-out cloth.
5. Wipe the control panel and its buttons or touchscreen with a barely damp cloth, sprayed onto the cloth and wrung out, so no liquid sits in the button gaps where every fingerprint on the machine collects.
6. Use a dry cloth only for toner dust inside the feed area, never a damp one, and vacuum the paper lint that gathers around the feed rollers and the tray edges.
7. Wipe the output tray where sheets stack, then brush and vacuum the vents and back panel clear of dust, keeping everything dry since this is the hottest and most electrical part of the machine.
8. Wipe out the paper bin beneath the printer and the labelled spare-cartridge box beside it, then run a dry cloth down the power cord from the machine to the wall.
9. Vacuum the floor around and behind the station and clear anything that has crept into the gap of clear air the machine needs at its back.
10. Set the zone back: paper directly under the printer with a min line and a max line drawn inside the bin, one matching spare cartridge in its labelled box, a single scan tray with a to-do side and a done side, and the machine plugged straight into a wall outlet rather than a strip chained off another strip.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** any spot where the machine sits tight against the wall with no clear air behind it, found as you dust the back and vents; a frayed section on the power cord, or a plug that feels warm or looks browned at the wall; a cracked or sagging lid hinge that no longer holds the lid where you put it; and heavy toner dust building on the paper path or scoring marks on feeding sheets, which point to a roller starting to fail. Flag each.

### Zone 6 · The Supply Cabinet

This holds the consumables this office genuinely burns through in a year, and no more. A supply cabinet cleans fast, so the real work here is looking while you wipe: the leaked pen, the split glue tube, the sticky ring under a roll of tape gone soft. Catch the seep before it spreads across the shelf.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy.
- A stable household step stool, min-max inventory labels, and a shelf location label set.

**Steps:**

1. Bring everything out and line it up on the floor in categories, so you can see the true count, because a dozen packs of sticky notes look like nothing until they stand side by side.
2. Clear the top of the cabinet and wipe it first with cleaner on a cloth, so the dust gathering up there does not fall onto the shelves you are about to clean.
3. Take one shelf at a time down to bare board, wipe it with a wrung-out cloth, and dry it before anything goes back, doing the front edge where hands grip and pull.
4. Run the detail brush into the back corners and along the shelf-pin holes and hinges to lift the grit and dried spills a flat cloth skips over.
5. Slide the heavy reams and boxes forward on the bottom shelf, vacuum the shelf beneath them, and wipe the dust off the box lids before you push them back low where they belong.
6. Wipe the door fronts and handles inside and out, then wipe down the step stool that lives in the corner of this room so it stays clean and grippy underfoot.
7. Vacuum the floor under the cabinet and the strip in front of it, clearing dropped clips, staples, and dust so nothing rolls underfoot when you reach in.
8. Reload with one open pack of each consumable at hand height and its backstock immediately behind it on the same shelf, so restocking the pen tray is a single reach rather than a hunt through three boxes of envelopes.
9. Put the reams of paper and the boxes of envelopes on the bottom shelf, never overhead, and write min and max on a card at the front edge of each shelf so a low stock is visible from the doorway.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a pen that has leaked, a marker that has wept, or a glue tube that has split, caught as you clear each shelf and before the mess spreads; a sticky ring under a roll of tape or a bottle, and the item that is going soft or seeping; a shelf board that bows or a shelf pin that has slipped under the weight of the paper, found by sighting along each loaded shelf; and a wobbly leg, a loose step, or a worn foot on the step stool, flagged before someone stands on it. Flag each.

## The Calls That Matter Here

Six decisions in this room are the whole job, and every one of them is a question you have been declining to answer.

The half-finished thing. One corner of the desk is held by a project you cannot put away, because putting it away feels like admitting it is over. Say the next physical action out loud in one sentence. If you can name it, call the insurer, sign page four, it is live: write that sentence on a card, clip it to the papers, and put it in the inbox tray rather than back on the desk. If you cannot name one, it is not stalled, it is abandoned. Box it, date the lid, and if it is still unopened on that date next year, it leaves unopened.

The cable bag. Somewhere in the pedestal there is a bag of cables, adapters, and wall bricks, and it survives because each individual cable is too cheap to think about and too specific to throw away. That decision cannot be made one cable at a time. Empty the whole lot onto the cleared desk at once, pick up each cable, name the device it powers, and point at where that device physically is in this house. No device, no keep. Then keep exactly one spare of each connector type you still use daily, coil and band it, and take the rest to electronics recycling this week. Made in one batch, it is a single sitting. Made one cable at a time, it never gets made at all.

What you are actually required to keep. Most of the drawer is neither obviously permanent nor obviously rubbish, and that middle band is why the job stalls. Split it three ways. Permanent originals, birth and marriage certificates, deeds, vehicle titles, wills, immigration papers, go into one closed fireproof box and never the filing cabinet. Tax returns and their supporting paper get one folder per year, held for your local retention period, which you look up once and write on the card. Everything else goes by one test: if the issuer will reprint it on request, or it already lives in an online account, it is information, not a record, and information does not get a folder.

The shelf that says who you used to be. The textbooks from a degree, the professional library from a job you left, the reference set a website now answers better and faster. These are the hardest books in the house to release, because they stand for a version of you that you have not formally retired. Ask when you last took it down since it arrived on this shelf, not whether you might one day. If you cannot name a specific occasion, and you could borrow or reorder it inside a week, it is no longer reference, it is display, and it must win a display slot in open competition with everything else on the shelf. Most will not. Appliance manuals are simpler: model still in the house, keep it; model gone, it goes today.

The backup printer. The old printer is still in the corner because it might be a backup. Settle it before you leave this corner today: plug it in and print a test page right now. If it prints, and you are prepared to keep a spare cartridge for it in stock, it is a backup and it gets a home. If you will not plug it in today, it is not a backup, it is a large object you have not decided about, and it goes to electronics recycling this week. The same blade settles the sealed cartridges: hold each box against the model number on the machine you actually use, and anything unmatched sells this week, because cartridge value only falls.

The deal you got. The bulk box of five hundred envelopes, the multipack of sticky notes, the case of paper bought because the price was good. The hard part is that you were not wrong about the price, so admitting the surplus was a mistake feels like throwing money away twice. It is not: the money left when you bought it, and the shelf space is being paid for now. Count the true stock and divide it by your honest annual use, out loud. Six letters a year against five hundred envelopes is a lifetime supply. Cap it at one open plus one spare of anything used monthly, a single year of use for everything else, and the surplus goes to a school, a charity shop, or a neighbour this week.

> **The Door That Closes Challenge**
> Walk into this room and name out loud every object that is not office work. The broken lamp, the unopened box from the last move, the bag of things with nowhere else to go. Every one is here because this room has a door that can be shut. All of it walks out today, because anything still here tomorrow has stopped being temporary and become permanent furniture.

## Watch for in the Home Office

The hazards here are quiet, and they are easy to miss for one reason: almost everything in the room is light, and light gets mistaken for safe.

> The hazards in this room are quiet, and most of them come from treating light objects as though light meant safe. Paper is the fuel load here, so a loaded filing cabinet does not stand against a heater and the overflow boxes never sit on a power strip. Anything tall and loaded tips forward onto the person opening it, so the cabinet and the shelf unit get anchored to the wall, the heavy binders and reams live on the bottom shelves rather than above the chair you sit in, and you open one drawer at a time. The rolling chair is not a step, because it moves the instant you shift your weight, so a real step stool lives in this room. Then the wiring: the power strip comes up off the floor and out of the arc the chair rolls through, drinks sit on the far side of the mouse, nothing rests on the printer lid, and a laser printer gets clear air at its back and its own wall outlet rather than a strip chained off another, because the fuser runs genuinely hot. And the drawer you reach into blind holds its scissors point-down in a fixed slot, with no loose button batteries rolling in it.

Burn or fire is paper meeting heat or power: a cabinet of dense paper against a heater with overflow boxes on a power strip, and a laser printer tight against a wall or in a closed cupboard, run off a strip plugged into another strip.

Fall, cut, or crush has the longest list here, and every item is something tall or heavy treated as harmless: a filing cabinet that tips when a loaded top drawer is pulled fully out, a pedestal that does the same, an unanchored shelf unit above the chair you sit in with the heaviest binders on top, reams stored above shoulder height, and loose scissors rolling in a drawer you reach into blind.

Fall is the cabling across the arc the chair rolls through, and reaching a top shelf from a rolling chair.

Water and electricity is drinks, twice: a mug behind the keyboard beside a power strip and a charger, and a plant or glass on the printer lid above the electronics.

Poison, choke, or strangle is thin in an adult office and real the moment a small child can open the door: loose button batteries in a drawer, a medical emergency if swallowed, and a long cable hanging in a loop within reach.

> **Common Mistake**
> Doing this room one item at a time, so the cable drawer is still a cable drawer after three sessions; taking a damp cloth to a screen or keyboard because that is what worked everywhere else; and starting the filing cabinet at the end of a session, when it is hundreds of keep-or-shred calls in a row and needs a fresh morning.
