# Chapter 47 Signature: The Mudroom

The Part 9 recurring close. The friction meter is retired (it reached GOAL at Chapter 30); each room playbook ends instead on a matched before-and-after room signature: the before kept per Chapter 6, the after held as the ideal-state standard per Chapter 25.

## The before-and-after room signature
Stand in the doorway, on a wet day if you can, with the hook wall, the bench, and the floor in one frame. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and do not pick the bags up off the ground first, because where they are sitting is the whole diagnosis. Then work the six zones, hooks first and floor last, and take the after from the same spot.

## No variation, but a specific thing to look at
This chapter uses the plain Part 9 close. What it adds is an instruction about **where to look** in the pair: the narrow strip of floor along the run of hooks. That band is where the change actually shows, because it is the surface that fills up when the wall runs out of room. A reader comparing the two photographs anywhere else may see very little difference; comparing that strip, they will see the whole chapter.

The second measure is a use test rather than a look test: whether the household can come through wet on a dark evening and leave nothing behind. That is stated in the frozen caption and it is the honest criterion for this room, because a mudroom that photographs well on a Sunday afternoon has not been tested at all.

Compare with the variations used so far: Ch 39 the child takes the after photo; Ch 40 the after is the nightly safe state; Ch 43 the after contains one detail that expires; Ch 44 the after is taped inside a drawer and matched nightly; Ch 45 the after keeps working as a diagnostic floor; Ch 46 the after is judged on time-to-first-cut.

## The before-and-after caption (frozen, byte-verbatim)
> This is the mudroom the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, one named column per person and bare floor along the run beneath, a bench you could sit two people on, the wet tray exactly where boots come off, the lead and bag roll hanging as one kit, and the broom and mop clipped clear of the floor. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then judge it as this room asks: not by how much it holds, but by whether the household can come through it wet on a dark evening and leave nothing behind.

## One Idea to Keep (frozen, byte-verbatim)
> A mudroom is not tidy because you found somewhere to put everything. It works because it catches: the grit stops at three layers before it reaches your floor, every person has a named column at their own reach so nothing has to go down onto the ground, and nothing in here has a lid to hide behind. Do one zone this week, and make it the hook wall, with the household standing there, because almost everything on that floor is only there because the wall above it ran out of room.
