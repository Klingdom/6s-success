# Chapter 47 LinkedIn Posts: The Mudroom

Ten standalone posts, each under 150 words. Product types only, no brands.

## 1. The room that catches
The mudroom is where the weather stops. Wet boots, muddy paws, dripping umbrellas, and the coat wall that grows a layer every October.

It is the first room in this book whose job is not to store things but to catch them, which means it is judged by what gets past it rather than by what it holds. A mudroom containing a great deal, neatly, has usually failed.

## 2. The floor is a symptom
Look at what is lying on your mudroom floor. Almost all of it is there for one reason: the wall above it ran out of room. A bag on the ground is a hook that was full. A boot in the walking line is a rack with no space left.

So the floor is a symptom and the wall is the cause. Clean the floor first and you will be cleaning it again within a week.

## 3. Three layers at the threshold
Grit does its damage underfoot, because grit ground into a floor sands the finish off it. So beat it at the door in three layers: a stiff bristle mat outside to scrape, an absorbent mat inside to soak, and a lipped tray for anything still dripping. Those three do more than any amount of wiping further in.

## 4. A loaded mat is not a mat
The threshold layers only work if they are maintained. Shake the outside mat and rinse the tray on the same day, because a mat that is already full of grit has stopped being a mat. It has become a supply of grit sitting at your door.

## 5. Do not buy anything with a lid
A mudroom is one door from the street, so it becomes the holding bay for the whole house: the returns, the charity bag, the kit for a season that finished in May. The instinct is to buy something with a lid on it, and that is exactly wrong. A lid hides the drift. This is the one room where the accumulation needs to stay visible.

## 6. Hand each coat to its owner
Do the hook wall with the household standing there. Not out of politeness, but because a coat is the most personal object in the room, and a coat removed by someone else is a coat that comes back. Hand each one to its owner and the decision sticks.

## 7. A named column at their own reach
Give every person a vertical strip of wall rather than a shared rail, with their hooks at their own height. A child who can reach their own hook stops leaving their coat on the floor. That one change does more than any amount of asking.

## 8. Where the tray actually goes
A wet sole carried past the tray does not leave a puddle. It leaves an invisible film, a few steps long, exactly where everyone turns towards the kitchen, and it is still there when the next person comes through in socks.

So the tray goes where boots actually come off, which is usually a step or two further in than people think.

## 9. The good coat you have not worn since March
The hard one is never the ugly coat. Hang everything, then at the end of one week take down any coat still hanging exactly as you left it and move it to a wardrobe. It is not gone. It is just not entitled to a hook.

## 10. The test that matters
"Judge it not by how much it holds, but by whether the household can come through it wet on a dark evening and leave nothing behind."
