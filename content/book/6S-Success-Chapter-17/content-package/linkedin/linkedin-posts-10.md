# Chapter 17 LinkedIn Posts: Shine Targets and Assignments

Ten standalone posts. Each is useful on its own, each under 150 words, each with a soft CTA. The CTA wording rotates. Anti-shame voice, fairness not lecture, permission not exhortation, no force metaphors.

---

## 1. One person, every time

In most homes, all the cleaning quietly falls to one person. Not by decision. By default.

Here is why. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time. So every unassigned job flows steadily toward them, like water finding the low point.

Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it.

The fix is not for that person to try harder or nag more. It is to end the default by writing a name against each line, so the target has an owner before anyone's patience runs out.

A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target.

## 2. Two walls

You cleaned one corner and it worked. So you think: fine, I will clean the rest of it too.

That thought runs into two walls almost at once.

The first is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning. You know the surfaces you look at, not the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet.

The second is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it?

Chapter 17 takes both walls in order. First we make what needs cleaning visible, grouped so nothing hides. Then we put a name against every target, so the work is shared instead of silently dumped.

We are not cleaning anything new today. We are making the list, and sharing it.

## 3. See targets by kind, not by room

A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks into a room and lands where it always lands.

The useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. There are four kinds, and between them they catch almost everything.

Surfaces: floors, walls, windows, counters. Equipment and fixtures: appliances, taps, furniture, tools. Stored things: whatever lives inside cabinets, closets, and drawers. Point of use: the handles, switches, and remotes your hands hit a hundred times a day.

That last one is the kind people forget most, and naming it is what pulls it into view.

Sorting by kind drags the overlooked targets into the light.

## 4. You can share a list

The invisible pile of things you feel you ought to get to has a strange property: you cannot hand any of it to anyone.

That is because it lives only in your head, as unease. A target that lives only in your sense of unease gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all.

Write it down and everything changes. A target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up. The list turns a feeling into a finite, shareable set of lines.

You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list.

That is why half the value of a Shine list is done just by writing it, before a single name goes on it.

## 5. Clean the whole thing

The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters.

The front of the microwave wipes clean in a second and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that costs you the microwave. The fridge looks fine; the coils behind it are why it runs hot and dies early. The sink gleams; the overflow hole is where the smell is coming from.

Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails.

Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows.

A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads.

## 6. The Shine list

A sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. That is the whole device.

A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands.

Down the page, write the four kinds as headings. Then walk your home, category by category, and list the real targets under each. Then, the harder half: a name against every line.

Do not aim for a perfect list on the first pass. A first list is usually longer than people expect, and that is just the first time it has all been written in one place instead of carried in one head.

## 7. Assign every line, including the ones you would keep

Here is the move most cleaning plans skip.

Go down your list and assign every line. Not most lines. Every line, including the ones you would have silently kept for yourself.

Because a target with no name is a target that has just been quietly assigned back to the usual person, and the whole point was to stop doing that.

A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching.

When each line has an owner, two things change at once: the work is genuinely shared, and each target finally has someone whose job it is to read it.

## 8. Let people choose

There is a right way and a wrong way to hand out the work, and the difference is whether people choose or get chosen for.

A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent.

So make the list together and let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers. Ownership you picked survives a bad week where an assignment does not.

Match jobs to who can actually do them: a child can genuinely own the light switches and low door handles, and will do it proudly if it is theirs. An adult takes the oven and the fridge coils.

And the jobs nobody wants? Share them out, or rotate them, instead of leaving them all with one person.

## 9. A home kept by everyone

When your room became their room a couple of chapters ago, something shifted. A Shine list assigned together does the same thing to the cleaning.

It stops being one person's endless private duty and becomes something the whole household holds. A few targets each, out in the open, fair.

This is not more work. A list with a name on every line is the same work, finally shared.

And it is the relief that matters most: a home kept by everyone asks less of anyone, and it stops depending on a single person never getting tired.

You cannot share a feeling of obligation. But you can share a list.

## 10. The needle holds, on purpose

Go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice that it does not move, and that this is correct.

Last chapter the needle took its first Shine step, because you actually cleaned something. This chapter you made a list and put names to it, which is real and necessary work, but nothing new got cleaned today.

So the needle holds exactly where the first Shine step left it, because the meter has told the truth for sixteen chapters by only moving when the room itself changed.

But just ahead of it is a new dashed arrow, projecting toward the goal. The plan is the loaded spring. The move is coming; it is simply honest to say it has not happened yet.
