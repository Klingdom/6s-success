# In Most Homes, All the Cleaning Quietly Falls to One Person. Here Is How to Fix That With a Sheet of Paper.

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Last chapter you cleaned one corner of your home and let the cleaning show you what it had been hiding. It worked, and it was meant to be small: one zone, one cloth, proof that a wipe can do two jobs. But a home is not one corner, and if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet, exactly as before. So the obvious next thought is, fine, I will clean the rest of it too. And that thought runs into two walls almost at once.

The first wall is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning. You know the surfaces you look at, not the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the handle of every door in the house. The second wall is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? Because right now, in most homes, the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most.

This chapter takes both walls in order. First we make what needs cleaning visible, all of it, grouped so nothing hides. Then we do the thing that makes Shine survive contact with a real household: we put a name against every target. Not to police anyone. To be fair. We are not cleaning anything new today. We are making the list, and sharing it.

## See targets by kind, not by room

Before you can list what needs cleaning, you need to be able to see it, and the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks into a room and lands where it always lands. Sorting the world into a few kinds of target drags the overlooked ones into the light.

There are four kinds. Surfaces: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors. Equipment and fixtures: the appliances, taps and sinks, furniture, and tools, the working things that both get dirty and can quietly fail. Stored things: whatever lives inside the cabinets, closets, drawers, and boxes, which need cleaning and checking too. Point of use, the one people forget most: the high-touch spots your hands hit a hundred times a day, the handles, the switches, the remotes.

## The Shine list

Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. Down the page, write the four kinds as headings, then walk your home category by category and list the real targets under each. Not every object you own; the things that genuinely need regular cleaning and inspection.

**A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands.**

The first half of its value is done just by writing it. A target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up. A target that lives only in your sense of unease gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list.

## Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows

There is a reason the list is worth making rather than just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in this chapter.

**Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows.**

The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that costs you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it are why it runs hot and dies early. Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails.

**The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads.**

## Put a name on every target

Here is where most cleaning plans quietly die, and where this one is built to survive. A list of targets with no names attached is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with. It has to become a list with a name on every line, and the name is the whole difference between a plan that happens and a plan that does not.

**A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target.**

Think about why the load falls on one person. It is rarely a decision; it is a default. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time. Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it.

**When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching.**

## Let people choose, do not hand out

A name on every line is the goal, but how the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year.

**A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person.**

Make the list together and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned. Match the targets to who can actually do them: a young child can genuinely own the light switches and the low door handles, and will do it proudly if it is theirs; an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. And the jobs nobody wants, do not leave those to default back to one person. Share them out, or rotate them. A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone, and it stops depending on a single person never getting tired.

## The needle holds, on purpose

Go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice that it does not move, and that this is correct. Last chapter the needle took its first Shine step because you actually cleaned something and caught a problem. This chapter you made a list and put names to it, which is real and necessary work, but nothing new got cleaned today. So the needle holds exactly where the first Shine step left it, because the meter has told the truth for sixteen chapters by only moving when the room itself changed.

But look just ahead of the live needle and you will see a dashed arrow, projecting forward toward the goal, drawn now because you have earned the projection even if you have not yet earned the move. The plan is the loaded spring. The move is coming; it is simply honest to say it has not happened yet.

## The assignment

Walk one room and write down five things that need regular cleaning but that nobody has ever been assigned, the top of something, the inside of something, a handle everyone touches. Do not clean them yet; just name them. You just started your Shine list. Then, if you can, make the rest of it with the people you live with, and let them pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers.

**List every target that needs cleaning, group it by kind so nothing hides, and put a name against each one. A Shine you have written down and shared is a Shine that actually happens, and it happens to the whole home, not just the parts that show and not just to the one person who always ends up doing it.**
