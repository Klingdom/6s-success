# Podcast Script: Shine Targets and Assignments

**Chapter 17, 6S Success: Home Edition (Part 5, Shine)**
**Slug:** shine-targets-and-assignments
**Estimated runtime:** 8 to 12 minutes (roughly 1,600 spoken words)
**Format:** Conversational, audio-first, single host. No reliance on visuals.

---

## INTRO

Hey, welcome back. Last time we stood at the door together and did something small and real: we cleaned one corner, the launch pad, and let the cleaning show us what it had been hiding. It worked. It caught two little problems while they were still small. And it was meant to be small, one zone, one cloth, proof that a wipe can do two jobs.

But here is the thought that shows up the moment you finish. A home is not one corner. And if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet, exactly as before. So the obvious next move is, fine, I will clean the rest of it too. And that thought runs straight into two walls.

The first wall is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning. You know the surfaces you look at. You do not have a clear picture of the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the underside of the bath mat, the handle of every door in the house. The second wall is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? Because in most homes, the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most, carrying the whole invisible load alone. This episode is about those two walls. And we take them in order.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: ONE ZONE CLEAN, A WHOLE HOUSE UNNAMED

Let me be honest about what that launch pad pass did and did not do, because it matters for what comes next. It did something real: it proved that cleaning and inspecting are the same motion, and it caught two small problems on one small zone. What it did not do is scale. You cannot run your whole home the way you ran that corner, on a whim, one surface at a time, whenever you happen to think of it. Because the things that most need Shine are precisely the things you never happen to think of.

That is the trap of cleaning by instinct. Instinct cleans what is in front of you and what is bothering you today, which means it cleans the same visible handful over and over and never once touches the rest. The kitchen counter gets wiped daily; the top of the kitchen cupboards gets wiped when you move house. The bathroom sink is cleaned constantly; the extractor fan clogged with dust has not been looked at in years. None of this is laziness. It is just what happens with no plan: the loud targets get all the attention and the quiet ones get none, until a quiet one fails.

And underneath the what sits the harder question, the who. In most homes the cleaning is not shared so much as absorbed, by one person, invisibly, until it feels like their personality rather than a pile of unassigned jobs. This chapter fixes both problems with one artifact, and the artifact is almost comically simple: a list. But a list built a particular way, and shared a particular way. So the usual fence before we start. We are not cleaning anything new today. We are making the list, and sharing it.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: WHAT COUNTS AS A TARGET

Before you can list what needs cleaning, you have to be able to see it, and the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks into a room and lands where it always lands. Sorting the world into a few kinds of target drags the overlooked ones into the light. There are four kinds, and between them they catch almost everything.

The first kind is surfaces: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors, the flat faces of the home that show dirt and take the most traffic. The second is equipment and fixtures: the appliances, the taps and sinks, the furniture, the tools, the working things that both get dirty and can quietly fail. The third is stored things: whatever lives inside the cabinets, closets, drawers, and boxes, which need cleaning and checking too, even though a closed door hides them. And the fourth, the one people forget most, is point of use: the high-touch spots your hands hit a hundred times a day, the handles, the switches, the remotes, the pull on the launch pad drawer, the places that get the most human contact and almost none of the cleaning.

Run those four kinds through your home like a net and you will be surprised what surfaces, in both senses. The point of the four categories is not to be exhaustive or official. It is to give your attention four different angles of approach, so the targets that hide from one angle get caught by another.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: THE SHINE LIST

Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. Down the page, write the four kinds as headings: surfaces, equipment and fixtures, stored things, point of use. Then walk your home, category by category, and list the real targets under each. Not every object you own; the things that genuinely need regular cleaning and inspection. Under surfaces, the kitchen floor, the bathroom mirror, the tops of the cupboards. Under equipment, the oven, the washing machine, the bathroom fan. Under stored things, the pantry shelves, the medicine cabinet. Under point of use, the door handles, the light switches, the remote.

So here is the definition, and it is worth keeping. A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands.

And half of its value is already done just by writing it. A target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up. A target that lives only in your sense of unease is a target that gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. The list takes the invisible pile of things you feel you ought to get to and turns it into a finite, shareable set of lines. And that matters, because you cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list. Do not aim for perfect on the first pass, and do not let the size of it alarm you. A first Shine list is longer than people expect, and that is not a verdict on your home; it is just the first time all of this has been written in one place instead of carried in one head.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: CLEAN THE WHOLE THING, NOT JUST THE PART THAT SHOWS

There is a reason the list is worth making rather than just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in this chapter. Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows.

The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean in a second and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that will eventually cost you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it, furred with dust, are why it runs hot and dies early. The bathroom sink gleams; the overflow hole you never look into is where the smell is coming from. Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails.

So keep this line. The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads. This is exactly why the list beats instinct. Instinct is drawn to the visible face; the list, built by kind, deliberately includes the tops and the backs and the undersides and the insides, the targets your eye slides past precisely because they do not show. You are not committing to scrubbing all of it today. You are making sure that when the cleaning does happen, it reaches the parts that were quietly deciding how long your things last.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FIVE: PUT A NAME ON EVERY TARGET

Here is where most cleaning plans quietly die, and where this one is built to survive. A list of targets with no names attached is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with. It has to become a list with a name on every line. A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target.

Think about why the load falls on one person in the first place. It is rarely a decision. It is a default. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time, so every unassigned target flows steadily toward them like water finding the low point. Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it. And the fix is not for that person to try harder or to nag more; it is to end the default by writing a name against each line, so the target has an owner before anyone's patience runs out.

So hear this plainly. When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching. So go down your list and assign every line. Not most lines. Every line, including the ones you would have silently kept for yourself. A target with no name is a target that has just been quietly assigned back to the usual person, and the point of this whole chapter was to stop doing that.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART SIX: CHOOSING, NOT ASSIGNING

A name on every line is the goal, but how the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year. And there is a right way and a wrong way. A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person.

So make the list together, and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned. Ownership you picked feels different from ownership you were saddled with, and it survives a bad week where an assignment does not. Match the targets to who can actually do them: a young child can genuinely own the light switches and the low door handles, and will do it proudly if it is theirs; an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. And the jobs nobody wants, the bins, the bathroom, the thing everyone wrinkles their nose at, do not leave those to default back to one person either. Share them out, or rotate them, so the worst of it moves around instead of always landing in the same place.

This is the same shift you made earlier in the book, when the room map turned your room into their room. A Shine list assigned together does the same thing to the cleaning: it stops being one person's endless private duty and becomes something the whole household holds, a few targets each, out in the open, fair. A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone, and it stops depending on a single person never getting tired.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART SEVEN: THE NEEDLE HOLDS

Now go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice that it does not move, and that this is correct.

Last chapter the needle took its first Shine step, because you actually cleaned something and caught a problem; the room's condition changed. This chapter you made a list and put names to it, which is real and necessary work, but nothing new got cleaned today. Not one target was scrubbed, not one surface was read that was not read yesterday. So the needle holds exactly where the first Shine step left it, and it should, because the meter has told the truth for sixteen chapters by only moving when the room itself changed. A planning chapter that nudged the needle would be a small lie.

But look just ahead of the live needle and you will see something new: a dashed arrow, projecting forward along the dial toward the goal. That is where the needle goes next, and it is drawn now because you have earned the projection even if you have not yet earned the move. You have a complete list of targets and a name on every one. The moment that plan is carried out, when the targets get their cleaning in the next chapter and the whole home starts getting read on a rhythm in the one after, the needle will follow that dashed arrow and step again. The plan is the loaded spring. The move is coming; it is simply honest to say it has not happened yet. The goal, the crosshair out on the calm side, is still ahead, as it has been all through Shine.

---

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So if you take one thing from this episode, take this: list every target that needs cleaning, group it by kind so nothing hides, and put a name against each one. A Shine you have written down and shared is a Shine that actually happens, and it happens to the whole home, not just the parts that show and not just to the one person who always ends up doing it.

And here is your assignment, and it is small on purpose. Walk one room and write down five things that need regular cleaning but that nobody has ever been assigned, the top of something, the inside of something, a handle everyone touches. You just started your Shine list. And if you want to make it a family thing, make the list together and let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers. A job you chose is a job you will actually do; a job you were handed is a job you will resent.

## OUTRO AND SOFT CTA

So that is Shine getting a plan. You did two things today that instinct never does on its own: you made the whole of the cleaning visible, grouped so the hidden targets could not hide, and you shared it out with a name on every line. That is the what and the who of Shine, settled for the whole home, not just one corner. The launch pad is no longer a lucky exception; it is the first entry on a list that now covers everything, owned by everyone.

What you have not done is the how, and that is deliberate, because knowing what to clean and who cleans it does not yet tell anyone how to clean it well. So that is next. For now, the plan is real and it is shared, which is further than most homes ever get. You know what needs reading, and you know whose job it is to read it.
