## SECTION 1: WHAT COUNTS AS A TARGET

[HOST]
Before you can list what needs cleaning, you have to be able to see it. And the trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind.

[VISUAL: A calm four-group reference. Surfaces (a floor, a window, a shelf). Equipment and fixtures (a kettle, a tap, a chair). Stored things (an open drawer, a cabinet interior, a box). Point of use (a door handle, a light switch, a remote). A faint room-by-room sweep walks in and lands only where the eye always lands, missing the same spots.]

[HOST, voice over]
A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks in and lands where it always lands. Sorting the world into four kinds of target drags the overlooked ones into the light. Surfaces: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors. Equipment and fixtures: the appliances, taps, furniture, and tools that both get dirty and can quietly fail. Stored things: whatever lives inside the cabinets, drawers, and boxes, cleaned and checked, not re-sorted. And point of use, the one people forget most: the handles, the switches, the remotes, the spots your hands hit a hundred times a day and almost never clean.

[HOST, on camera]
Run those four kinds through your home like a net and you will be surprised what surfaces. The point is not to be official. It is four different angles of approach, so the targets that hide from one angle get caught by another.

## SECTION 2: THE SHINE LIST

[HOST]
Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes.

[VISUAL: The list itself, drawn as a simple sheet pinned to the fridge. Four headings in slate, surfaces, equipment and fixtures, stored things, point of use, with two or three target lines under each, and a written name to the right of every line. One line near the bottom still has a blank name space with a light circle around it.]

[HOST, voice over]
Down the page, write the four kinds as headings. Then walk your home, category by category, and list the real targets under each. Not every object you own; the things that genuinely need regular cleaning and inspection.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list.]

[HOST, on camera]
So here is the definition, and it is worth keeping. A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands. And half its value is already done just by writing it. A target that lives only in your sense of unease gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list.

## SECTION 3: CLEAN THE WHOLE THING, NOT JUST THE PART THAT SHOWS

[HOST]
There is a reason the list beats just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in this chapter.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows.]

[VISUAL: Paired face-and-hidden-side reveals: a clean microwave front over a greasy underside vent, a gleaming fridge front over dust-furred coils, a gleaming sink over its unlooked-into overflow.]

[HOST, voice over]
The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean in a second and tells you nothing; the greasy vent on its underside is the part that will eventually cost you the microwave. The fridge looks fine; the coils behind it, furred with dust, are why it runs hot and dies early. Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails.

[HOST, on camera]
So keep this line. The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads. The list, built by kind, deliberately includes the tops and the backs and the undersides and the insides.

## SECTION 4: PUT A NAME ON EVERY TARGET

[HOST]
Here is where most cleaning plans quietly die, and where this one is built to survive.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target.]

[VISUAL: The finished list, then every unassigned line drifting toward one pair of tired hands like water finding the low point. Then a pen goes down the page writing a different name against each line, and the drift stops.]

[HOST, voice over]
A list of targets with no names is just a longer version of the guilt you started with. Think about why the load falls on one person. It is rarely a decision, it is a default. A target that is nobody's job becomes the job of whoever cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time. Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it.

[HOST, on camera]
So hear this. When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching. So assign every line. Not most lines. Every line, including the ones you would have silently kept for yourself.

## SECTION 5: CHOOSING, NOT ASSIGNING

[HOST]
A name on every line is the goal, but how the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year.

[VISUAL: The household around the list together, hands reaching in to claim targets before anything is handed out. A young child proudly puts a name on the light switches and low door handles; an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. A small rotation arrow sits beside the jobs nobody wants.]

[HOST, voice over]
A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So make the list together, and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned. Ownership you picked feels different from ownership you were saddled with, and it survives a bad week where an assignment does not. Match the targets to who can do them. And the jobs nobody wants, the bins, the bathroom, share them out or rotate them, so the worst of it moves around instead of always landing in the same place.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone.]

[HOST, on camera]
A Shine list assigned together stops being one person's endless private duty and becomes something the whole household holds, a few targets each, out in the open, fair. A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone, and it stops depending on a single person never getting tired.
