# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 17, Shine Targets and Assignments

Last chapter you cleaned one corner and let the cleaning show you what it had been hiding. It worked, and it was meant to be small: one zone, one cloth, proof that a wipe can do two jobs at once. But a home is not one corner, and if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet, exactly as before. So the obvious next thought is, fine, I will clean the rest of it too. And that thought runs straight into two walls. This chapter is about those two walls, and it takes them in order.

The first wall is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning. You know the surfaces you look at. You do not have a clear picture of the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the underside of the bath mat, the handle of every door in the house, all the targets that never announce themselves because you never see them. The second wall is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? Because right now, in most homes, the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most, carrying the whole invisible load alone. None of this is a moral failing. It is just what happens with no plan.

So we make what needs cleaning visible, all of it, and the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks into a room and lands where it always lands. Sorting the world into a few kinds of target drags the overlooked ones into the light. There are four kinds. Surfaces: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors. Equipment and fixtures: the appliances, the taps and sinks, the furniture, the tools, the working things that get dirty and can quietly fail. Stored things: whatever lives inside the cabinets, closets, drawers, and boxes, which need cleaning and checking too, even though a closed door hides them from view. And point of use, the one people forget most: the high-touch spots your hands hit a hundred times a day, the handles, the switches, the remotes, the pull on the launch pad drawer.

Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. Write the four kinds down the page as headings, then walk your home, category by category, and list the real targets under each. A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands. Half of its value is done just by writing it down. A target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up; a target that lives only in your sense of unease gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list.

There is a rule that makes the list worth building rather than just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in the chapter: clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows. The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean in a second and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that will eventually cost you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it are why it runs hot and dies early. Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails. The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads.

Then the harder half, the names, because this is where most cleaning plans quietly die and where this one is built to survive. A list of targets with no names attached is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with. It has to become a list with a name on every line, because a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Think about why the load falls on one person in the first place. It is rarely a decision; it is a default. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time. Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it. And how the names get there matters as much as the names themselves: a job you chose is a job you will actually do, a job you were handed is a job you will resent. So make the list together and let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, match the targets to who can actually do them, a child can genuinely own the low switches and door handles, an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils, and share out or rotate the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person.

Go and look at the friction meter, and this time notice that it does not move, and that this is correct. Last chapter the needle took its first Shine step, because you actually cleaned something and caught a problem; the room's condition changed. This chapter you made a list and put names to it, which is real and necessary work, but nothing new got cleaned today, so the needle holds exactly where the first Shine step left it, honest as ever. But look just ahead of the live needle and you will see something new: a dashed arrow, projecting forward toward the goal, drawn because you have earned the projection even if you have not yet earned the move. The plan is the loaded spring; the move comes once the targets get their cleaning and the whole home starts getting read on a rhythm.
