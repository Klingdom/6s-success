## 1. Chapter Opening: Two walls
Last chapter you cleaned one corner and let the cleaning show you what it had been hiding. It worked, and it was meant to be small. But a home is not one corner, and if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet. The obvious next thought, I will clean the rest of it too, runs into two walls. The first is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning; you know the surfaces you look at, not the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet. The second is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? In most homes the honest answer is one person, the same person. This chapter takes both walls in order: first make what needs cleaning visible, grouped so nothing hides, then put a name against every target. Not to police anyone; to be fair. We are not cleaning anything new today. We are making the list, and sharing it.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- See why cleaning the whole home needs a plan, not just more effort from one person
- Recognize the four kinds of target so nothing gets overlooked: surfaces, equipment, stored things, and point of use
- Build one Shine list for your home, grouped by kind so the invisible targets come into view
- Put a name against every target, so the work is shared instead of silently dumped
- Let people choose their targets, so the jobs actually get done and not resented

## 3. One Zone Clean, a Whole House Unnamed
The launch pad pass did something real, it proved that cleaning and inspecting are the same motion and caught two small problems, but it did not scale. You cannot run your whole home the way you ran that corner, on a whim, one surface at a time, because the things that most need Shine are precisely the things you never happen to think of. That is the trap of cleaning by instinct: it cleans the same visible handful over and over and never touches the rest. The kitchen counter gets wiped daily; the top of the cupboards gets wiped when you move house. None of this is laziness; it is what happens with no plan. Underneath the what sits the harder question, the who: in most homes the cleaning is not shared so much as absorbed, by one person, invisibly. This chapter fixes both with one artifact, a list, built a particular way and shared a particular way. Then the fence: we are planning the clean, not cleaning; not re-sorting or re-arranging; when the list touches stored things it means clean and check them, not decide again whether to keep them. Today has one job: name every target, and name who.

## 4. What Counts as a Target
Before you can list what needs cleaning, you need to be able to see it, and the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things because your eye lands where it always lands; sorting the world into a few kinds of target drags the overlooked ones into the light. There are four kinds. Surfaces: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors. Equipment and fixtures: the appliances, taps and sinks, furniture, tools, the working things that both get dirty and can quietly fail. Stored things: whatever lives inside the cabinets, closets, drawers, and boxes, which need cleaning and checking too. Point of use, the one people forget most: the high-touch spots your hands hit a hundred times a day, the handles, switches, remotes, the pull on the launch pad drawer. The four categories are not meant to be official; they give your attention four different angles of approach.
- Callout (6S Tip): Group targets by kind, surfaces, equipment, stored things, point of use, not by room. Grouping by kind catches the things a room-by-room sweep always skips, like the tops of the cupboards and the handles in every doorway.
- Infographic (the four kinds of target): four calm labeled groups in a simple grid, each with two or three warm home examples. Surfaces (a floor, a window, a shelf). Equipment and fixtures (a kettle, a tap, a chair). Stored things (an open drawer, a cabinet interior, a box). Point of use (a door handle, a light switch, a TV remote).

## 5. The Shine List
Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. Down the page, write the four kinds as headings, then walk your home category by category and list the real targets under each. Not every object you own; the things that genuinely need regular cleaning and inspection. The first half of the value is already done just by writing it: a target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up, while a target that lives only in your sense of unease gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. The list turns the invisible pile of obligation into a finite, shareable set of lines. Do not aim for a perfect or complete list on the first pass, and do not let the size of it alarm you; a first Shine list is usually longer than people expect, and that is just the first time all of this has been written in one place instead of carried in one head.
- Definition box (frozen): A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands.
- Illustration (the Shine list): the list itself, a simple sheet with a pinned-to-the-fridge feel. ## 6. Clean the Whole Thing, Not Just the Part That Shows
There is a reason the list is worth making rather than just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in this chapter. The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that costs you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it are why it runs hot and dies early. Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails. This is exactly why the list beats instinct: built by kind, it deliberately includes the tops, backs, undersides, and insides. You are not committing to scrubbing all of it today; you are making sure that when the cleaning does happen, it reaches the parts that were quietly deciding how long your things last.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the targets rule): Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the invisible-target payoff): The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads.
- Callout (Quick Win): Walk one room and write down five things that need regular cleaning but that nobody has ever been assigned, the top of something, the inside of something, a handle everyone touches. You just started your Shine list.

## 7. Put a Name on Every Target
Here is where most cleaning plans quietly die, and where this one is built to survive. A list of targets with no names attached is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with. It has to become a list of targets with a name on every line, and the name is the whole difference between a plan that happens and a plan that does not. Think about why the load falls on one person: it is rarely a decision, it is a default. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time. Nobody chose this; the absence of names chose it. The fix is not to try harder or nag more; it is to end the default by writing a name against each line. Go down your list and assign every line, including the ones you would have silently kept for yourself, because a target with no name has just been quietly assigned back to the usual person.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the assignments rule): A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the assignments reframe): When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching.

## 8. Choosing, Not Assigning
A name on every line is the goal, but how the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year. There is a right way and a wrong way to hand out the work, and the difference is whether people choose or get chosen for. Make the list together and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned; ownership you picked survives a bad week where an assignment does not. Match the targets to who can actually do them: a young child can genuinely own the light switches and the low door handles, and will do it proudly if it is theirs; an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. And the jobs nobody wants, the bins, the bathroom, do not leave those to default back to one person; share them out or rotate them. This is the same shift you made with the room map, when your room became their room. A Shine list assigned together stops being one person's endless private duty and becomes something the whole household holds. A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone.
- Pull-quote (frozen, choosing not assigning): A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Make the list together and let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers. A job you chose is a job you will actually do; a job you were handed is a job you will resent.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Quietly assigning the whole list to yourself and calling it easier. It is easier today, and it is exactly why Shine collapses by next month. A list with a name on every line is not more work; it is the same work, finally shared.

## 10. From a List to a Habit
You did two things today that instinct never does on its own: you made the whole of the cleaning visible, grouped so the hidden targets could not hide, and you shared it out with a name on every line. That is the what and the who of Shine, settled for the whole home. The launch pad is no longer a lucky exception; it is the first entry on a list that now covers everything, owned by everyone. What you have not done is the how, and that is deliberate, because knowing what to clean and who cleans it does not yet tell anyone how to clean it well. The next chapter is about making each target quick and genuinely easy to keep, the methods that actually work, so the list is something people can do rather than something they feel bad about. For now, the plan is real and it is shared, which is further than most homes ever get.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): List every target that needs cleaning, group it by kind so nothing hides, and put a name against each one. A Shine you have written down and shared is a Shine that actually happens, and it happens to the whole home, not just the parts that show and not just to the one person who always ends up doing it.
