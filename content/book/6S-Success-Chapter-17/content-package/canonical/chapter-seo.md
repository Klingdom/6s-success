## Search intent
Turning a single first cleaning pass into a whole-home plan and sharing the labor. Readers arriving here have already reframed cleaning as inspection (Chapter 16) and cleaned one zone, but the rest of the house is still unread and, in most homes, the work quietly falls to one person. They want two things: a way to know everything that actually needs cleaning, and a fair way to divide it. The chapter answers with one artifact and two rules: the Shine list, grouped by kind of target so nothing hides, with a name on every line; clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows; and a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, put a name on every target. It teaches the four target categories (surfaces, equipment and fixtures, stored things, point of use), shows why the surface that faces away is the one that fails, and lets people choose their targets rather than get handed them. Because it plans but cleans nothing new, the friction meter holds.

## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Groups cleaning targets by kind (surfaces, equipment and fixtures, stored things, point of use) instead of the usual room-by-room checklist that keeps missing the same spots
- Names the invisible targets directly, the tops, backs, undersides, insides, and handles, rather than only the visible surfaces
- Treats the labor question head on, put a name on every target, instead of assuming one person does it all
- Frames sharing as fairness and relief, not a chore chart imposed from above, with choosing over assigning as the mechanism that makes it last
- Keeps scope tight: this decides what to clean and who does it; it does not teach how to clean or products (Chapter 18), build a repeatable routine or schedule (Chapter 19), or build documented standards (Standardize and Sustain)
- Holds the friction meter honestly, planning is real work but not cleaning, instead of claiming progress that has not happened yet
