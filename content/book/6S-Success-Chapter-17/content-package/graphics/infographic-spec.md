# Infographic Spec: The Shine List

**Chapter 17, "Shine Targets and Assignments"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Five · Shine**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: one zone is
clean, but the rest of the home is still unnamed, and Shine that belongs to no one lands on the same
tired pair of hands, so this chapter lists every target worth cleaning, groups it by four kinds so
nothing hides, and puts a name against each one, so the whole home gets read and the work gets shared.
The concept pairs the hero device, **the Shine list**, with its two frozen rules: **Clean the whole
thing, not just the part that shows.** for the targets, and **A job that belongs to everyone belongs to
no one. Put a name on every target.** for the assignments. So the viewer sees both halves of the device
at once, the grouping that drags the hidden targets into view, and the names that keep the whole list
from landing on one person.

It stays consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_17_final.html`. Slate carries the
rules, the definition, the headings, and the tags; wood fill warms the objects; a single small warm red
is held back for the one warm-red magnet that pins the sheet and is used nowhere else on this calm
image; soft green marks the one blank line still waiting for a name. "Spotless" does not appear; the
target is a home that is read and shared, not a spotless one. Anti-shame throughout: a home where the
cleaning fell to one person is the common case, not a fault, and the fix is a shared list, not trying
harder.

## Layout notes

- The two rules are the loudest words on the page. Draw them as the plan the chapter makes: the targets
  rule, clean the whole thing not just the part that shows, tells you what to reach; the assignments
  rule, a job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, tells you who reaches it. The eye should read
  the two rules, then see the Shine list model both at once.
- The Shine list is the hero, so make both halves of the device unmistakable: the four kinds grouped
  down the page, and a name against every single line. The names must look hand-written and must come
  from more than one hand, at least one plainly a child's, because the whole argument is that the work
  is shared, not dumped on one person. Keep the one blank line with its light green circle: it is the
  last target still waiting to be claimed, the live edge of a plan being made, quietly hopeful, not a
  gap or a failure.
- Hold the color discipline: the single small warm red is the magnet pinning the sheet, held back for
  that one job and used nowhere else; slate carries the rules, the definition, and the headings; soft
  green marks only the one waiting line. This image is calm, so no fill-line red anywhere except the
  one small magnet.
- This image makes the plan, the list and the names. Do NOT re-argue why we clean (Chapter 16, done).
  Do NOT re-sort or re-arrange anything (Chapters 12 to 15); the list cleans and checks stored things,
  it does not re-decide whether to keep them. Do NOT teach a cleaning method, product, or tool (Chapter
  18). Do NOT build a routine or schedule (Chapter 19). Do NOT build documented standards or
  maintenance systems (the later Standardize and Sustain S's). And show nothing being cleaned: this
  chapter plans, it does not clean.
- Anti-shame: a home where the cleaning silently fell to one person is the common case, not a moral
  failing, and the fix is a list and shared names, not trying harder or nagging more. This touches a
  live nerve, who does the housework, so keep the register fairness and relief, no guilt cues, no
  pressure framing, no force framing anywhere. "Spotless" does not appear; the target is a home that is
  read and shared, not a spotless one.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the sheet drawn as a
  real household's page pinned a little askew rather than a clean corporate form, the names set as if
  jotted by different hands rather than typeset.
