**The recurring motif.** The hero device of the chapter is the Shine list: targets grouped by kind, with a name on every line. Two images carry the whole set. The first is the blank name-tag: a whole-home cutaway where one launch pad glows bright and read, and the rest of the house sits in faint ghost #8A8073 outline, quietly crowded with small unnamed targets, each wearing a tiny empty name-tag. Everything present, nothing claimed. The second is the same list once the tags are filled: one page, four slate #3C5A6B headings, two or three target lines under each, and against every line a written name, some in an adult's hand, some in a child's, so the whole sheet reads as shared. The arc of the chapter is the move from the first image to the second: the invisible targets are dragged into view by kind, and the blank tags fill with names, so the work is both seen and shared. A second, supporting device runs through the middle: the targets rule made literal, an object shown twice, its clean visible face telling you nothing and its hidden face (a vent, a coil, an overflow) marked with a friction spark #CB4B36, the part that faces away and fails. Green marks a target that is clean and read or a line that now has an owner; friction spark #CB4B36 is reserved for the surface that faces away and the friction side of the meter; slate carries the chapter labels, the four category headings, the GOAL crosshair, and the milestone ticks; terracotta stays the launch pad zone and the chapter kicker; ghost #8A8073 is the unnamed targets and the earlier ghost-needle steps.

**Two fences to hold visually.** First, this is planning, not cleaning: the reader builds one list and shares it, and nothing new is cleaned this chapter. The room's condition does not change, so nothing here should read as a fresh clean or a caught problem beyond Chapter 16's two (the launch pad's rust ring and loose hook, already caught). The visuals show targets being named and claimed, not scrubbed. Second, the list is a target inventory plus owners, not a re-sort, a method, a routine, a schedule, or a written standard. The list cleans and checks stored things; it does not re-sort or re-arrange them (that is done, Sort and Straighten). Do not draw cleaning technique, products, or tools (that is Chapter 18), and do not draw a repeatable routine, schedule, checklist, or documented standard (that is Chapter 19 and the later Standardize and Sustain S's, teaser only). Keep the focus on seeing targets by kind and putting a name on every one. The friction meter HOLDS with a dashed forward projection; it must read as a principled planning hold, not a stall and not a move.

## Beat 2 · Two walls, and they come in order

The gap made plain: even the obvious next thought, I will clean the rest of it too, runs into two walls, first the what, then the who.

- Composition: two faint ghost #8A8073 walls set across the page on paper ground. On the first wall, a scatter of unnamed targets in ghost outline: the coils behind a fridge, a cabinet interior, the handle of a door, the underside of a bath mat. On the second wall, a single figure carrying an armful of blank name-tags, the whole invisible load resting on one pair of hands.
- Palette: paper ground, ghost #8A8073 on the two walls and the unnamed targets, ink #2B2622 outlines. No verdict colours; nobody is failing here.
- Slate #3C5A6B labels below the two walls: "the what" and "the who."
- Point: the first wall is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning, because the targets that most need Shine are the ones you never see: the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the handle of every door. The second wall is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who does it? In most homes the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most. This chapter takes both in order, first the list, then the names, and it cleans nothing new today; it makes the list and shares it.

- Palette: slate #3C5A6B on the tags and footnote, ghost #8A8073 on the untouched tops and backs, friction spark #CB4B36 only on the quiet targets that fail. Paper ground, ink outlines.
- A slate #3C5A6B footnote: "the loud targets get all of it; the quiet ones get none, until a quiet one fails."
- Point: instinct cleans what is in front of you and what is bothering you today, the same visible handful over and over, and never once touches the rest. The kitchen counter gets wiped daily; the tops of the cupboards get wiped when you move house. None of this is laziness; it is what happens with no plan. And underneath the what sits the harder who: in most homes the cleaning is absorbed by one person, invisibly. Name the fence in the frame: planning, not cleaning; not re-sorting or re-arranging; when the list touches stored things it means clean and check them, not decide again whether to keep them. Today's one job: name every target, and name who.

## Beat 4 · See targets by kind, not by room

The secondary device: teach the reader to see cleaning targets by kind, so the overlooked ones come into view. This is the four-category reference block.

- Composition: a calm reference block on paper ground, four labeled groups arranged in a simple grid, each headed in slate #3C5A6B, each with two or three warm hand-drawn home examples in ink #2B2622 line-and-flat-color. Surfaces (a floor, a window, a shelf). Equipment and fixtures (a kettle, a tap, a chair). Stored things (an open drawer, a cabinet interior, a box). Point of use (a door handle, a light switch, a TV remote). The point-of-use group is nudged forward or lightly marked as the one people forget most.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B category headings, ink #2B2622 line work on the examples, warm flat colour fills. Calm and legible at a glance, no verdict colours.
- Caption, from the book: "Four kinds of target, four angles of approach. A room-by-room sweep keeps missing the same spots; sorting by kind, especially the stored things behind closed doors and the handles everyone touches, is what pulls them into the open."
- Point: the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind, because a room-by-room sweep always lands where your eye always lands. The four kinds, home-scaled from the Traditional Shine Target Categories, give attention four different angles of approach so the targets that hide from one angle get caught by another. The 6S Tip sits beneath in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Group targets by kind, surfaces, equipment, stored things, point of use, not by room. Grouping by kind catches the things a room-by-room sweep always skips, like the tops of the cupboards and the handles in every doorway."

## Beat 7 · Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows

The targets rule made literal by the object shown twice, its clean face telling you nothing and its hidden face the part that fails. This is the supporting device of the set.

- Composition: a single object drawn twice on paper ground: a microwave, its clean front face lit and marked with a soft #6A625A "?", telling you nothing; and the same microwave turned to show the grease-thick vent on its underside, ringed in friction spark #CB4B36, the part that fails. Beside it a fridge with its dusty back coils in the same spark, and a hint of a sink overflow.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, soft #6A625A on the clean-but-uninformative face, friction spark #CB4B36 on the hidden face that fails. A slate #3C5A6B caption.
- A slate #3C5A6B caption: "the surface that faces away is the one that fails."
- Point: the dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside is the part that costs you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it are why it runs hot and dies early. This is exactly why the list, built by kind, beats instinct: it deliberately includes the tops, backs, undersides, and insides. You are not committing to scrubbing all of it today; you are making sure that when the cleaning does happen, it reaches the parts that were quietly deciding how long your things last.
- The frozen targets rule sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows." The frozen invisible-target payoff runs beneath it, also in Fraunces: "The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads." The Quick Win sits in a green #6E8B5B bordered callout, verbatim: "Walk one room and write down five things that need regular cleaning but that nobody has ever been assigned, the top of something, the inside of something, a handle everyone touches. You just started your Shine list."

## Beat 8 · Put a name on every target

The assignments rule made literal: the blank name-tags on the list fill in with written owners, one still waiting.

- Composition: the Shine list with the blank name-tags being filled in on paper ground: a hand writing a name against each line, the tags turning from empty ghost #8A8073 to a written owner, a green calm-dot #6E8B5B beside each claimed line. One line near the bottom still carries a blank name space with a light circle around it, the last target waiting to be claimed.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines and written names, ghost #8A8073 on the tags not yet filled, green #6E8B5B beside each claimed line. Warm, no verdict colours.
- Point: a list of targets with no names is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with; the name is the whole difference between a plan that happens and a plan that does not. The load falls on one person by default, not by decision: an unassigned target flows to whoever notices first and cannot let it sit, the same person every time. Nobody chose this; the absence of names chose it. So assign every line, including the ones you would have silently kept for yourself, because a target with no name has just been quietly assigned back to the usual person.
- The frozen assignments rule sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target." The frozen assignments reframe runs beneath it, also in Fraunces: "When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching."

## Beat 9 · Choosing, not assigning

How the names get on the list decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year: let people choose, match to ability, share or rotate the worst jobs.

- Composition: a household gathered around one list on paper ground, hands reaching in to claim their own lines: a child's name written against the low light switches and door handles, an adult's against the oven and the fridge coils. On the jobs nobody wants (the bins, the bathroom) a small honey #DDA63A rotation-arrow shows them moving around rather than landing on one person.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, green #6E8B5B on the claimed lines, honey #DDA63A on the rotate-the-worst-jobs arrow, slate #3C5A6B on the card rules. Warm and shared.
- Point: a job you chose is a job you will actually do; a job you were handed is a job you will resent. Make the list together and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned; ownership you picked survives a bad week where an assignment does not. Match targets to who can actually do them; a young child can genuinely own the low switches and handles and will do it proudly if it is theirs, an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. The jobs nobody wants do not default back to one person; share them out or rotate them. This is the same shift as Chapter 15's room map, your room became their room: a Shine list assigned together stops being one person's endless private duty and becomes something the whole household holds. A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone.
- The frozen choosing line sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person." Beneath, the Family Challenge in a honey #DDA63A bordered callout, verbatim: "Make the list together and let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers. A job you chose is a job you will actually do; a job you were handed is a job you will resent." And the Common Mistake in a friction spark #CB4B36 bordered callout, verbatim: "Quietly assigning the whole list to yourself and calling it easier. It is easier today, and it is exactly why Shine collapses by next month. A list with a name on every line is not more work; it is the same work, finally shared."
