# Back Cover Copy: Shine Targets and Assignments

## Back-cover text

Last chapter you cleaned one corner and let the cleaning show you what it had been hiding. It worked, and it was meant to be small. "But a home is not one corner, and if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet, exactly as before." So the obvious next thought is, fine, I will clean the rest of it too, and that thought runs into two walls almost at once. The first is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning: the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the handle of every door, all the targets that never announce themselves because you never see them. The second is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? In most homes the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most.

Chapter 17 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the second chapter of Part Five, and Shine continues. It does not re-sort or re-arrange a single thing, and it cleans nothing new; it turns one first pass into a plan for the whole home. It takes both walls in order. First it teaches you to see targets by kind rather than by room, because a room-by-room sweep always misses the same spots. There are four kinds: surfaces, equipment and fixtures, stored things, and point of use, the high-touch handles and switches people forget most. Then you build the thing the chapter is named for, on a sheet of paper in about twenty minutes. "A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands." Half its value is done just by writing it down, because "you cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list."

The chapter carries two rules. The first is for the targets: "Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows." The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. "The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads." The second rule is for the names, and it is where most cleaning plans quietly die: "A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target." The load falls on one person not by decision but by default. "Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it." A name against every target is not about control. "It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching."

How the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year. "A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent." So make the list together, let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out or rotate the jobs nobody wants. Because the chapter plans but cleans nothing new, the friction meter holds exactly where the first Shine step left it, honest as ever, with a dashed arrow projecting forward toward the goal. "The plan is the loaded spring. The move is coming; it is simply honest to say it has not happened yet," once the targets get their cleaning in Chapter 18 and the whole home gets read on a rhythm in Chapter 19. Walk one room and write down five things that need regular cleaning but that nobody has ever been assigned, the top of something, the inside of something, a handle everyone touches. You just started your Shine list. "You know what needs reading, and you know whose job it is to read it."
