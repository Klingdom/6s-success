# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

Last chapter you cleaned one corner and let the cleaning show you what it had been hiding. It worked, and it was meant to be small: one zone, one cloth, proof that a wipe can do two jobs. "But a home is not one corner, and if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet, exactly as before." Chapter 17 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where the single first pass becomes a plan for the whole home.

"Shine Targets and Assignments" is the second chapter of Part Five, and Shine continues, the third of the six S's. It keeps the same house and cleans nothing new; it changes one thing, the reach. The obvious next thought, I will clean the rest of it too, runs into two walls almost at once. The first is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning. You know the surfaces you look at. You do not have a clear picture of the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the underside of the bath mat, the handle of every door in the house, all the targets that never announce themselves because you never see them. The second wall is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? Because right now, in most homes, the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most, carrying the whole invisible load alone.

This chapter takes both walls in order, and it fixes both with one artifact that is almost comically simple: a list. But a list built a particular way, and shared a particular way, and that is the whole chapter. It names its fence plainly. It does not re-argue why we clean, which was Chapter 16; it does not teach how to clean, products, or tools, which is Chapter 18; it does not build the repeatable routine or reset, which is Chapter 19; and it does not build documented standards or schedules, which belong to Standardize and Sustain. It does not re-sort or re-arrange either. When the list touches the things stored in your cabinets, it means clean and check them, not decide again whether to keep them, which you did back in Sort. Today has one job: name every target, and name who.

Before you can list what needs cleaning, you need to be able to see it, and the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks into a room and lands where it always lands. There are four kinds of target, and between them they catch almost everything. Surfaces: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors. Equipment and fixtures: the appliances, taps and sinks, furniture, tools, the working things that both get dirty and can quietly fail. Stored things: whatever lives inside the cabinets, closets, drawers, and boxes, which need cleaning and checking too, even though a closed door hides them from view. And point of use, the one people forget most: the high-touch spots your hands hit a hundred times a day, the handles, the switches, the remotes, the pull on the launch pad drawer, the places that get the most human contact and almost none of the cleaning.

Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. Down the page, write the four kinds as headings, then walk your home category by category and list the real targets under each, not every object you own, the things that genuinely need regular cleaning and inspection. "A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands." The first half of its value is already done just by writing it. A target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up; a target that lives only in your sense of unease gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. "You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list." And do not aim for a perfect list on the first pass. A first Shine list is usually longer than people expect, and that is not a verdict on how dirty your home is; it is just the first time all of this has ever been written in one place instead of carried in one head.

There is a reason the list is worth making rather than just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in this chapter: "Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows." The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean in a second and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that will eventually cost you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it, furred with dust, are why it runs hot and dies early. "Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails." That is why the list, built by kind, beats instinct: it deliberately includes the tops and the backs and the undersides and the insides. "The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads."

Then the harder half, and the place where most cleaning plans quietly die. A list of targets with no names attached is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with. "A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target." Think about why the load falls on one person in the first place. It is rarely a decision. It is a default. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time, so every unassigned target flows steadily toward them like water finding the low point. "Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it." The fix is not for that person to try harder or to nag more; it is to end the default by writing a name against each line. "When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching."

How the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year. "A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person." Make the list together and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned; ownership you picked survives a bad week where an assignment does not. Match the targets to who can actually do them: a young child can genuinely own the light switches and the low door handles, and will do it proudly if it is theirs; an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. And the jobs nobody wants, the bins, the bathroom, do not leave those to default back to one person; share them out or rotate them. This is the same shift you made in the last part of the book, when the room map turned your room into their room. "A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone, and it stops depending on a single person never getting tired."

Because the chapter plans but cleans nothing new, the friction meter holds exactly where the first Shine step left it. Last chapter the needle took its first Shine step, because you actually cleaned something and caught a problem; the room's condition changed. This chapter you made a list and put names to it, which is real and necessary work, but nothing new got cleaned today, so the needle holds, honoring the book's rule that the meter moves only when the room itself changes. But look just ahead of the live needle and you will see something new: a dashed arrow, projecting forward toward the goal, drawn now because you have earned the projection even if you have not yet earned the move. "The plan is the loaded spring. The move is coming; it is simply honest to say it has not happened yet," once the targets get their cleaning in the next chapter and the whole home starts getting read on a rhythm in the one after. The goal crosshair, out on the calm side, is still ahead, with three chapters still to run in Shine and Safety, Standardize, and Sustain beyond.
