## Chapter Opening

Last chapter you cleaned one corner and let the cleaning show you what it had been hiding. It worked, and it was meant to be small: one zone, one cloth, proof that a wipe can do two jobs. But a home is not one corner, and if Shine stops at the launch pad, the rest of the house goes on wearing out in the quiet, exactly as before. So the obvious next thought is, fine, I will clean the rest of it too. And that thought runs into two walls almost at once.

The first wall is that you do not actually know everything that needs cleaning. You know the surfaces you look at. You do not have a clear picture of the top of the wardrobe, the coils behind the fridge, the inside of the medicine cabinet, the underside of the bath mat, the handle of every door in the house, all the targets that never announce themselves because you never see them. The second wall is quieter and heavier: even if you listed all of it, who is going to do it? Because right now, in most homes, the honest answer is one person, the same person, the one who notices first and minds most, carrying the whole invisible load alone.

This chapter is about those two walls, and it takes them in order. First we make what needs cleaning visible, all of it, grouped so nothing hides. Then we do the thing that actually makes Shine survive contact with a real household: we put a name against every target. Not to police anyone. To be fair, and to make sure the home stays cared for whether or not one particular person is watching. We are not cleaning anything new today. We are making the list, and sharing it.

## One Zone Clean, a Whole House Unnamed

Let me be honest about what the launch pad pass did and did not do, because it matters for what comes next. It did something real: it proved that cleaning and inspecting are the same motion, and it caught two small problems on one small zone. What it did not do is scale. You cannot run your whole home the way you ran that corner, on a whim, one surface at a time, whenever you happen to think of it, because the things that most need Shine are precisely the things you never happen to think of.

That is the trap of cleaning by instinct. Instinct cleans what is in front of you and what is bothering you today, which means it cleans the same visible handful over and over and never once touches the rest. The kitchen counter gets wiped daily; the top of the kitchen cupboards gets wiped when you move house. The bathroom sink is cleaned constantly; the extractor fan clogged with dust has not been looked at in years. None of this is laziness. It is just what happens with no plan: the loud targets get all the attention and the quiet ones get none, until a quiet one fails.

And underneath the what sits the harder question, the who. In most homes the cleaning is not shared so much as absorbed, by one person, invisibly, until it feels like their personality rather than a pile of unassigned jobs. Everyone else lives in the home as a guest of that person's effort, mostly without meaning to, because no one ever wrote down the work and split it. This chapter fixes both problems with one artifact, and the artifact is almost comically simple: a list. But a list built a particular way, and shared a particular way, and that is the whole chapter.

So, the usual fence before we start. We are not cleaning today, not really; we are planning the clean. We are not re-sorting or re-arranging anything, and when the list touches the things stored in your cabinets, it means clean and check them, not decide again whether to keep them, which you did back in Sort. We are not yet talking about how to clean each target well, which is the next chapter, or how to keep the whole thing going week after week, which is the chapter after that. Today has one job: name every target, and name who.

## What Counts as a Target

Before you can list what needs cleaning, you need to be able to see it, and the useful trick, straight from the original method, is to stop thinking room by room and start thinking kind by kind. A room-by-room sweep always misses the same things, because your eye walks into a room and lands where it always lands. Sorting the world into a few kinds of target drags the overlooked ones into the light. There are four kinds, and between them they catch almost everything.

The first kind is **surfaces**: the floors, walls, windows, counters, shelves, and doors, the flat faces of the home that show dirt and take the most traffic. The second is **equipment and fixtures**: the appliances, the taps and sinks, the furniture, the tools, the working things that both get dirty and can quietly fail. The third is **stored things**: whatever lives inside the cabinets, closets, drawers, and boxes, which need cleaning and checking too, even though a closed door hides them from view. The fourth, and the one people forget most, is **point of use**: the high-touch spots your hands hit a hundred times a day, the handles, the switches, the remotes, the pull on the launch pad drawer, the places that get the most human contact and almost none of the cleaning.

> **6S Tip**
> Group targets by kind, surfaces, equipment, stored things, point of use, not by room. Grouping by kind catches the things a room-by-room sweep always skips, like the tops of the cupboards and the handles in every doorway.

Run those four kinds through your home like a net and you will be surprised what surfaces, in both senses. The point of the four categories is not to be exhaustive or official. It is to give your attention four different angles of approach, so the targets that hide from one angle get caught by another.

## The Shine List

Now you make the thing this chapter is named for, and it takes a sheet of paper and about twenty minutes. Down the page, write the four kinds as headings: surfaces, equipment and fixtures, stored things, point of use. Then walk your home, category by category, and list the real targets under each. Not every object you own; the things that genuinely need regular cleaning and inspection. Under surfaces, the kitchen floor, the bathroom mirror, the tops of the cupboards. Under equipment, the oven, the washing machine, the bathroom fan. Under stored things, the pantry shelves, the medicine cabinet, the junk drawer. Under point of use, the door handles, the light switches, the remote, the fridge door.

> A Shine list is one shared inventory of everything in a space that needs regular cleaning and inspection, grouped by kind of target, with a person's name against each one, so nothing is forgotten and no job quietly lands on a single pair of hands.

That is the whole device, and the first half of its value is already done just by writing it. A target on a list is a target someone can see, question, and pick up. A target that lives only in your sense of unease is a target that gets cleaned by you, resentfully, or not at all. The list takes the invisible pile of "things I feel I ought to get to" and turns it into a finite, shareable set of lines. You cannot share a feeling of obligation. You can share a list.

Do not aim for a perfect or complete list on the first pass, and do not let the size of it alarm you. A first Shine list is usually longer than people expect, and that is not a verdict on how dirty your home is; it is just the first time all of this has ever been written in one place instead of carried in one head. You can always add a target you missed. The point today is to get most of it out of your head and onto the page, where the second half of the work, the names, can begin.

## Clean the Whole Thing, Not Just the Part That Shows

There is a reason the list is worth making rather than just cleaning what looks dirty, and it is the single most useful idea in this chapter.

> Clean the whole thing, not just the part that shows.

The dirt that shows is almost never the dirt that matters. The front of the microwave wipes clean in a second and tells you nothing; the vent on its underside, thick with grease, is the part that will eventually cost you the microwave. The visible half of the fridge looks fine; the coils behind it, furred with dust, are why it runs hot and dies early. The bathroom sink gleams; the overflow hole you never look into is where the smell is coming from. Over and over, the surface that faces the room is the one you clean, and the surface that faces away is the one that fails.

> The dirt that matters is rarely the dirt you can see. It is on the top of the cupboard, behind the machine, under the mat, inside the drawer, and on the handle every hand in the house touches. A target you never named is a surface no one ever reads.

This is exactly why the list beats instinct. Instinct is drawn to the visible face; the list, built by kind, deliberately includes the tops and the backs and the undersides and the insides, the targets your eye slides past precisely because they do not show. You are not committing to scrubbing all of it today. You are making sure that when the cleaning does happen, it reaches the parts that were quietly deciding how long your things last.

> **Quick Win**
> Walk one room and write down five things that need regular cleaning but that nobody has ever been assigned, the top of something, the inside of something, a handle everyone touches. You just started your Shine list.

## Put a Name on Every Target

Here is where most cleaning plans quietly die, and where this one is built to survive. A list of targets with no names attached is just a longer, more detailed version of the guilt you started with. It has to become a list of targets with a name on every line, and the name is not a detail. It is the whole difference between a plan that happens and a plan that does not.

> A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Put a name on every target.

Think about why the load falls on one person in the first place. It is rarely a decision. It is a default. A target that is nobody's job in particular becomes the job of whoever notices it and cannot let it sit, and that same person notices first every time, so every unassigned target flows steadily toward them like water finding the low point. Nobody chose this. The absence of names chose it. And the fix is not for that person to try harder or to nag more; it is to end the default by writing a name against each line, so the target has an owner before anyone's patience runs out.

> When cleaning belongs to no one in particular, it belongs to whoever cannot stand it any longer, which is the same person every time. A name against every target is not about control. It is about fairness, and about a home that stays cared for whether or not one person is watching.

Go down your list and assign every line. Not most lines. Every line, including the ones you would have silently kept for yourself. A target with no name is a target that has just been quietly assigned back to the usual person, and the point of this whole chapter was to stop doing that. When each line has an owner, two things change at once: the work is genuinely shared, and each target finally has someone whose job it is to read it, so the whole home gets inspected, not just the launch pad.

## Choosing, Not Assigning

A name on every line is the goal, but how the names get there decides whether the plan lasts a week or a year. There is a right way and a wrong way to hand out the work, and the difference is whether people choose or get chosen for.

> A job you chose is a job you will actually do. A job you were handed is a job you will resent. So let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers, and share out the jobs nobody wants instead of leaving them all with one person.

So make the list together, and let people claim targets before anything gets assigned. Ownership you picked feels different from ownership you were saddled with, and it survives a bad week where an assignment does not. Match the targets to who can actually do them: a young child can genuinely own the light switches and the low door handles, and will do it proudly if it is theirs; an adult takes the oven and the fridge coils. And the jobs nobody wants, the bins, the bathroom, the thing everyone wrinkles their nose at, do not leave those to default back to one person either. Share them out, or rotate them, so the worst of it moves around instead of always landing in the same place.

> **Family Challenge**
> Make the list together and let people pick their targets before anyone gets handed the leftovers. A job you chose is a job you will actually do; a job you were handed is a job you will resent.

This is the same shift you made in the last part of the book, when the room map turned your room into their room. A Shine list assigned together does the same thing to the cleaning: it stops being one person's endless private duty and becomes something the whole household holds, a few targets each, out in the open, fair. A home kept by everyone asks less of anyone, and it stops depending on a single person never getting tired.

> **Common Mistake**
> Quietly assigning the whole list to yourself and calling it easier. It is easier today, and it is exactly why Shine collapses by next month. A list with a name on every line is not more work; it is the same work, finally shared.

## From a List to a Habit

You did two things today that instinct never does on its own: you made the whole of the cleaning visible, grouped so the hidden targets could not hide, and you shared it out with a name on every line. That is the what and the who of Shine, settled for the whole home, not just one corner of it. The launch pad is no longer a lucky exception; it is the first entry on a list that now covers everything, owned by everyone.

What you have not done is the how, and that is deliberate, because knowing what to clean and who cleans it does not yet tell anyone how to clean it well. A target owned but dreaded still does not get done; a method that takes an hour and a cupboard of products is a method people quietly abandon. So the next chapter is about making each target on your list quick and genuinely easy to keep, the methods that actually work, so the list is something people can do rather than something they feel bad about. And the chapter after that turns the whole thing into a light rhythm that keeps itself going.

For now, the plan is real and it is shared, which is further than most homes ever get. You know what needs reading, and you know whose job it is to read it.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> List every target that needs cleaning, group it by kind so nothing hides, and put a name against each one. A Shine you have written down and shared is a Shine that actually happens, and it happens to the whole home, not just the parts that show and not just to the one person who always ends up doing it.
