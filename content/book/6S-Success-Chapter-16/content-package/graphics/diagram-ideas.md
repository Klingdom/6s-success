# Diagram Ideas: Chapter 16

**"Shine: Cleaning as Inspection"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Five · Shine*

Five diagrams that carry the chapter's logic. All use the warm book palette and the two chapter
fonts (Fraunces display, Newsreader body, a clean sans for labels). These are working specs, not
finished art. They stay consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_16_final.html`: the
arranged launch pad seen through a faint haze with two or three hidden red problem-sparks, a hand
wiping a surface where the clean stripe reveals the small red spark the grime had hidden, a calm
two-column reference that teaches reading a surface, and the friction meter taking its first Shine
step off the marked Straighten-complete milestone toward the goal, the first step of the third S.

Palette tokens (from the chapter HTML :root): paper `#F7F2E9`, panel `#FBF7EF`, ink `#2B2622`,
soft `#6A625A`, terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate `#3C5A6B`, calm green `#6E8B5B`,
friction spark `#CB4B36`, rule `#E2D8C4` / `#D9CDB8`, wood fill `#E7C58B`.

Scope fence: this chapter is the first chapter of Part Five and it OPENS Shine, the third S. Chapters
12 to 15 already finished Straighten: the room is cleared (Sort) and fully arranged (Straighten), and
that work is done and stays. Chapter 16 does one narrow thing: it reframes cleaning as INSPECTION,
clean so you can SEE, so the act of cleaning doubles as the act of inspecting and small problems show
themselves early, while they are still small; then it makes one worked first pass on the launch pad
and moves the needle its first Shine step. It does NOT re-sort or re-arrange anything (Chapters 12 to
15, done and closed), it does NOT build the list of what to clean across the home or divide the labor
of who does what (Chapter 17), it does NOT teach cleaning methods, products, or tools (Chapter 18), it
does NOT build a repeatable routine or schedule to keep it up (Chapter 19), and it does NOT build
standards or maintenance-over-time systems (the later Standardize and Sustain S's). Separator
discipline: labels use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 16 · SHINE`, never a floating hyphen or a
floating dot between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: cleaning-as-inspection,
self-explaining, launch-pad label glyphs, mail-and-admin, whole-home, whole-room, at-a-glance,
Sort-complete, Straighten-complete, two-column, like-new, follow-the-eyes. "Spotless" appears here
only as a negated non-goal, the target the chapter argues against, never as a thing to reach for.
Anti-shame throughout: a home that has gone unread is a completely normal home, not a dirty one, and
not cleaning on purpose is not a character flaw; the small problems under the daily blur are
information the room has been trying to give you, not a verdict on anyone. Keep it calm and
non-coercive, no guilt cues, no pressure framing, and no force or violence framing anywhere; the whole
move is a quiet shift in what cleaning is for, so nothing in the scene is fought or driven out.

## 1. Arranged, and Never Looked At (the opener)

**Purpose:** Signal the chapter's starting point in one image. The launch pad from Chapters 12 to 15
is finished, cleared, well placed, and self-explaining, and that is real progress, but it has never
once been inspected, so a few small problems sit under the daily blur, cheap to fix today and
expensive to ignore. Mirrors Figure 1 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** The launch pad by the door, drawn well and complete, seen through a thin gray film
as if a faint haze sits over the whole corner. Tucked in the haze and easy to miss are two or three
small red problem-sparks: the base of the umbrella stand sitting in a shallow ring of standing
rainwater with the first orange bloom of rust, one key hook tilted a few degrees where its screw has
worked loose, a scuffed smudge on the wall behind the bag hook. Everything is arranged and correct.
Nothing has been looked at.

**Rough layout:**
- A warm dawn wash (a soft honey-to-cream vertical gradient), a low floor line in ink, the corner
  calm and settled, the same launch pad the reader already knows.
- The umbrella stand at left, its base in a shallow slate-tinted ring of rainwater `#BFD0D8`, a faint
  rust bloom `#C87A3A` where the metal meets the water, and a small red friction-spark `#CB4B36` at
  the rusting base. A small Newsreader label beneath in muted `#7C736A`: `umbrella stand`.
- The key hook at center, drawn a few degrees off level (a gentle rotate), a red friction-spark at
  the loosened screw, a `key hook` label beneath.
- The bag hook at right with a soft brownish scuff `#8A7E6E` on the wall behind it, a red
  friction-spark at the wall scuff, a `bag hook` label beneath.
- Over the whole corner, a faint gray film `#6A625A` at about 0.13 opacity, so the eye reads the
  scene as arranged but unseen, everything present and nothing yet examined.
- A faint Newsreader italic line floating in the haze, muted: `arranged, and never once looked at`.
- A small sans caps note along the base in `#7C736A`: `everything placed · nothing yet read`.
- Caption idea: "Everything here is cleared and arranged. It is also completely unread. Under the
  daily blur sit a few small things, a little standing water, a loosening screw, that are cheap to fix
  today and expensive to ignore. Shine is how you find them."

**Note for the illustrator:** the launch pad here is good, not a mess, so draw the corner as tidy,
finished, and settled, never as clutter or neglect. The quiet tension is that a well-arranged corner
can still hold small problems no one has seen, because no one has cleaned it closely enough to see
them. Keep the red problem-sparks small and half-hidden in the haze, two or three at most, so they
read as things you would miss on a normal day, not as alarms. Do not re-sort, re-arrange, or re-mark
anything; that work is finished and stays. Do not yet show the cleaning, the wipe, or the fix; the
opener's whole point is the unseen state before anyone looks. Keep it warm and matter-of-fact, no
scolding about the dust, just a quiet noticing that no one has looked.

## 2. The Two Jobs of a Wipe (the hero, clean to reveal)

**Purpose:** Make the hero move concrete in one glance: a wipe does two jobs at once, it makes the
surface clean and it makes the surface legible, and the second job, the reading, is the one that
protects the home. Mirrors Figure 2 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A hand holding a cloth wipes across a surface. To the left, still under its dull
film of grime, the surface tells you nothing. Where the cloth has passed, the film is gone, the
surface comes up clean, and in that clean stripe a small red problem-spark is revealed that the grime
had been hiding, a hairline crack or a water mark. The same wipe did two jobs: it made the surface
clean, and it made the surface readable.

**Rough layout:**
- A calm panel background `#FBF7EF`. Top, a small sans caps label in soft gray: `THE TWO JOBS OF A
  WIPE`.
- A wide surface band across the middle in warm `#F3EEE2` with an ink outline. The left half carries
  a dull gray film `#ADA491` with scattered specks of grime, and a Newsreader italic note beneath:
  `still under its film, it tells you nothing`.
- The right half is the clean, read stripe in pale `#FCF8F0`, carrying a revealed hairline crack
  drawn as a thin `#CB4B36` line and a small red friction-spark marking the tell. A Newsreader italic
  note beneath in terracotta `#BC4B2A`: `clean, and now it shows you the crack`.
- A hand-and-cloth glyph in wood fill `#E7C58B` and warm cloth tones, mid-wipe at the boundary
  between the two halves, with a short slate `#3C5A6B` dashed motion arrow showing the direction of
  the wipe from film into clean.
- A slate Newsreader italic line along the bottom: "One motion, two jobs: the same wipe makes the
  surface clean and makes it readable."
- The frozen rule set as if jotted beside the wipe, Fraunces in ink: "Don't just clean it. Read it."
- Caption idea: "Every wipe is also a look. The first job makes the surface clean; the second job
  makes it legible, and the second job is the one that protects the home, because the same cloth that
  lifts the grime lifts the blindfold with it."

**Note for the illustrator:** keep the two halves reading as one continuous surface caught mid-wipe,
not two separate panels; the point is that a single cloth crossing a single surface does both jobs at
once. The revealed spark is small and specific, a hairline crack or a water mark, not damage and not
drama; it is the thing the grime was quietly hiding. Use exactly one small red spark; the red is a
rare accent held for the tell alone. Do not add cleaning products, tools, or technique (that is
Chapter 18), and do not turn this into a before-and-after scrub comparison; the move is noticing
while your hand is already moving, not cleaning harder. Keep it warm, plain, and unhurried.

## 3. What a Surface Tells You (the inspection vocabulary)

**Purpose:** Teach the short, learnable vocabulary of reading a surface, so abnormal conditions become
easy to spot while they are still small. It shows the mark on one side and its meaning on the other,
a calm reference anyone can hold at a glance. Mirrors Figure 3 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A calm two-column reference. On the left, THE MARK; on the right, WHAT IT MEANS.
Four quiet rows: a water mark or damp patch means moisture reaching where it should not, a slow leak,
a bad seal, condensation; a fresh scratch or worn line means two things rubbing that should not, a
drawer catching, a door dragging; crumbs gathering in one corner mean a gap, a crack, or a draft
funnelling them there; a fitting that gives when touched means a fixing working loose, and loose
fixings only get looser.

**Rough layout:**
- A calm panel background `#FBF7EF`. Top, a small sans caps label in soft gray: `WHAT A SURFACE TELLS
  YOU`. Beneath it, two small sans caps column heads: `THE MARK` in terracotta on the left, `WHAT IT
  MEANS` in slate on the right, split by a faint vertical rule `#D9CDB8`.
- Row 1, water mark: a soft slate-blue bloom glyph `#BFD0D8`, `a water mark or damp patch` in ink,
  helper `a soft, dark bloom on the surface` in Newsreader italic, meaning in slate: `moisture
  reaching where it should not: a slow leak, a bad seal, condensation.`
- Row 2, worn line: a bright `#CB4B36` scratch stroke with a paler track under it, `a fresh scratch or
  worn line` in ink, helper `a bright track where the finish is gone`, meaning in slate: `two things
  rubbing that should not: a drawer catching, a door dragging.`
- Row 3, gathering crumbs: a small drift of specks `#8A7E6E` piling into one corner, `crumbs gathering
  in one corner` in ink, helper `the same spot, filling up again and again`, meaning in slate: `a gap,
  a crack, or a draft funnelling them into that corner.`
- Row 4, tilted hook: a small wood-fill hook `#E7C58B` drawn off level with a tiny red friction-spark
  beside it, `a fitting that gives when touched` in ink, helper `a hook, a handle, a rail sitting off
  level`, meaning in slate: `a fixing working loose, and loose fixings only get looser.`
- Rows alternate quiet fills (`#F3EEE2` and `#FBF7EF`) so they read apart at a glance; keep the whole
  card legible and unhurried.
- Caption idea: "A surface talks in marks. You do not need to fix anything to listen; you only need to
  know that a stain, a scratch, a drift of crumbs, or a hook gone loose each means something, and that
  catching it now is what keeps it small."

**Note for the illustrator:** this is a reference, so keep it calm, plain, and legible, a short
catalog, not a busy chart; four rows is the whole set, do not crowd it with more. The mark glyph on
the left should be simple and recognizable at a glance; the meaning on the right is one plain slate
sentence. Hold the red spark back to the single loose-fixing row where it does real work; the rest of
the card is calm. Reading is not fixing: do not draw tools, repairs, or a handyman; the card only
teaches noticing. Do not add cleaning methods or products (Chapter 18). Keep the mapping one to one
and honest.

## 4. The Cheapest Maintenance You Will Ever Do (catch it early, not late)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's payoff as a picture: everything in a home is slowly wearing out all
the time, and the only real question is whether you meet the wear early, when it is small and cheap,
or late, when it is a repair bill and a bad morning. Cleaning-as-inspection is how you meet it early.
This diagram has no direct HTML twin; it renders the "The Cheapest Maintenance You Will Ever Do"
section as a paired-panel figure, with the frozen One Idea to Keep beneath it.

**What it shows:** Two facing panels of the same two problems, read at two different moments. The left
is CAUGHT EARLY: the umbrella-stand base in a shallow ring of rainwater with a faint rust bloom, and
the key hook a few degrees off level, each with a small calm note, a rinse and dry, a screw turned
tight, ten minutes and no cost. The right is MET LATE: the same stand rusted through over a stained
patch of floor, the same hook pulled free with the keys still on it, each tagged with the expensive
outcome it became. The two panels are the same two tells, early and late, side by side.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: sans caps label, soft gray, `THE CHEAPEST MAINTENANCE`, with a Fraunces ink line beneath: "meet
  the wear early, while it is still small".
- Left panel, green header bar `CAUGHT EARLY`: the umbrella stand in its ring of water with a faint
  rust bloom and a small red friction-spark, the key hook tilted with a spark at the loose screw, each
  with a calm green note, `pour it out, dry the base, set something under it` and `turn the screw a
  half turn tight`. A green sans caps line at the base: `TEN MINUTES, NO COST`.
- Right panel, muted header bar `MET LATE`: the same stand rusted through over a spreading floor stain
  in terracotta-tint, the same hook torn from the wall with keys on the floor beneath it, each with a
  slate note, `a rusted-through stand and a ruined patch of floor` and `a hook that lets go with the
  keys attached`. A slate sans caps line at the base: `A REPAIR BILL AND A BAD MORNING`.
- Between the panels, a faint slate arrow from early to late labeled `if the cleaning never looks`, so
  the eye reads the right panel as what the left becomes when no one reads the surface.
- Beneath the two panels, a slate bordered strip carrying the frozen One Idea to Keep: "Clean to see,
  not to impress. When cleaning doubles as inspecting, the house tells you what it needs while problems
  are still small, and Shine turns a chore you dread into the cheapest maintenance you will ever do."
- Footer, Newsreader italic, slate: "You were going to clean anyway; Shine only asks the cleaning to
  pull double duty."

**Note for the illustrator:** this is relief, not a warning poster, so draw the left panel as the
warm, easy, ordinary one, the moment you actually live in, and keep the right panel matter-of-fact and
undramatised, a plain picture of what small wear becomes when it goes unread, never a scare image or a
guilt cue. The two problems are the same two from the launch pad first pass, the standing-water rust
and the loose hook; keep them identical across the panels so the eye reads one thing at two moments.
Use the red spark only on the early panel where the tell is still small and catchable. Do not draw
tools, technique, or a repair scene (that is later chapters); the point is the choice between the
cheap moment and the expensive one, not how to fix anything. Warm and quietly reassuring; the whole
message is that meeting wear early is smaller, calmer, and cheaper than meeting it late.

## 5. The Needle Takes Its First Shine Step (the friction meter)

**Purpose:** Close the chapter with the honest first-Shine-step move. Unlike a pure understanding
chapter, this one changed the room's state: a surface got clean and a small problem got caught early,
so the live needle honestly earns a move, stepping off the marked Straighten-complete milestone toward
calm, the first of Shine's steps, with the goal still ahead because Shine has only begun and three
more S's follow. Mirrors Figure 4 in the HTML, and drives the live data-driven gauge.

**What it shows:** The recurring half-circle gauge with the live needle stepped a short way off
Chapter 15's marked `STRAIGHTEN COMPLETE` tick toward the calm side, its reached position flagged
`FIRST SHINE STEP` in terracotta, marking a step and not a milestone. Chapter 15's Straighten-complete
milestone is now a small passed marker just behind the live needle; Chapters 14, 13, and 12 are
fainter dashed ghosts further back, and the Sort-complete milestone sits further back still. The goal
crosshair, tagged purpose, is still ahead on the green calm side because three more S's remain.

**Rough layout:**
- Half-circle gauge, arc colored calm green (left) through honey (middle) to friction spark (right),
  on a pale `#EDE3CE` track. The gauge is a live, data-driven SVG, consistent with prior chapters.
- The `SORT COMPLETE` milestone in slate at reduced opacity, marked on the band with a small slate
  chip, sitting furthest back as ground covered many chapters ago.
- The `STRAIGHTEN COMPLETE` milestone in slate, the second milestone, drawn clearly but now as the
  passed marker just behind the live needle, its chip and leader line reading as ground just covered.
- Faint dashed ghost needles at Chapter 14 (about 50 percent opacity), Chapter 13 (about 42 percent),
  and Chapter 12 (about 30 percent), tagged `Ch 14`, `Ch 13`, `Ch 12`, stepping back in order.
- The live needle in ink, stepped a short, honest distance off the Straighten-complete tick toward
  calm, its resting spot flagged by a small green dot and a terracotta `FIRST SHINE STEP` label, so it
  reads plainly as a step, not a milestone.
- A slate `GOAL` crosshair fully on the green calm side, carrying its `PURPOSE` flag, still clearly
  ahead of the live needle, because Safety, Standardize, and Sustain still follow and Shine itself has
  three more chapters to run.
- Bottom band: `CALM` (green, left) and `FRICTION` (spark, right).
- Footer, Newsreader italic, soft gray: "The needle takes its first Shine step. The goal is still
  ahead."
- Caption (frozen): "The needle takes its first Shine step, off the Straighten-complete mark toward
  calm. This time the room did change: a surface got clean, and cleaning it caught a small problem
  early, while it was still small. The goal is still ahead, because Shine has only begun and Safety,
  Standardize, and Sustain still follow."

**Honesty note for the illustrator:** this is a STEP, the first step of the third S, not a milestone
and not the finish line. The live needle must move a real but SMALL distance off the marked
Straighten-complete tick toward calm; that mark is now the passed marker just behind it, with the
earlier Straighten and Sort steps fainter still further back. Unlike prior Straighten chapters, the
needle genuinely earns this move, because the room's condition changed: a surface got clean and two
small problems were caught early. The needle must NOT reach the goal crosshair; the goal stays clearly
ahead on the green side, because Shine has only begun (targets, methods, and the reset are still to
come across Chapters 17 to 19) and Safety, Standardize, and Sustain still follow. A real first step
and a long road left are both true at once. No confetti, no completion glow, no swing all the way to
calm.
