# Facebook Longform Post: Photograph Before You Fix (Chapter 6)

---

There is a pile somewhere in your home that you cannot see anymore.

I do not mean it is hidden. It is probably in plain sight, somewhere you walk past a dozen times a day. The stack of papers, the tangle of cords, the chair that slowly became a wardrobe, the drop zone by the door. You looked right at it this morning, and your mind filed it under "normal, ignore," the same way it ignores the hum of the refrigerator and the feeling of your own socks.

This is not a flaw. It is actually a clever feature. Your brain habituates to anything that stays the same so it can spend its attention on what is new and what might matter. The downside is that the mess you live with becomes invisible to the one person most able to fix it, which is you. A guest walks in and spots it instantly. You have not really seen it in months.

Here is the part I love. There is a simple cure for this blindness, and you are probably holding it right now. A camera.

The lens does not habituate. It has no history with your home, no story about how the pile got there, no fond tolerance built up over a hundred busy mornings. It just records what is actually in front of it, sharply and honestly, with all the little frictions your eye has learned to smooth away. Point a camera at a space you have stopped seeing and the photo shows you a room you half recognize, a little worse than you remembered, suddenly visible again.

People are routinely a little shocked by the first before photo of a space they see every single day. Not because the room got worse overnight, but because they are finally seeing it without the soft filter of habit. That small shock is not a bad feeling to run from. It is accurate vision returning, and it is genuinely useful, because you cannot aim your effort at a mess you have trained yourself not to see.

Now, the real reason to take the picture is not about today. It is about a month from now.

The before and after pair is the most motivating thing in this whole method. You have felt its pull, scrolling past someone's transformed closet or cleared countertop, that little jolt of "I want that." And here is the secret: the power is not in the after. A tidy space on its own is just a nice photo. The power lives entirely in the gap between the two images, and that gap only exists if you captured an honest before.

Which is exactly why the embarrassing before is the valuable one. The urge to clean up a little first, just so it is not so bad, is really the urge to dodge a moment of mild discomfort. But that discomfort is the raw material of your future pride.

So here is your one small task. Go to the space that has gone invisible to you. Lift your phone, frame the whole thing, and take the picture.

Do not tidy first. The mess is the point. 📷
