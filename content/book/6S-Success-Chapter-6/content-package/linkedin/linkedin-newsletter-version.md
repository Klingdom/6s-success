# Take the Picture You Would Rather Not Take

*6S Success: Home Edition, Chapter 6*

There is a pile somewhere in your home that you cannot see anymore. I mean that quite literally. It is in plain sight, somewhere you walk past a dozen times a day, and your brain has quietly stopped reporting it. The stack of papers, the tangle of cords, the drop zone by the door. You looked right at it this morning and filed it under "normal, ignore."

I want to make the case for spending sixty seconds, before you fix that space, doing the one thing most people who try to get organized never do. Photographing it first.

## Why your eye lies to you

Your brain habituates to anything that stays the same. It does this so it can save its attention for what is new and what might matter. This is a feature, not a flaw. The trouble is that "stopped being news" and "stopped being a problem" feel identical from the inside, and they are not the same thing at all. A new pile stops registering in about three days. A guest walks in and sees it instantly. You have not really seen it in months.

There is a simple cure for this blindness, and you are holding it. A camera. The lens does not habituate. It has no history with your home and no fond tolerance built up over a hundred busy mornings. It flattens your familiar, forgiven space into a flat rectangle full of stuff, and a flat rectangle is something you can look at the way a stranger would. People are routinely a little shocked by the first before photo of a space they see every day. That shock is not a feeling to avoid. It is accurate vision returning.

## The before is for the after

Here is the real reason to take the picture, and it is not about today. It is about a month from now.

The before and after pair is the single most motivating artifact in this entire method. You have felt its pull already, scrolling past someone else's cleared countertop. But the power of a before and after is not the after. A tidy space on its own is forgettable. The power lives entirely in the gap between the two images, and that gap only exists if you captured an honest before. So the before photo is not vanity. It is evidence, the visual baseline that, paired with your audit number, proves the change actually happened.

Which is why the embarrassing before is the valuable one. A modest before makes a modest after. An honest, slightly mortifying before makes an after that genuinely moves you. You are taking it for the version of you, a few weeks from now, who will want to feel how far they came.

## How to shoot it

Treat it as one idea: you are not taking a photo, you are taking the first half of a matched pair.

- **Mark where you stand.** A scuff, a tile line, a note in your phone. Both shots come from the same spot, so the room is the only thing that changes.
- **Frame the whole area.** A little of the surroundings for context, not just a flattering corner. Wide shot first, then a detail shot of the worst friction point.
- **Get the light right.** Turn on the lights, open the blinds. You are making a clear record, not art.
- **Do not tidy first.** This is the rule people break every time. Every item you straighten before the before is transformation you erase in advance. A staged before is a lie, and the only person it fools is you.

## One last thing

The before is yours. You are under no obligation to show it to anyone. Keep it in a private album no one will ever see, use it purely as your own evidence, and delete it whenever you like. The photo serves you, not an audience. If you do want a little accountability, one trusted person you text the pair to is plenty.

So go to the space you audited. Choose your spot, frame the whole thing, resist the urge to fix even one item, and take the picture. The starting line is now drawn, in ink, and the only direction left to go is forward.
