# Chapter 6 Key Takeaways: Photograph Before You Fix

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **You stop seeing the messes you live with.** Your brain habituates to anything that stays the same, so the pile you walk past becomes invisible to you and obvious to a guest. It takes about three days.

2. **The camera is the cure, because it never habituates.** A photo shows your space the way a stranger sees it, which is the way it actually is.

3. **"Stopped being news" is not "stopped being a problem."** They feel identical from the inside. The photo separates them.

4. **A before photo is evidence, not vanity.** It is the visual baseline that, paired with your audit number, proves the change actually happened.

5. **A photo is also a second inspection.** Flattened into a frame, the space reveals the cord, the drawer that does not close, the colonized chair. The audit named the weakest S; the photo often shows where it lives.

6. **The before is for the after.** The before/after pair is the most motivating artifact in the method, and its power lives entirely in the gap between the two images.

7. **The gap only exists if the before is honest.** Skip the before and your finished space has nothing to be measured against. The work disappears.

8. **The embarrassing before is the valuable one.** A modest before makes a modest after. An honest, slightly mortifying before makes an after that genuinely moves you.

9. **Shoot a matched pair.** The drama comes from the before and the future after sharing the same spot, same frame, same light, so the room is the only thing that changed.

10. **Mark where you stood.** A scuff, a tile line, a note in your phone. The after only lands if it is shot from the exact same spot.

11. **Do not tidy first.** The same rule as Chapter 1. Every item you straighten before the before is transformation you erase in advance.

12. **Keep it, and keep it yours.** Save the photos in a named "6S Before" album. The before is private if you want it to be; you never have to post it. One trusted person is plenty for accountability.
