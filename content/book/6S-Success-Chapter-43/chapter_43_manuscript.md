Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks

## Chapter Opening

Every room you have worked so far in this book has been a place where somebody does something: sleeps, eats, washes, plays. The laundry room is the first one that is not. It is a process room. Nobody spends an evening in here. It has no comfort to protect and no atmosphere to get right, and it is judged by exactly one thing, which is whether the work gets through it without stopping.

That makes it simpler than the bedrooms and much less forgiving. Laundry arrives dirty, sits wet, and is not finished until it is back in a drawer, and it can stall at any one of those three points. A washer you have to empty before you can fill is a stall. A dryer that needs two cycles because its duct is packed is a stall. A shelf of bottles you do not trust is a stall, a bag that never fills is a stall, and a counter holding yesterday's basket is the biggest stall of all, because it is the one that sends a dry load back in for a second tumble. When a household says it is drowning in laundry, it is almost never doing more washing than it used to. It is running the same washing twice.

This room also carries a risk none of the others have carried, and it is why the Safety pass here is not a tidy-up. It is the reason the chapter exists at all, and it is why one of the standards in this room has a date on it.

The good news is that everything you need is already yours. The nine rules of Shine from the start of Part Nine carry over unchanged: top to bottom, dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, two cloths to clean and then dry, clean to inspect and flag rather than to fix. What this room adds is a layer of its own, and it is the layer of a working machine rather than a living space: get the lint out, put nothing away wet, clean the machine that does the cleaning, and keep the flow moving through the counter. Do that, one zone at a time, starting with both machines, and the room stops being the place laundry goes to wait.

## Six Zones, Not One Room

You do not clean a laundry room. You clean the two machines, then the shelf of chemistry, then the sorting bags, then the counter, then the drying rack, then the corner where the broom lives. Each is a job with a clear finish. And unlike most rooms in this book, the order here is not a suggestion, because five of the six zones are downstream of the first one. A bag of sorted darks is worthless if the washer is full of somebody's forgotten load. A bare counter does nothing if the dryer takes two cycles. Start at the machines, get them clean, clear and safe, and the rest of the room unblocks behind them.

> The laundry room is six small zones, not one big job: the washer and dryer, the detergent and treatment zone, the sorting and hamper zone, the folding surface, the hanging and air-dry zone, and the utility and cleaning zone. Do them one at a time, and start at both machines, because every other zone in this room is waiting on those two being ready to take a load.

There is one thing you do before you start, though, and it takes ninety seconds. Clear both lint screens and set the washer running on its longest, hottest empty cycle with a drum cleaning tablet in it. The machine then cleans itself in the background while you work the rest of the room, and you come back to Zone 1 at the end to finish the gasket, the drawer, and everything behind.

> A finished laundry room is one a household can run a load through without anything stopping it: both drums empty with the washer door propped ajar, nothing at all on either lid, a clean lint screen back in its slot and a strip of tape on the dryer with the month you last cleared the duct, one product per job standing on a wipeable tray with the true dose inked on the cap, three labelled bags below half full and clear of the walking line, a counter bare enough to roll pastry on with an empty basket per bedroom stacked under it, the rack folded flat with its tray dry, and every long handle hanging heads up over a painted outline.

## The Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

The passes take on a distinctly mechanical flavour here. In Sort, the ruthless question is not whether a thing is useful in principle but whether it has actually run: the stream that never fills, the treatment you never reach for first, the second vacuum that has not been switched on in a year. In Straighten, the governing idea is that nothing in this room rests on the floor and nothing leans, because handles that lean fall and bristles that sit on concrete splay. Safety is the whole reason this room is dangerous, and it runs through every zone below rather than waiting at the end. Standardize is where this room shines, because it is the most measurable room in the house: a dose line inked on a cap, a bag labelled by wash setting rather than colour, a photograph of the empty counter taped inside a cupboard door, an outline painted behind every hook, and a month written on tape. And Sustain is one motion attached to a sound you already respond to: when the dryer buzzer goes, the lint screen is cleared before the door shuts again.

## How to Shine the Laundry Room: The Method

The nine rules of Shine hold here exactly as they have all through Part Nine. You still work top to bottom, still go dry before wet, still mist the cloth rather than the surface, still clean with one cloth and dry with a second, still keep your cloth colours honest, and still clean in order to inspect and flag rather than to fix on the spot. If any of that is thin, Chapter Thirty-One has it in full.

What this room adds is the layer of a machine rather than a living space, and it has four parts.

> The laundry room is the first room in this book that is not somewhere you live, it is a machine you live with, and it is judged only by whether the work flows through it. Laundry arrives dirty, sits wet, and is not finished until it is back in a drawer, and it can stall at any one of those points. So you clean this room for the flow rather than for the look of it. You clear the lint, because lint is fuel and this is the hottest room in the house outside the kitchen. You put nothing away wet, because damp is what turns a working room sour. You clean the machine that does the cleaning, because it hides its own dirt in three places you have never looked. And you keep the folding counter bare, because that is the surface the whole household jams at, and a room that jams sends the dry load back in for a second tumble.

The first part is the one that makes this chapter different from every other room chapter in the book.

> The lint screen you clear is the part of this room you can see, and it is not the part that burns. The fuel that matters is the soft grey packing that builds where the duct bends behind the dryer, in the one room in your house that combines the most heat with the most fine dry tinder outside the kitchen. This is why the test is not whether the dryer looks clean, it is whether you can name the month you last pulled the machine out and cleared the duct. If you cannot name it, that is today's job and there is no debate, and while the machine is out and the duct is off, look at what the duct is made of, because if it is the flexible foil accordion kind it traps far more lint than a smooth rigid metal pipe and it should be replaced now rather than next time. There is rarely a next time. Once a year is a cheap habit, and the failure it prevents burns the room it happens in.

The second part is the one that explains why a clean laundry room can still smell.

> Every other room in this book gets dirty. This one gets damp, and damp is the failure that spreads, because a laundry room is full of things that hold water in folds you cannot see. So the standard here is not that a surface is clean, it is that a surface is dry and left open to the air. The washer door stands ajar between loads so the rubber boot is never sitting wet, the folds of that boot are wiped dry rather than merely wiped, the detergent drawer goes back into a dry cavity, the drying rack is folded flat so both faces get air rather than pressing wet metal against wet metal, the mop hangs to dry before it returns to the rail, and the bag liner that actually holds the smell is laundered rather than wiped. A thing put away damp in this room does not stay a damp thing. It becomes a musty one, and then it hands that smell to the next clean load that touches it.

The third part is the one most households have never done once.

> The most useful thing to understand about a washing machine is that it is not self-cleaning, and neither is a dryer. A machine that looks spotless from the front hides its dirt in exactly three places. There is the rubber gasket, whose folds you have to peel back one at a time to find the coins, the hairpins, and the grey slime. There is the detergent drawer, which pulls right out to its stop and lifts free so it can be washed in the sink, over a cavity whose roof is filmed with black mould. And there is the drum itself, whose biofilm sits deeper than any cloth reaches, which is why you finish by running the longest hottest cycle empty with a drum cleaning tablet. Do that and a washer stops being the thing that makes clothes smell faintly of the machine. Skip it and you are washing every load in the residue of every load before it.

And the fourth part is not about cleaning at all. It is about why the room jams.

> If the folding counter is holding yesterday's basket, the problem is not the counter and it is not the folding. Laundry is a flow with four stages, dirty, washed, dry, away, and a flow stops at whichever stage nobody owns. Almost every household owns the first three. Somebody puts the load in, somebody moves it to the dryer, somebody tips it out. Then the clean clothes sit, because carrying them out of this room and putting them in a drawer is the only stage that has never been given to a person by name. That is the whole jam, and it is why the counter is the surface the room is judged on. Give the last stage a name and a basket per bedroom so folding and delivering are one motion, and the counter empties. Leave it unnamed and the dry load goes back in for a second tumble, which is the sound of a household paying for the same work twice.

> **Quick Win**
> Clear both lint screens and start the washer on its longest hottest empty cycle with a drum cleaning tablet before you touch anything else, so the machine cleans itself while you work the rest of the room. Then do the two machines: empty both drums, clear both lids, and prop the washer door ajar. Every other zone in here is waiting on those two being ready to take a load.

> **6S Tip**
> Write the month on a strip of masking tape and stick it to the side of the dryer every time you clear the duct. The next check then costs you a glance instead of an argument with your memory, and the rule becomes something anyone in the house can audit from the doorway: if you cannot read a month on that tape, the duct is today's job.

## Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the heart of the chapter. For each of the six zones you get the inputs to gather before you start and the exact order of steps to clean and shine it. Start the empty hot cycle, then work the machines, and take the zones in order, because the room unblocks from the machines outward. Every zone finishes the same way, with a look for the things to flag for the Safety pass.

### Zone 1 · The Washer and Dryer

These two machines are what every other zone in this room is waiting on, and they hide their dirt in the three places you never open. Work top down, save the gasket and the lint screen for last, and finish behind the machines, where the duct and the fill hoses live and where most of the real time in this zone goes.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner.
- A washer drum cleaning tablet, and a dryer duct cleaning brush.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
- A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.
- A strip of masking tape and a permanent marker, and impact-rated safety glasses for the work behind the machines.

**Steps:**

1. Clear the tops and lids of both machines completely, taking everything off the room rather than shuffling it sideways, then mist the neutral pH cleaner onto a cloth rather than onto the machine and wipe each top from the back edge forward, buffing dry with a second cloth so no film is left where the next basket lands.
2. Go over the control panel, dials, and door handles with a dry cloth first to lift the lint, then a barely damp cloth over the handle and the panel where hands leave grime, keeping water out of the dial seams and working the detail brush into the ridges where grime has set.
3. Pull the detergent drawer out to its stop and lift it free, wash it in the sink in warm water to shift the caked powder and the blue softener gel, scrub the siphon cap with the detail brush, and set it aside to dry completely.
4. Wipe inside the empty drawer cavity, where black mould films the roof of it, and dry the cavity before the drawer slides back, because this is the single most commonly missed surface in the room.
5. Peel back each fold of the washer door gasket one at a time and wipe out the coins, hairpins, and grey slime, working right around the seal, then wipe the inside of the glass where detergent scum clouds it.
6. Dry the gasket folds properly rather than merely wiping them, and prop the door ajar so the drum breathes, since a boot left sitting wet is where the smell in a laundry room actually starts.
7. Wipe the washer drum with a damp cloth, then run the longest hottest cycle empty with a drum cleaning tablet or the machine's own clean setting, to clear the biofilm your cloth cannot reach. If you started this cycle at the top of the session, this is where you check it has finished.
8. Pull the dryer lint screen and peel the lint mat off it, and if dryer sheets have left a waxy film, wash the mesh under running water and dry it fully before it goes back, then vacuum inside the empty slot with the crevice tool.
9. Wipe the dryer drum with a barely damp cloth to lift the fabric softener film and stray lint, dry it, clean the inside of the door and its seal where lint packs into the lip, and leave the door open afterward so the drum airs.
10. Ease both machines out on their feet, disconnect the duct behind the dryer and clear it right through with the duct brush, then vacuum the dust and lint drift off the back panel and the slab and wipe the skirting and the floor where damp collects.
11. Reconnect the duct, walk both machines back, check they sit level and do not rock, then write the month on a strip of masking tape and stick it to the side of the dryer before you leave the zone.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a water stain or damp ring on the floor under the washer that dries and comes back, which points to a slow leak at a hose collar or the pump seal, so mark it and note the date; rust bleeding from under the detergent drawer or around the drum lip, which is the enamel giving way, flagged before it stains the next white load; a musty smell that survives a hot clean cycle, meaning biofilm deeper than the drum reaches; a scorch mark or a warm brown patch on the dryer cabinet, or a power cord that feels stiff or looks scuffed near the plug; and the fill hoses gone hard or bulged at the collar. Flag each. If the duct you have just disconnected is the flexible foil accordion kind, that is not a flag, that is a replacement.

### Zone 2 · The Detergent and Treatment Zone

This holds the chemistry that actually gets used, at a dose you can measure and out of a child's reach. Everything in this zone drips, so the shelf under a weeping spigot is the real job here, not the bottles. Work from the pod tub above down to the tray, and stand every container on plastic you can rinse.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, and a small detail brush set.
- A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.
- A wipeable leak tray, a chemical storage bin, and a drawer safety latch for the pod tub.
- A permanent marker and removable write-on labels.

**Steps:**

1. Dust the top of the high shelf with a dry cloth, then wipe the outside of the latched pod tub and the shelf lip with a damp cloth, keeping the lid shut throughout so no water reaches the pods.
2. Lift every container off the shelf and stand them on the floor, so you are cleaning a bare shelf rather than wiping around the problem.
3. Wipe each bottle from the shoulder down, paying attention to the detergent spigot and the crusted cap threads where dried powder cakes, and run the detail brush around the measuring cap's fill line so the true dose stays readable.
4. Mist the neutral pH cleaner onto the cloth and wipe the whole bare shelf, working the set drips and the sticky softener rings, then dry it before anything returns.
5. Wipe the shelf underside, its front edge where drips run down, and the bracket, where bleach fumes and stray drips work on the metal.
6. Carry the drip tray to the sink, rinse it, scrub the tacky corners, dry it, and set it back so the next drip lands on plastic you can rinse rather than a shelf that stays tacky for a year.
7. Vacuum any spilled powder from the floor beneath the shelf first, dry, then wipe the floor with the floor-material-compatible cleaner where a bleach splash has marked it.
8. Set the zone back deliberately: the everyday detergent and its cap within arm's reach of the open washer lid, bleach and the stain sprays behind them on the tray, the pod tub latched and above shoulder height, and bleach nowhere near any ammonia based cleaner.
9. Ink the true dose line on the cap with the marker, because the moulded cap is bigger than the dose almost every time, and write the job on the tray edge under each bottle, wash, whites, stains, so a returned bottle lands back in its own footprint.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a crystallized crust building where a spigot weeps, which tells you that jug drips every single use, so flag the drip and not just the crust; rust rings or a corroding shelf bracket under the bleach, which is the fumes eating the metal; a pod that has swollen, sweated, or split inside the tub; and any container whose label has worn or bleached until you cannot read it, which gets flagged as unnamed. Flag each.

### Zone 3 · The Sorting and Hamper Zone

This turns dirty clothes into loads that go straight into the machine without being handled twice. The thing to understand before you start is that damp gym kit is what makes a hamper smell, not dirt, so the liner matters more than the bin and air matters more than cleaner.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
- A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.
- A laundry hamper sorter, and removable write-on labels or a portable label maker.

**Steps:**

1. Empty the small dish on the shelf, wipe the shelf with a damp cloth, and clear anything sitting there older than today.
2. Tip each sorting bag completely empty, so you are working with bare bins rather than reaching past clothes.
3. Vacuum the crumbs and grit out of the bottom seam of each bag, which is where the weight of a season's pockets ends up.
4. Wipe the inside walls of each bag with the neutral pH cleaner on a cloth, then wipe the outside where hands grab it, and stand each one open to air fully before it lines up again.
5. Pull out any washable liner and put it in the machine, because the liner is what actually holds the smell, and dry it right through before it goes back in the bag.
6. Wipe the lid rims and the frame or stand the bags sit in, where damp and lint settle unseen, and dry them off.
7. Vacuum the strip of bare floor between the door and the machines, then mop it with the floor-material-compatible cleaner so no detergent film is left to make it slick.
8. Set the bags back standing clear of the walking line, each labelled with the load it makes rather than the colour it holds, hot towels, cold darks, gentle, so anyone in the house can start a machine without asking which setting the bag wants.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** soft dark spotting or a musty note in a bag seam, which is mould starting in the weave; a liner that has torn, or a bag bottom gone soft and damp; a frayed carry strap or handle on a bag you haul up the stairs loaded, flagged before it lets go; and anything the small dish has caught that should never have been near the drum, a button battery, a coin, a screw that rode in on a pocket. Flag each.

### Zone 4 · The Folding Surface

This is the stall point of the whole room, and the rule for it is the simplest in the chapter: the counter is either completely empty or holding exactly one load in progress, and there is no third state. Wipe it before you fold rather than after, because clean clothes pick up whatever detergent dust and lint the last load shed here.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a food-area degreaser for the iron film.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
- A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.
- One folding laundry basket per bedroom, and six spare hangers.

**Steps:**

1. Clear the counter right down, including everything that was never laundry, the post, the toolbox, the parcel waiting to go back, the vase from Christmas, because whatever is parked here is the reason a dry load goes back in for a second tumble.
2. Open the wall cupboard above and wipe its interior shelves and the spare stock stored there, then wipe the door fronts and the top edge, and dust the very top of the cupboard where lint settles and nobody looks.
3. Lift any folding board off the counter, mist the neutral pH cleaner onto the cloth, and wipe the whole run from the wall edge forward so grit travels off the front rather than around in circles.
4. Take a second pass with the food-area degreaser wherever an iron or a warm basket has left a starchy or greasy film, and buff dry, so the folding board does not skate on grit.
5. Wipe the counter's front edge and the underside lip where you grip and lean in to fold, which collects more grime than the top does.
6. Pull each basket out from underneath, vacuum the lint and stray socks from the bottom, wipe the rims, and stack them back one per bedroom.
7. Wipe the hanger rail at the end and the hooks of the six spare hangers where dust greys them, and hang the hangers rather than leaving them in a heap you have to unpick.
8. Vacuum the lint drift and any dropped pegs from the floor at your standing spot, then wipe the floor so nothing underfoot moves while your arms are full.
9. Photograph the counter empty and tape the photo inside the cupboard door, so nobody in the house has to guess what clear means.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a scorch mark or a heat-blistered patch on the counter from the iron soleplate; a loose screw or a bracket starting to pull on the wall cupboard above the spot where you lean in; the iron cord scuffed, kinked, or gone stiff near the plug; and a water stain or soft swelling on the underside of the counter, or a wobble in its legs. Flag each.

### Zone 5 · The Hanging and Air-Dry Zone

This dries the few things a hot drum would ruin, and its whole job is to get them off the rack the moment they are dry. A rack put away damp grows a musty smell and hands it straight to the next load hung on it, so drying the rack matters as much as drying the clothes.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
- A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.
- A drying rack with a drip tray, and clip hangers.

**Steps:**

1. Strip the rack completely, sending anything still damp back on and anything dry to its drawer right now rather than later.
2. Wipe each top bar and rail with a damp cloth to lift the film clothes leave behind, then dry each with a second cloth.
3. Wipe down into the rungs, joints, and pivots where water sits after a load, and dry them so they neither seize nor spot with rust.
4. Carry the drip tray out, rinse it, scrub the corners where mould starts, dry it, and set it back level under the rack.
5. Wipe the clip hangers and their pegs where wet garments hang and drip, working off the chalky lime spots the drying water leaves, then dry them so they do not mark the next shirt.
6. Wipe the skirting behind the rack for splash marks, then mop the floor under it with the floor-material-compatible cleaner so the wet patch does not stay slick.
7. Fold the rack flat against the wall so both faces get air rather than pressing wet metal against wet metal, and check as you do that it is not standing over a wall outlet or across an extension lead.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** rust blooming at the rack's joints and folding pivots where water pools and never fully dries; black mould speckling the tray corners, or a bar that still smells musty after you have wiped it; a hinge or lock gone loose, or a bar that no longer holds its angle when you set it; and a water stain spreading on the wall or skirting behind the rack. Flag each.

### Zone 6 · The Utility and Cleaning Zone

This holds the tools that keep the room and the strip of floor in front of the machines clean, dry and ready, and the principle governing it is that the tools that clean the room have to be clean and dry themselves. Nothing here leans and nothing stores wet.

**Inputs:**

- A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a floor-material-compatible cleaner.
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set.
- A compact vacuum with the attachments, and your caddy.
- A wall hook rail and broom and tool wall clips, floor boundary tape, and a medium clear lidded bin for the rags.

**Steps:**

1. Take the broom and dustpan off their hooks, knock the broom out over the bin, and pull the wound hair and lint off the dustpan lip and its brush.
2. Wipe both handles down where hands leave grime, and hang them back heads up and clear of the floor, because bristles resting on concrete splay and then re-soil the floor they touch.
3. Wipe the wall hooks and the painted outlines behind them with a damp cloth, so the shapes stay crisp and you can still see at a glance what is missing.
4. Tip the vacuum attachments out of the caddy, clear the hair wound around the brush tool and the crevice nozzle, wipe each one, and empty the vacuum canister before the caddy goes back.
5. Rinse the mop head until the water runs clear, wring it hard, and hang it to dry before it returns to the rail, so it does not spread grey water down the next floor it touches.
6. Rinse and dry the bucket inside and out, wipe the taped outline on the floor so its edges still read, and set the bucket back empty inside its square with nothing stored inside it.
7. Wipe the bottle exteriors and the shelf above the bucket where drain cleaner and bleach weep, keeping the cloth off the labels so they stay readable, and check that nothing corrosive is sitting at floor level beside the bucket.
8. Vacuum the dust and lint from the corner, then mop it with the floor-material-compatible cleaner.
9. Check the whole corner for anything leaning, and hang or bin it, because a long handle against a wall behind a door comes down at head height the moment the door opens.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a mop head still slimy or sour after rinsing, or a broom head shedding its bristles; a cracked bucket, a split broom or mop handle, or a dustpan edge gone rusty; the vacuum cord scuffed or stiff near the plug, or a canister seal that no longer closes cleanly; and a weeping drain cleaner or bleach bottle with the shelf bracket beneath it corroding or loosening under the weight. Flag each.

## The Calls That Matter Here

Six decisions in this room are harder than the cleaning, and every one of them is a thing you have been quietly postponing.

The duct you have not looked at. You have been telling yourself the dryer just runs a bit slow these days, and that towels have always needed two cycles. The dryer is not slow, it is blocked, and the rule from the method section applies here without debate: name the month, or do it today.

The five half used stain removers. A box of dye grabbing sheets, two spray bottles, a stick that dried solid in the cap, and something a relative swore by. You keep them because binning a mostly full bottle feels wasteful, so instead they sit there being wasteful slowly. Keep the one treatment you have personally watched lift a stain, plus a tub of oxygen powder, which handles most of what the others promise. A treatment you do not trust enough to reach for first is not a supply, it is a postponed decision holding the one arm's reach of shelf next to the open washer lid.

The delicates bag that never fills. You sort into five streams because a care label once told you to, and one bag has sat a third full for three weeks while the thing you wanted washed waits inside it. A stream earns its own bag only if it has run as a full load, by itself, in the last month. Everything else merges into cold and gentle with a mesh bag inside the drum. A bag that waits three weeks is not sorting, it is clothes you have taken out of circulation.

The single sock box. You keep a growing bag of unmatched socks because tomorrow the partner might surface. Occasionally one does, which is exactly why the bag never gets thrown out, and meanwhile it has become a permanent tenant on the one surface in this room that has to stay clear. Singles get one shoebox and nothing larger, emptied when it fills or at the change of season. A sock that has waited a whole season is not going to be reunited. It is just going to keep charging you the counter, and the counter is where the whole room jams.

The garment that only ever hangs. There is at least one thing on that rack you have washed more times than you have worn. It is hand wash only, it takes two days to give up its water, and it holds the rack hostage the whole time. Name the occasion you wore it for on each of the last three washes. If you cannot name all three, it goes, because line dry only is a running cost, not a neutral care label.

The rag pile. Worn towels, holed shirts and a retired set of sheets have quietly become a rag supply large enough to clean a small hotel, because turning a worn item into a rag feels like thrift rather than a decision, so nothing is ever discarded, only reclassified. Rags live in one bin and only one bin, and nothing new becomes a rag until something old leaves as waste. The same rule settles the second vacuum kept for the garage: if it has not run in a year, it is not a spare, it is a broken machine with an excuse.

> **The Nothing-Goes-Away-Wet Challenge**
> For one week, nothing in this room gets put away damp. The washer door stands ajar between loads, the gasket folds get wiped dry and not just wiped, the rack is folded flat rather than left standing half loaded, and the mop hangs to dry before it goes back on the rail. It is the single change that stops a laundry room smelling like a laundry room.

## Watch for in the Laundry Room

The Safety pass in this room is not a formality, and it is not last because it matters least. It is where the five questions have the most to say of anywhere in the house.

> This is the room where the six S's stop being about tidiness and start being about fire. The lint screen is cleared every load and the duct once a year, because lint plus heat is a fire and a crushed foil duct holds far more of it than a rigid pipe. The rubber fill hoses behind the washer get looked at for hardening and bulging at the collar, because a split hose sprays under pressure and does not stop until somebody reaches the tap, and it is usually sitting a hand's width from an outlet. The detergent pods go latched or above shoulder height without exception, because a pod looks exactly like a sweet to a toddler and bursts in the mouth. Bleach and any ammonia based cleaner never share a shelf and never share a bucket, and the drain cleaner does not live at floor level beside the mop bucket where a rinse can mix them. Nothing with a long handle ever leans, because a broom behind a door comes down at head height the moment the door opens. And if you iron on the folding counter, the iron gets a heatproof rest, the cord runs behind it where no arm can hook through, and you unplug at the wall rather than trusting the switch.

Burn or fire is the question this room answers first: lint in the duct and on the heating element side of the screen housing, an iron left face down or on its cord with clean clothing within reach of the soleplate, and textiles stacked against a water heater, a hot pipe, or its cable.

Water and electricity: fill hoses gone hard or bulged a hand's width from an outlet, and a drying rack standing over an outlet or across an extension lead with garments dripping beneath it.

Poison, choke, or strangle, all three of them storage decisions: pods within a child's reach, bleach shelved beside an ammonia based cleaner, and drain cleaner at floor level next to the mop bucket.

Fall, both of them taken with your arms full and your view blocked: a hamper in the walking line between the door and the machines or resting on a stair tread, and the wet patch under the rack crossed in socks.

Fall, cut, or crush has the longest list, because this room is full of heavy things and sharp edges: a machine that walks on an unbalanced spin because a foot is not level, and the sheet metal edge underneath when you tilt it back; reaching blind into a bag of work clothes where a utility knife or a pocketful of screws came home; long handled tools leaned against a wall or behind a door; a full jug of detergent above head height, reached into blind with wet hands; and a wall cupboard loaded with heavy backstock above the spot where you lean in to fold.

> **Common Mistake**
> Cleaning the outsides of two machines that are filthy on the inside, so every load comes out smelling faintly of the washer; telling yourself the dryer has just got slow when it is actually blocked; and blaming the folding counter for a jam that is really an unnamed job, because nobody has been made responsible for carrying the clean clothes out of the room.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> A laundry room is not clean because the surfaces are wiped. It is clean because the work flows through it: the lint is out, nothing was put away wet, the machine that does the cleaning has itself been cleaned, and the counter is bare because somebody by name carries the clean clothes out. Do one zone this week, and make it the machines, because every other zone in this room is waiting on them. Then write this month on a strip of tape and stick it to the side of the dryer.
