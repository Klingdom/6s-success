# Chapter 43 Podcast Script: The Laundry Room

*Eight to twelve minutes. Conversational, unhurried.*

---

Here is a claim to start with. If your household feels like it is drowning in laundry, you are almost certainly not doing more washing than you used to. You are doing the same washing twice.

Let me explain what I mean, because it is the whole idea of this chapter.

Every room we have worked so far in this book has been a place where somebody does something. You sleep in a bedroom. You eat in a dining room. You wash in a bathroom. The laundry room is the first one that is not like that at all. Nobody spends an evening in here. It has no atmosphere to get right and no comfort to protect. It is a process room, and it is judged by exactly one thing: whether the work gets through it without stopping.

And laundry is a flow with four stages. It arrives dirty. It gets washed. It gets dry. And then it goes away, back into a drawer. It is not finished until that fourth stage happens, and it can stall at any one of them.

Think about where yours stalls. A washer you have to empty before you can fill is a stall. A dryer that needs two cycles because the duct behind it is packed with lint is a stall. A shelf of bottles you do not trust is a stall. And a counter holding yesterday's basket is the biggest stall of all, because that is the one that sends a dry load back into the dryer for a second tumble. That second tumble is pure waste. Same clothes, same work, twice.

Now here is the part that surprised me most when I worked this room. The counter is not the problem.

A flow stops at whichever stage nobody owns. And almost every household owns the first three stages without ever having discussed them. Somebody puts the load in. Somebody moves it across to the dryer. Somebody tips it out onto the counter. Then the clean clothes sit there, sometimes for days, because the fourth stage, carrying them out of this room and putting them in a drawer, is the only one that has never been given to a person by name.

That is the whole jam. It is not laziness and it is not the folding. It is an unowned step. Give it a name, put a basket under the counter for each bedroom so that folding and delivering are one motion instead of two separate jobs, and the counter empties on its own.

Second thing, and this one is genuinely useful: a washing machine is not self-cleaning.

I think most of us assume that a machine that spends its life full of hot water and detergent must be the cleanest object in the house. It is not. It hides its dirt in exactly three places, and none of them are the part you can see.

The first is the rubber gasket around the door. You have to peel those folds back one at a time, and what you find in there is coins, hairpins, and a grey slime.

The second is the detergent drawer. It pulls right out to its stop and lifts free, so you can wash it in the sink. And behind it there is a cavity, and the roof of that cavity is very often filmed with black mould.

The third is the drum itself, where a biofilm builds up deeper than any cloth reaches, which is why you finish by running the longest, hottest cycle you have, empty, with a drum cleaning tablet in it.

So if your clean laundry has a faint smell you cannot quite place, that is not your detergent. That is the machine, and you have been washing every load in the residue of every load before it.

And now the part that matters most.

Outside the kitchen, this is the hottest room in your house. It is also the room with the most fine, dry tinder in it. And those two facts sit about a metre apart, behind the dryer.

The lint screen you clear every load is the part of this room you can see, and it is not the part that burns. The fuel that matters is the soft grey packing that builds up where the duct bends behind the machine.

So here is the test, and it is a single question. Can you name the month you last pulled the dryer out and cleared that duct?

If you can, good. If you cannot, then that is today's job, and there is no debate about it. And while the machine is already out and the duct is off, look at what the duct is actually made of. If it is the flexible foil accordion kind, replace it with rigid metal now, rather than promising yourself you will do it next time, because there is rarely a next time.

Once a year is a cheap habit. And the failure it prevents burns the room it happens in.

That is also why this chapter's before-and-after photo is unlike every other one in the book. You take the before, you work the six zones, you take the after, and you tape the after up as the standard. But there is one detail in that after photo that expires: a strip of masking tape on the side of the dryer with a month written on it. Every other thing in that picture you can restore in an evening. That one you have to earn again next year.

So: do one zone this week, and make it the machines, because every other zone in the room is waiting on them. Then write this month on a strip of tape and stick it to the side of the dryer.
