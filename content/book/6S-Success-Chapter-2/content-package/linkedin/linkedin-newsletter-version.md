# The drawer that hands you the answer

*A newsletter edition from 6S Success: Home Edition, Chapter 2*

Hi friend,

Let me start with a tiny moment you have probably lived.

You are at someone's place for dinner, and you offer to help. "Could you grab the bottle opener?" they say, nodding at a drawer. You open it, and there it is, right on top, in a little tray with the corkscrew and the can opener, the three of them lined up like friends waiting for the same bus. You did not have to ask which drawer. You did not have to dig past a tangle of menus and rubber bands.

Two seconds. Maybe three. And if you have ever lost a frustrated minute in a drawer holding eleven takeout menus, a dead battery, a warranty for a blender you no longer own, and exactly one chopstick, you know those easy seconds were not luck. Somebody set that drawer up to behave that way.

That is what I want to talk about today, because it has a name, and a surprising backstory.

## What you actually felt

The feeling was not tidiness. A drawer can be tidy and still useless. What you felt was findability: the space arranged so the answer comes to you, fast, with no effort and no thinking. The towel where your wet hand expects it. The scissors where you reach without looking. You only notice findability when it is missing, the way you only notice a chair when it is not there and you sit down hard on the floor.

6S is simply the method for building findability on purpose, then keeping it, cleaning it, and making it safe. That is really all it is.

## Where it came from

Here is the strange part. The method was worked out on a factory floor on the other side of the world.

After the Second World War, Toyota was trying to compete with much larger, much richer rivals. They could not win by spending more, because they did not have more. They had to win by wasting less. Out of that pressure came an idea called the visual workplace: set a space up so anyone can glance at it and tell whether things are where they belong. A missing tool leaves an obvious gap. A box in the aisle looks wrong immediately. Normal becomes visible, so problems get caught early.

The foundation was five practices, each starting with the letter S in Japanese: Seiri, Seiton, Seiso, Seiketsu, Shitsuke. Later named 5S, it walked out of the auto plant and into hospitals, airlines, offices, and eventually homes. Because the real point was never manufacturing. It was helping ordinary people do good work under pressure, without having to be heroes about it. Which is a fairly accurate description of a Tuesday morning with three kids and one missing shoe.

## The six words

In plain English: **Sort** what you need from what you do not. **Straighten** by giving what stays a deliberate home. **Shine**, cleaning in a way that lets you spot problems. **Safety**, catching the wobbly shelf and the chemicals stored above the cereal. **Standardize**, making the good arrangement the normal one. **Sustain**, with small habits that keep it from sliding back.

Five became six when people added Safety. At work it is one more pillar. At home it might be the most important step, because workplaces have inspectors and rules, and your home has you.

One quiet reassurance before I sign off. We are borrowing the logic of 6S, not the look of a factory. No yellow floor tape. The fruit bowl, the kids' art, the worn spot on the couch are not failures. They are the life that 6S exists to protect. We are not making your house quieter than a hospital. We are making your Tuesday mornings quieter.

## Before you go

Tonight, open any drawer and ask it one honest question: do you answer me, or do you interrogate me? That instinct is all six S's talking at once.
