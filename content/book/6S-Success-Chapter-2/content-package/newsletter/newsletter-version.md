# Newsletter Edition: What Is 6S?

**Subject line (placeholder):** The drawer that hands you the bottle opener

Hi friend,

Picture this. You are at a friend's place for dinner, and you offer to help. "Could you grab the bottle opener?" she says, nodding at a drawer.

You open it, and there it is, right on top, sitting in a little tray with the corkscrew and the can opener. You did not have to ask which drawer. You did not have to dig past a tangle of takeout menus and rubber bands. The drawer just handed you the thing you came for.

It is a small moment. Two seconds, maybe three. But if you have ever spent a frustrated minute pawing through a drawer that held eleven menus, a dead battery, a warranty for a blender you no longer own, and exactly one chopstick, then you know those two easy seconds are not luck. Somebody set that drawer up to behave that way.

That feeling, a space answering your question almost before you finish asking it, has a name. It is called findability, and there is a whole method for building it on purpose. The method is called 6S, and that is what Chapter 2 of the book is about.

## Where it actually came from

Here is the strange part. The method behind that bottle-opener drawer was not invented for kitchens. It was worked out on a factory floor in postwar Japan, where a car company called Toyota was trying to compete with bigger, richer rivals. They could not win by spending more, because they did not have more. They had to win by wasting less. Less material, less motion, less searching, less time.

Out of that came the Toyota Production System and a simple, very human idea: the visual workplace. Set a space up well, and anyone can glance at it and tell whether things are where they belong. The right tool is outlined on the board, so a missing one leaves an obvious gap. Normal becomes visible, which means problems get caught early instead of festering. The set of practices underneath it spread from car factories to hospitals, airlines, offices, and eventually, inevitably, homes.

## The six words

Here is the whole method in six words, in order:

Sort. Straighten. Shine. Safety. Standardize. Sustain.

Three make space, one keeps people safe, and two make it last. If you ever blank on a name, hold on to the order instead. The sequence is the method. The labels are only handles you grab it by.

And here it is in one plain sentence: 6S is a simple, repeatable method for making the right thing easy to find, easy to use, and safe, in any space, and keeping it that way.

Why does a factory method work at home? Because it was never really about cars. It was about helping ordinary people do good work under pressure, without having to be heroes about it. Which is a pretty fair description of a Tuesday morning with three kids and one missing shoe.

One quick reassurance, since it comes up. Borrowing 6S does not mean importing yellow floor tape and a label maker gone rogue. You are borrowing the logic, not the look. The fruit bowl on the counter, the kids' art on the fridge, the worn spot on the couch where everyone sits, those are not failures of 6S. They are the life it exists to protect.

## Try this one this week

You do not need to organize anything yet. Just do the thirty-second Safety walk. Tonight, walk the path you take in the dark, bedroom to bathroom. Is there anything on the floor you would trip on? Move it. That is your first thirty seconds of 6S, and you have not even started organizing.

That small instinct, the one that makes you move the thing on the floor, is the whole method talking. The rest is just detail.
