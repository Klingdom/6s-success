# Podcast Script: What Is 6S?

**Source:** Chapter 2, "What Is 6S?" from *6S Success: Home Edition*
**Slug:** what-is-6s
**Audience:** Busy home keepers and parents new to 6S
**Format:** Audio-first, conversational, single host. No reliance on visuals.
**Estimated runtime:** 8 to 12 minutes (approximately 1,550 spoken words)

---

## INTRO (about 1 minute)

Hey, and welcome back. I want to start today with a tiny moment, and I bet you have lived some version of it.

You are at a friend's place for dinner. You offer to help, and she says, "Could you grab the bottle opener?" and tips her head toward a drawer. So you open it. And there it is. Right on top. Sitting in a little tray with the corkscrew and the can opener, the three of them lined up like friends waiting for the same bus.

You did not have to ask which drawer. You did not have to dig past a tangle of takeout menus and rubber bands and a warranty for a blender she does not even own anymore. The drawer just handed you the thing you came for. Two seconds, maybe three.

That tiny, almost invisible moment is what this whole episode is about. Because that feeling, the feeling of a space answering your question almost before you finish asking it, has a name, and a method behind it. And the method was not invented for kitchens. It was worked out on a factory floor on the other side of the world, by people building cars with very little money and no room to waste.

So today, what is 6S? Six S words. By the end of this, you will know all six, where they came from, and why a method built in a factory belongs in your home. And you will not have to move a single object to follow along.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: FINDABILITY (about 2 minutes)

Let us sit with that drawer for one more second, because it is teaching us something important.

What you felt when it handed you the bottle opener was not tidiness. And that distinction really matters. A drawer can be tidy and still completely useless. You can line up junk very neatly and still not be able to find the one thing you actually need. We have all opened a "tidy" drawer and stood there, defeated.

What you felt was findability. The drawer was arranged so the answer came to you. Fast. With no effort and no thinking.

And once you have the word, you start noticing it everywhere. The towel is where your wet hand expects it. The scissors are where you reach without even looking. The phone charger lives in one known place, so charging your phone is a non-event instead of a small nightly quest where you check three rooms.

Here is the funny thing about findability. You only notice it when it is missing. It is like a chair. You never think about the chair until the one time it is not there and you sit down hard on the floor. Findability is the same. When it works, it is invisible. When it is gone, it is the most annoying part of your day.

So here is the simplest definition I can give you. 6S is the method for building findability on purpose, and then keeping it, cleaning it, and making it safe. That is really all it is. Everything else is just detail. A good space answers your questions. A cluttered one asks them. And you can feel that difference long before you can name it.

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: WHERE IT CAME FROM (about 2 minutes)

Okay, the factory. Here is the short version, and it really is a good story.

In the years after the Second World War, Japan was rebuilding, and a car company called Toyota was trying to compete with much larger, much richer rivals. And they had a real problem. They could not win by spending more, because they simply did not have more. So they had to win by wasting less. Less material, less motion, less searching, less time, less of everything that did not actually add value for the person buying the car.

Out of that pressure came what we now call the Toyota Production System. And one of its most powerful and honestly most human ideas was something called the visual workplace. The thinking is simple. If a workspace is set up well, anyone should be able to glance at it and instantly tell whether things are where they should be.

So the tool is outlined on the board, which means a missing tool leaves an obvious gap that you see immediately. The walkway is marked, so a box left in the aisle looks wrong right away. Normal becomes visible. And once normal is visible, abnormal jumps out at you, which means problems get caught early instead of quietly festering until they become expensive.

Now, the foundation of that visual workplace was a set of five practices, and each one began, in Japanese, with the letter S. Later, engineers and consultants gave the set a tidy name, 5S, and it walked right out of the car factory.

It went into hospitals, where a missing surgical instrument is a genuine emergency. Into airline maintenance, where a tool left inside an engine can cost lives. Into offices and warehouses and laboratories and kitchens. And eventually, inevitably, into homes. Because the truth underneath the whole thing was never really about manufacturing. It was about helping ordinary people do good work under pressure, without having to be heroes about it.

And honestly, if you think about it, "ordinary people doing good work under pressure" is a pretty accurate description of a Tuesday morning with three kids and one missing shoe.

Quick myth to clear up while we are here. A lot of people think 6S is "a Japanese cleaning system." It is not. Cleaning is one part of one step. The real engine is findability, flow, and safety.

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: THE SIX WORDS (about 2.5 minutes)

So let us actually meet the words. We will do the original five first, then the one that got added.

The first is Seiri, which we call Sort. Separate what you need from what you do not, and remove what you do not. And here is the thing most people get backwards. Most spaces are not short on room. They are full of things that belong somewhere else, or nowhere at all. Sort is where you make those decisions you have been avoiding.

Second is Seiton, which we call Straighten. Give everything that stays a clear, deliberate home, arranged by how often you actually use it. The daily things get the easy spots, right at hand. The rare things move out of the way. That is it.

Third is Seiso, which we call Shine. Clean the space, but clean it in a way that lets you notice problems. Because in 6S, cleaning is partly inspection. While your hands are already in there wiping things down, you find the leak, the crack, the loose hinge. You catch the small thing before it becomes the big thing.

Now, the original method goes straight to the next two, but in this book we put a step here, and I will explain why in a second. So hold that thought.

The fourth original idea is Seiketsu, which we call Standardize. Turn the good arrangement into the normal arrangement, out in the open, so it survives a busy week and survives a person who has never used the space before. And the fifth is Shitsuke, which we call Sustain. Keep it up. The small habits and gentle check-ins that stop your good work from quietly sliding back into a junk drawer.

So that is the original five. Sort, Straighten, Shine, Standardize, Sustain. Where does the sixth come from?

People running this method in real workplaces kept bumping into the same realization. None of it matters if someone gets hurt. A perfectly sorted, beautifully labeled workshop is a failure if a frayed cord or a blocked exit puts a person in danger. So a lot of organizations added a sixth pillar and made it explicit instead of just assumed. Safety. You will sometimes hear this called "5S plus 1," which is just a way of saying "the original five, plus this one we refuse to leave to chance." When Safety joins the family, 5S becomes 6S.

And in this book, we place Safety fourth, right after Shine and before Standardize. Remember that thought I asked you to hold? Here is the reason. The first three steps have you handling, moving, and looking closely at every single part of a space. That is the perfect moment to notice hazards, because you are already there with your hands on everything. The wobbly shelf, the overloaded outlet, the cleaning chemicals stored above the cereal, the rug that slides, the heavy box balanced where it could fall on a small head. Safety catches all of that before you lock the arrangement in.

And one last thing on the words. If you ever blank on a name, do not let it rattle you. Different books use slightly different words. One says "Set in Order," another says "Straighten." The words are just handles. The order is the method. Hold onto the sequence.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY (about 1.5 minutes)

So here is what I want you to actually walk away with.

First, organized does not mean cold. When people hear "factory method," they picture yellow tape on the floor and every spatula traced on a pegboard. That is not the goal. We are borrowing the logic of 6S, not the look of a factory. The logic is gentle. Make the right action the easy action. Make normal visible so problems show up early. Protect the people who use the space. A well-run home is not an empty one. The fruit bowl on the counter, the kids' art on the fridge, the worn spot on the couch where everyone piles in. Those are not failures of organizing. That is the life the method exists to protect. We are not trying to make your house quieter than a hospital. We are just trying to make your Tuesday mornings quieter.

Second, and this is the part I love, you only learn one system. You do not need a different method for the kitchen and the closet and the garage. The same six steps fix a single junk drawer in fifteen minutes or a whole garage over a few weekends. The scale changes. The steps do not. Which means every small project you do is practice for the big ones. The drawer you sort out tonight is teaching you the exact moves you will use on the garage in spring.

And here is the honest, slightly surprising payoff. You already understand more about this method than most people ever will, and you have not moved a single object yet.

So if you want one tiny thing to do tonight, here it is. Walk the path you take in the dark, from your bed to the bathroom. Is there anything on the floor you would trip on? Move it. That is your first thirty seconds of the Safety S. You are already doing 6S.

## OUTRO AND SOFT CTA (about 30 seconds)

So, what is 6S? A simple, repeatable method for making the right thing easy to find, easy to use, and safe in any space, and then keeping it that way. Six words. Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, Sustain. Three make space, one keeps people safe, two make it last.
