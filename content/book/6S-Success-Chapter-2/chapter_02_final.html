Part One · Discovering 6S

Chapter Two

What Is 6S?

Six odd little words, one factory floor in Japan, and a method that turns out to belong in your kitchen drawer.

You are at a friend's place for dinner, and you offer to help. "Could you grab the bottle opener?" she says, tipping her head toward a drawer.

You open it, and there it is, right on top, sitting in a little tray with the corkscrew and the can opener, the three of them lined up like friends waiting for the same bus. You did not have to ask which drawer. You did not have to dig past a tangle of takeout menus and rubber bands. The drawer practically handed you the thing you came for.

It is a small moment. Two seconds, maybe three. But if you have ever spent a frustrated minute pawing through a drawer that held eleven menus, a dead battery, a warranty for a blender you no longer own, and exactly one chopstick, then you know those two easy seconds are not luck. Somebody set that drawer up to behave that way.

That feeling, the feeling of a space answering your question almost before you finish asking it, is the whole point of this book. And here is the strange part. The method behind that bottle-opener drawer was not invented for kitchens. It was not invented for homes at all. It was worked out on a factory floor on the other side of the world, by people trying to build cars with very little money and no room to waste.

In Chapter 1, I promised you a factory in Japan that has everything to do with your kitchen drawer. This is that chapter. Stay with me, because the story is shorter and more useful than you might expect, and by the end you will know exactly what those six S words mean and why they belong in your home.

6S Tip

A good space answers questions. A cluttered one asks them. You can feel the difference long before you can name it.

In this chapter

  • What the five original S's actually mean, in plain English.

The felt senseThe Calm You Can Feel

Before we get to the history, sit with that drawer for one more second, because it is teaching you something important.

What you felt when it handed you the bottle opener was not tidiness, exactly. A drawer can be tidy and still useless. You can line up junk very neatly and still not find anything that matters. What you felt was findability. The drawer was arranged so the answer came to you, fast, with no effort and no thinking.

Findability is the quiet superpower running underneath every space that feels good to use. The towel is where your wet hand expects it. The scissors are where you reach without looking. The charger lives in one known place, so charging your phone is a non-event instead of a small nightly quest. None of this announces itself. You only notice findability when it is missing, the way you only notice a chair when it is not there and you sit down hard on the floor.

6S is the method for building findability on purpose, and then for keeping it, cleaning it, and making it safe. That is really all it is. Everything else is detail.

The short versionWhere This Actually Came From

Now, the factory.

In the years after the Second World War, Japan was rebuilding, and a car company called Toyota was trying to compete with much larger, much richer rivals. They could not win by spending more, because they did not have more. They had to win by wasting less. Less material, less motion, less searching, less time, less of everything that did not add value for the customer.

Out of that pressure came what is now called the Toyota Production System, an approach to work obsessed with removing waste in all its forms. One of its most powerful and most human ideas was the visual workplace. The thinking was simple. If a space is set up well, anyone should be able to glance at it and instantly tell whether things are where they belong. The right tool is outlined on the board, so a missing tool leaves an obvious gap. The walkway is marked, so a box left in the aisle looks wrong immediately. Normal becomes visible, which means abnormal becomes visible too, and problems get caught early instead of festering.

The foundation of that visual workplace was a set of five practices, each beginning, in Japanese, with the letter S. Sort the necessary from the unnecessary. Set everything that remains in a deliberate place. Keep it clean, and inspect while you clean. Make the good arrangement the standard. And sustain it, so the gains do not melt away the moment everyone gets busy. Later, Japanese engineers and consultants gave the set a tidy name, 5S, and it spread far beyond car factories.

It turned out that "make the right thing easy and the wrong thing obvious" is not a car idea. It is a human idea. So 5S walked out of the auto plant and into hospitals, where a missing instrument is a real emergency, into airline maintenance, where a tool left in an engine can cost lives, and into offices, warehouses, and eventually, inevitably, homes. Because the truth underneath the whole thing was never about manufacturing. It was about helping ordinary people do good work under pressure, without having to be heroes about it. Which, if you think about it, is a fairly accurate description of a Tuesday morning with three kids and one missing shoe.

Look at what the visual workplace turns into once it stops wearing steel-toed boots.

Common Mistake

Thinking 6S is "a Japanese cleaning system." Cleaning is one part of one step. The real engine is findability, flow, and safety.

The original setThe Five S's, in Plain English

Let us meet the five that started it all. Each is one Japanese word and one English idea.

A small confession about those English words. If you pick up three different books on this topic, you may find three slightly different sets. One calls the second step "Set in Order," another "Straighten," another "Systematize." One calls the third "Shine," another "Sweep." This is because the originals are Japanese, and translators have spent decades keeping both the meaning and the satisfying string of S's intact. Do not let it rattle you. The words are just handles. What matters is the idea behind each one, and the order they come in.

6S Tip

If you ever blank on a name, hold on to the order instead. The sequence is the method. The labels are only handles you grab it by.

Adding the sixthHow Five Became Six

So if the original set is five, where does the sixth S come from?

For a long time, people running this method in real workplaces kept bumping into the same realization. None of it matters if someone gets hurt. A perfectly sorted, beautifully labeled workshop is a failure if a frayed cord or a blocked exit puts a person in danger. So many organizations added a sixth pillar and made it explicit rather than assumed: Safety. You will sometimes see this written as 5S plus 1, which is just a way of saying "the original five, plus this one we refuse to leave to chance." When Safety joins the family, 5S becomes 6S.

In this book, Safety is our fourth S, and we place it deliberately, right after Shine and before we lock anything in with Standardize. There is a reason for that position. The first three steps have you handling, moving, and looking closely at every part of a space. That is the perfect moment to notice the hazards, because you are already there. You spot the wobbly shelf, the overloaded outlet, the cleaning chemicals stored above the cereal, the rug that slides, the box of heavy things balanced where it could fall on a small head. Safety catches all of that before you make the arrangement permanent.

At home, the Safety S is not a formality. It might be the most important step in the whole method. Workplaces have inspectors and rules. Your home has you. The stairs in the dark, the knives in the drawer, the medicine within a toddler's reach, the garage where the heavy and the sharp and the flammable all live together. Nobody is going to audit those for you. The good news is that the same walk-through that organizes a space can secure it at the same time, if you simply remember to look.

Quick Win

Walk the path you take in the dark, bedroom to bathroom. Anything on the floor you would trip on? Move it. You just did your first thirty seconds of the Safety S.

A gentle reassuranceYour Home Is Not a Factory

Right about now, some readers start to worry. They picture yellow tape on the kitchen floor, every spatula traced in marker on a pegboard, a label maker gone rogue, a home that looks like a tidy little warehouse where no one would ever curl up with a book.

Please relax. That is not where we are going.

Here is the distinction that makes this whole book work. We are borrowing the logic of 6S, not the look of a factory. The logic is timeless and gentle: make the right action the easy action, make normal visible so problems show up early, and protect the people who use the space. The look of a factory is just one way that logic dressed itself for one kind of work. Your home gets to dress the same logic in its own clothes.

A well-run home is not an empty one. It is not a minimalist showroom and it is not a museum where nothing may be touched. The fruit bowl on the counter, the kids' art on the fridge, the worn spot on the couch where everyone sits, those are not failures of 6S. They are the life that 6S exists to protect. The method is in service of your home feeling more like itself, not less. We are not trying to make your house quieter than a hospital. We are trying to make your Tuesday mornings quieter, which is a very different goal.

Common Mistake

Believing that organized means bare, or that tidy means cold. 6S serves the life in your home. It is not there to erase it.

It scalesSame Method, Any Size

Here is the last thing to understand before we start using 6S, and it is the thing that makes the method so forgiving. You do not learn a different system for every room. You learn one system and point it wherever you like.

The same six steps that fix a single junk drawer will fix a shelf, a closet, a countertop, an entire bedroom, the garage, the car, even the photos on your phone. The scale changes. The steps do not. A drawer might take fifteen minutes. A garage might take a few weekends. But in both cases you are sorting, then straightening, then shining, then making it safe, then standardizing, then sustaining. Always the same loop.

This is why the second half of this book is a set of room-by-room playbooks. Those chapters are not six new methods for six new rooms. They are the same six steps, with specific tips for the quirks of a kitchen versus a bathroom versus a kid's room. Learn the loop once, here, and the rest of the book becomes a collection of friendly suggestions rather than a pile of new homework.

Family Challenge

Each person names the most findable spot in the house and the least findable. The loser's pick becomes a future project. The winner gets bragging rights and possibly the last cookie.

Say it simplyPutting It Together

So, what is 6S? It is a simple, repeatable method for making the right thing easy to find, easy to use, and safe, in any space, and then keeping it that way. It was forged in a car factory under real pressure, refined in hospitals and offices and workshops around the world, and it works at home for the same reason it worked everywhere else. It is built around how people actually behave when they are busy and tired, not how we wish they would behave.

Six words carry the whole thing. Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, Sustain. Three make space, one keeps people safe, and two make it last. You already understand more about this method than most people ever will, and you have not moved a single object yet.

6S in one sentence

A simple, repeatable method for making the right thing easy to find, easy to use, and safe, in any space, and keeping it that way.

Quick Win

Open any drawer in your home right now and ask it one honest question: do you answer me, or do you interrogate me? That instinct you just used is all six S's talking at once.

In closingThe Spring Is Loaded

You came into this chapter with six odd little S words and a vague sense that they came from somewhere serious. You leave it knowing what each one means, where they were born, and why a method designed to build cars turns out to be exactly what a busy household needs.

Remember the heart of it. 6S was created so that ordinary people could do good work under pressure, without needing to be perfect or heroic about it. That is not a factory idea. That is a kindness, and it is a remarkably good description of what it takes to run a home full of real people and real life.

In Chapter 3 we go through all six steps in order, one connected flow from beginning to end, so you can see how each step hands off to the next before we dive deep into any single one. Think of it as a quick lap around the track before the real training begins.
