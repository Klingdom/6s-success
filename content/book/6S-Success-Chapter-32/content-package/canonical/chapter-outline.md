## 1. Chapter Opening: the room that matters most, and the chapter that is about the clean
If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most. It is the busiest space in the house, the one the whole family passes through and works in every day, and, minute for minute, the one where a proper 6S pays back the fastest and the hardest. It is also, by a distance, the dirtiest, because everything that makes a kitchen a kitchen also makes a mess: heat bakes grease onto surfaces, food leaves residue in every corner, water and steam get everywhere. That is why this chapter, more than any other, is about the clean. You already have the method from the door: the nine rules of Shine, and the habit of inspecting every surface your hand passes over. The kitchen takes all of that and adds one big new element, grease, which behaves differently from ordinary dirt and needs a different approach. A kitchen that is genuinely clean is one where the crumbs under the toaster are gone, the grease is off the top of the cabinets, the filter in the dishwasher has been rinsed, and the mold is out of the fridge gasket: the difference between a room that looks clean and a room that is safe to cook in.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- Break the kitchen into its seven small zones and run them one at a time.
- Gather the exact inputs, including the degreaser and brushes, each kitchen zone needs.
- Clean and shine every zone with a precise, professional method, soaking the grease first.
- Handle grease, dwell time, and food-safety cloth discipline the way a professional does.
- Inspect each surface as you clean it, flag what needs fixing, and hold the room with a before-and-after pair.

## 3. Seven Zones, Not One Room
The kitchen looks like the most daunting room in the house to deep clean, and if you face it as one job it is. Faced as its zones, it is a series of finishable pieces, and you can do them across a few sessions or knock out the worst two on a Saturday morning. As at the door, the trick is to stop seeing the room and see the zones. Each zone has a clear edge and a clear finish: you do not clean the kitchen, you clean the cooking zone, which is done when the hood, the grates, the splash wall and the hob are degreased and the crock is wiped. If you want the highest payback for the least effort, start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the seven zones): The kitchen is seven small zones, not one big job: the prep counter, the cooking zone, the sink, the upper cabinets, the lower cabinets and cookware, the utensil and utility drawers, and the fridge and freezer. Do them one at a time and the busiest room in the house comes back to you a zone at a time.
- Pull-quote (frozen, what a finished kitchen is): A finished kitchen is one you could start cooking in with your eyes shut: a clear run of prep counter, oil and your turner within one reach of a clean hob, an empty dry sink the room resets from, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, drawers that close on one push, and a fridge where the eat-first bin sits right where the door opens onto it.

## 4. The Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain
As at the door, four of the passes are quick, so the chapter moves through them and then slows right down for Shine. Sort each zone before you clean it, because a buried surface cannot be cleaned: clear the counter to a board-wide run, cap the frying pans at your busiest week plus one, keep one everyday set of dishes, and bin the leftovers you have moved forward three times. Straighten the survivors by use: oil and turner within one reach of the hob, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, one named job per drawer. Safety, Standardize and Sustain come after the shine and each is short: Safety acts on what the cleaning turned up (anchor a tall unit, turn pan handles in, get the loose blade out of the drawer); Standardize locks it (a date label on every leftover, a name inside each drawer's front edge, the eat-first bin where the door opens onto it); Sustain hangs the upkeep on what already happens (when the dishwasher starts, the counter clears; when the coffee brews, the sink gets checked; on the way to write the shopping list, the fridge gets a look).

## 5. How to Shine the Kitchen: The Method
You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one still holds. The kitchen simply adds the hardest opponent in the house, grease, and grease does not behave like ordinary dirt. Three things follow: soaking (the single biggest time-saver in the room); the right input for the job (a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for everyday food residue, the food-area degreaser the moment a surface feels tacky rather than dirty, followed by a clean damp pass); and lifting, not wiping around. One more thing particular to the kitchen: food safety, keep a dedicated cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, treat the sink sponge as the dirtiest object in the room, and never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the basin.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the nine Shine rules recap): You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door: work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the grease rule): Grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward. Set the soakable parts to soak before you do anything else, work top to bottom so what you loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner only smears a tacky film around.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the soak-and-dwell reframe): The most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time. Set the burner grates and the hood filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touch anything else, and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time. The chemistry lifts baked grease while you work elsewhere, so you scrub a fraction of what you otherwise would.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the lift-do-not-wipe-around reframe): The crumbs and grease you never see are the ones living under the toaster's feet, on top of the cabinets, and in the fold of the fridge gasket. A wipe around the clutter is not a clean; lifting the appliance, emptying the shelf, and pulling the cloth into the fold is. In a kitchen, that difference is the difference between a room that looks tidy and a room that is actually hygienic.

## 6. Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It (the centerpiece)
Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag on the way. Gather the inputs into your caddy first. Where a zone needs soaking, that step comes first so the soak works while you do the rest.
- **Zone 1, the primary prep counter** (the stretch of counter where food is cut, mixed, and plated; the crumbs live under the appliance feet). Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; food-area degreaser; a colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth); the caddy. Steps: (1) clear the counter completely; (2) do the backsplash first, top to bottom; (3) clean the knife block or strip; (4) lift the toaster and kettle and clean under them; (5) clean the open run of counter, degreasing where it is tacky; (6) finish with the board and the edges. Inspect and flag: a scorch mark behind the toaster or kettle; a cracked or fraying cord; the sealant bead along the counter-to-backsplash seam lifting or gone dark; a soft or swollen patch near that seam; a loose rivet or wobbling handle on the knife, or a hairline split in the cutting board.
- **Zone 2, the cooking zone** (the space either side of the burners, and the hood above; grease is the whole job here, and it hides upward). Inputs: food-area degreaser; neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; a colour-coded cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth); a small detail brush set; hot water and dish soap for soaking; the caddy. Steps: (1) soak the soakable parts first (hood filter, burner grates and caps); (2) degrease the hood, top down; (3) degrease the splash wall, top to bottom and back to front; (4) clean the hob and burner wells; (5) scrub and dry the soaked grates and refit the hood filter; (6) do the oven door front and the panel; (7) wipe the crock, the hook, and the spice fronts; (8) finish with the landing space. Inspect and flag: a grease-soaked hood filter and a strained fan; a burner that lights slow or burns yellow, or a grate that rocks; scorching or bubbling paint on the splash wall or hood underside; a chip or crack in the hob surface; a frayed or heat-stiffened cord on the burner side.
- **Zone 3, the sink and dishwashing zone** (the washing point the whole kitchen resets from; the sponge and the dishwasher filter are the ones that matter most). Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; a colour-coded cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth); a small detail brush set; the caddy. Steps: (1) clean the tap, base, and aerator; (2) clean the basin, drain, and overflow; (3) wipe the dispensers and the rim; (4) deal with the sponge, brush, and rack; (5) clean the dishwasher gasket and filter; (6) clear under the sink. Inspect and flag: a damp patch or warped board under the trap, the sign of a slow leak; soft black mold in the folds of the dishwasher gasket or the sealant around the sink rim; rust or a green crust on a pipe joint; a sponge or brush that smells while dry; swelling or dark staining along the front edge of the cabinet base.
- **Zone 4, the upper cabinets** (eye-level storage for the dishes you use every day; the dust band at the back tells you how long it has really been). Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; streak-free glass and mirror cleaner (for glass fronts); food-area degreaser (for the greasy underside nearest the hob); a colour-coded cloth set (a surface cloth, a glass cloth, a dry cloth); a small detail brush set; a compact vacuum with the brush attachment; the caddy. Steps: (1) do the cabinet tops first (vacuum then wipe); (2) empty and wipe each interior shelf; (3) degrease the underside nearest the hob; (4) do the glass fronts; (5) detail the doors and hinges. Inspect and flag: a shelf that flexes or bows, or a loose shelf pin; a hinge screw pulling out, or a door out of square; a water stain on a shelf, hinting at a leak above; a greasy film that keeps returning fast, a sign the extractor is not clearing the air; a soft, swollen patch on the cabinet bottom above the kettle.
- **Zone 5, the lower cabinets and cookware** (deep, low storage for the heavy pieces; the prize is the shelf floor and its tacky oil film). Inputs: food-area degreaser; neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; a colour-coded cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth); a small detail brush set; a compact vacuum with the crevice tool; the caddy. Steps: (1) empty the cabinet fully; (2) degrease the shelf floor (let it dwell, then wipe, because a dry cloth just skates over it); (3) wipe the walls and back, top to bottom; (4) do the door interior and lid rail; (5) wipe each piece as it goes back and twist each pan handle; (6) clear the runners and the toe-kick. Inspect and flag: a loose rivet, wobbling handle, or cracked helper handle on a pan; the tacky oil film returning to the shelf floor faster than it should; a damp patch on the cabinet floor along the plumbing wall; rust on cast iron, a carbon-steel pan, or a bare baking sheet; a pull-out runner that binds or drops.
- **Zone 6, the utensil and utility drawers** (the shallow drawers of hand tools and wraps; the crumbs that jam a drawer live under the divider and in the runner track). Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; food-area degreaser (for a drawer near the hob); a colour-coded cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth); a small detail brush set; the caddy. Steps: (1) lift the insert out and tip the grit; (2) wipe the drawer base; (3) clean the runner tracks; (4) do the front and the label edge; (5) wipe the wrap boxes. Inspect and flag: a runner that binds, drops, or grinds even once cleared, or a runner bracket screw backing out; a drawer front that no longer closes flush; rust or pitting on a peeler edge, a paring knife, or a mandoline blade; crumbs or grease gone tacky under the insert; a cracked or splitting plastic insert leaving a sharp edge.
- **Zone 7, the refrigerator and freezer** (cold storage that keeps food safe and shows what you own; the gasket, the vents, and the coils are the parts a quick wipe always misses). Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner; a colour-coded cloth set with a dedicated colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf; a compact vacuum with the brush attachment; warm water and dish soap for washing the crisper drawers; the caddy. Steps: (1) do the top of the unit first (vacuum then wipe); (2) wipe the exterior doors and handle; (3) wipe the interior shelves and walls, top to bottom; (4) pull and wash the crisper drawers and clear the sticky ring beneath; (5) do the door bins, seals, and gasket (pull the cloth into the folds); (6) clear the vents and the freezer; (7) vacuum the condenser coils. Inspect and flag: soft black mold in the folds of the door gasket; water pooling under a crisper drawer, the sign of a blocked defrost drain; a gasket gone slack, torn, or no longer springing back; thick frost on the freezer wall; a shelf thermometer reading warm, or a motor that runs long and hot once you reach the dusty coils.
- Callout (Quick Win): Do the cooking zone's soak-first trick. Before anything else, lift the burner grates and caps and the hood filter into hot degreasing water and leave them. While they soften on their own, degrease the splash wall and the hood from the top down. Come back, scrub the loosened grates in a fraction of the time, dry them fully, and the dirtiest corner of the kitchen is done.
- Callout (6S Tip): Give the degreaser its dwell time instead of scrubbing: spray it, leave it the time on the label, and it lifts the grease for you. Keep a separate cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, and never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the sink, because together they make a gas that will drive you out of the room.

## 7. The Calls That Matter Here
Cleaning to a method makes the kitchen shine, but a few genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean. The first is the appliance you love but rarely use: count real uses over the past month; anything you reach for less than a couple of times a week comes off the counter and into a low cabinet, and anything you have not switched on in a year leaves the kitchen. The second is the spice rack you are not honest about: open the jars and smell them over the bin, and keep spices for the food you actually cooked in the last year. The third is whether the sink is allowed to be a staging area (settle soaking with a deadline rather than a ban). And the last is the container of good leftovers you have moved to the front three times: name the meal, the day, and who eats it, or bin it today and cook a little less next time.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the reset-point reframe): The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, which means it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. The sponge is the dirtiest object in the room and the dishwasher filter is why your glasses come out gritty, so in this one zone the things you never think to clean are exactly the things that matter most.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Give each person a zone to own on reset day, and put the fridge eat-first bin right where the door opens onto it, so the answer to what is for dinner comes from the bin rather than the cupboard, and far less food is thrown away.

## 8. Watch for in the Kitchen
The kitchen holds more real hazards than any other room, and cleaning to inspect will surface most of them, but run the five safety questions once directly. Burn or fire: never leave oil heating unattended, and keep tea towels and the paper-towel roll away from a live burner. Fall, cut, or crush: never leave a knife blade-up in the drying rack or hidden under suds, turn pan handles in over the hob, keep glassware off shelves above head height, and store blades sheathed. Water and electricity: keep the kettle, the sockets, and a charging phone out of splash distance of the basin. Poison, choke, or strangle: never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner, keep loose button batteries out of the utility drawer, and seal raw meat on the lowest shelf so its juices cannot drip onto ready-to-eat food. And the plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool, never a chair, a counter edge, or an open drawer.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Wiping around the appliances and the clutter instead of lifting and emptying, so the crumbs under the toaster, the dust felt on the cabinet tops, the oil film on the shelf floor, and the mold in the fridge gasket all stay. A kitchen cleaned only where you can see is tidy, not hygienic. Lift, empty, and pull the cloth into the fold.
