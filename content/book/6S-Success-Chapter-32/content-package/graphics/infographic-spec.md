# Infographic Spec: The Kitchen Shine Method

**Chapter 32, "The Kitchen"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks · THE SECOND PLAYBOOK**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter: the KITCHEN SHINE METHOD, the grease layer that makes a
kitchen clean different from a doorway clean, built on the soak-first move, the one governing idea that grease
hides upward, and the exact inputs. It carries the chapter's spine in one glance: you already know the nine rules
of Shine from the door, and the kitchen simply adds the hardest opponent in the house, grease, which does not
behave like ordinary dirt. The concept pairs the hero grease rule, **Grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and
grease hides upward. Set the soakable parts to soak before you do anything else, work top to bottom so what you
loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner
only smears a tacky film around.**, with the picture at its heart: the soak set going first (the burner grates
and the hood filter submerged in hot degreasing water while you work elsewhere), the order of work drawn top to
bottom (hood and cabinet tops, then counter, then toe-kick), the switch to the degreaser the moment a surface is
tacky, and the kitchen kit, the exact inputs, laid out by type. So the viewer sees the whole move at once: this
is how you beat grease to a professional standard, and every zone procedure in the chapter is just this method
applied to a particular surface.

It stays consistent with the four inline figures in `chapter_32_final.html`, and in particular with Figure 3, the
two-panel `THE KITCHEN SHINE METHOD AND THE KIT`, drawn in the accent greens of the Shine step. The Shine step
owns the green accent: the method arrow, the numbered nodes, and the soak-first move are green; slate carries the
labels and the "glass" cloth; terracotta carries the warm accents. The single small warm red is held back
entirely except for the one true hazard mark in this room, the never-mix-bleach warning. This is not a lecture
and not a scolding: it is a professional's method laid out plainly so anyone can follow it with their sleeves
rolled up. Types, never brands: every cleaner, cloth, brush, tool, and appliance is a plain generic type, never a
brand name or a real logo. The register is warm, practical, and expert, capability and a little pride, no shame
about the before, the confidence of a method that is faster because it actually works. Clean to a method, not
until it looks better.

## Layout notes

- The grease rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the whole kitchen Shine pass compressed: grease
  hides upward, so soak first and work top to bottom, and reach for the degreaser the moment an everyday cleaner
  only smears. The eye should read the grease rule, then see the order of work (soak first, then top to bottom,
  hood and cabinet tops down to the toe-kick) and the four moves make it concrete.
- The order of work and the four moves are the hero, so make the soak-first move and the top-to-bottom direction
  unmistakable: the burner grates and the hood filter submerged in a basin of hot degreasing water at the head,
  drawn largest and greenest with a small dwell-time clock, then a single green arrow running down a kitchen
  elevation from hood-and-cabinet-tops to the toe-kick. Draw the degrease-the-tacky switch plainly, an everyday
  neutral pH cleaner failing on a tacky film beside the food-area degreaser succeeding. Set the grease rule
  byte-for-byte as it reads in the canonical quotes; it is the frozen string.
- The kit sits in support, not competing with the method: the exact inputs laid out by TYPE, a food-area
  degreaser (the one input that changes everything), a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a streak-free glass and
  mirror cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber set with one colour dedicated to the fridge and raw-meat shelf, a
  detail brush set, a compact vacuum with brush and crevice attachments, and a portable caddy. Label every one by
  type, never by brand, draw no logo and no recognisable packaging.
- Hold the color discipline: the green Shine accent carries the method arrow, the numbered nodes, and the
  soak-first move; slate carries the labels and the "glass" cloth; terracotta carries the warm accents; the
  deeper label green carries the small labels; the single small warm red is held back entirely except for the one
  never-mix-bleach hazard mark by the sink, because this is a calm method card and the kitchen has exactly one
  chemistry hazard worth a red mark.
- This image makes the concept, the thing that makes a kitchen clean harder than a doorway clean: grease hides
  upward, and the answer is time, not a scrubbing arm, so you soak first. Do NOT re-teach the six S's or fully
  re-teach the nine-rule Shine method (recap only, it was taught at the door in Chapter 31), and do NOT teach the
  pantry of Chapter 33, which is named only as where the reader points the loop next. Draw no friction meter,
  retired for Part 9. And name no brand and draw no specific product; the bottles, cloths, brushes, tools, and
  cloth colours are plain and generic, teaching the method, not a shopping list.
- Register: warm, practical, and expert, sleeves-rolled-up instruction rather than a lecture; the method exists
  because it is faster and it actually works. No force or discipline-as-punishment language, no scolding, no
  hype, no corporate or boardroom look, no smugness, and no shame about the before. Give the soak-first move its
  due, because time, not a scrubbing arm, is the most powerful tool in a kitchen clean: set the grates and the
  filter to soak first and they soften on their own while you degrease from the top down.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the order of work and the four
  moves drawn by hand as a calm method card rather than a clinical diagram, the green arrow a hand-drawn stroke
  over a kitchen elevation, the soak basin and the dwell-time clock plain and friendly, the whole image the warm,
  capable register of a professional method taught plainly: not a tidy kitchen, a genuinely clean one, safe to
  cook in.
