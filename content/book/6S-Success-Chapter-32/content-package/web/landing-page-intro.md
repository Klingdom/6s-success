# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 32, The Kitchen

You already own the whole six-S method and the loop that keeps it turning, and at the door you saw the applied format work: for every small zone of a room, the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, done the way a professional would rather than the way most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better. Now the same method meets the room that matters most.

If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most. It is the busiest space in the house, the one the whole family passes through and works in every day, and, minute for minute, the one where a proper 6S pays back the fastest and the hardest. It is also, by a distance, the dirtiest, because everything that makes a kitchen a kitchen also makes a mess: heat bakes grease onto surfaces, food leaves residue in every corner, water and steam get everywhere. That is why this chapter, more than any other, is about the clean, and why it spends most of its length on precisely how to do it. By the end you will be able to take the kitchen apart zone by zone and put it back genuinely clean, and you will know what you are holding in your hand at every step.

The reason a whole kitchen feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not. It is seven small zones that share a name and a bit of counter, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones. You do not clean the kitchen. You clean the cooking zone, which is done when the hood, the grates, the splash wall and the hob are degreased and the crock is wiped, then you clean the next zone. The kitchen is seven small zones, not one big job: the prep counter, the cooking zone, the sink, the upper cabinets, the lower cabinets and cookware, the utensil and utility drawers, and the fridge and freezer. Do them one at a time and the busiest room in the house comes back to you a zone at a time. If you want the highest payback for the least effort, start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime.

You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one of them still holds: work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix. The kitchen simply adds the hardest opponent in the house, grease, and grease does not behave like ordinary dirt. Grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward. Set the soakable parts to soak before you do anything else, work top to bottom so what you loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner only smears a tacky film around. Two more moves make the difference. The most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time: set the burner grates and the hood filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touch anything else, and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time, and the chemistry lifts baked grease while you work elsewhere. And lift, do not wipe around: the crumbs and grease you never see live under the toaster's feet, on top of the cabinets, and in the fold of the fridge gasket, so lifting the appliance, emptying the shelf, and pulling the cloth into the fold is the clean, and a wipe around the clutter is not. One thing is particular to the kitchen: food safety. Keep a dedicated cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, treat the sink sponge as the dirtiest object in the room, and never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the basin.

Then comes the heart of the chapter, Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather into your caddy before you start, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Gather the inputs first, and where a zone needs soaking, that step comes first so the soak works while you do the rest. You get the real supplies list for each zone, which cleaner, the food-area degreaser for the greasy parts, which colour-coded cloth with a dedicated colour for the fridge and raw-meat shelf, the detail brush, the vacuum attachment, the caddy, and the true order of work. It walks you through the hard-to-clean spots most guides skip: the soaked-then-scrubbed burner grates and hood filter, the degreased splash wall and hood underside, the dishwasher door gasket and the filter twisted out of the floor of the machine, the greasy cabinet tops and the tacky oil film on a shelf floor, the crisper drawers washed in the sink and the sticky ring beneath, the fridge gasket folds, and the dusty condenser coils.

Cleaning to a method makes the kitchen shine, but a few genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and the chapter names them. The appliance you love but rarely use gets counted by real uses over the past month. The spice rack you are not honest about gets opened and smelled over the bin. Whether the sink is allowed to be a staging area gets settled with a deadline rather than a ban. And the container of good leftovers you have moved to the front three times gets a name, a day, and an eater, or it is binned today. The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, which means it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed, and the sponge and the dishwasher filter, the two things you never think to clean, are the ones that matter most.
