# The Kitchen

**Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks**
Chapter 32 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

Subtitle · The kitchen is the busiest room in the house and the one 6S pays back fastest, because it is worked hardest and gets dirtiest: grease, food, spills, and heat. This playbook points the whole loop at it one zone at a time, and gives you, for each of the seven zones, the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it to a professional standard, degreasing and all, so the room that feeds you is genuinely, inspectably clean.

---

## Chapter descriptor

The second chapter of Part Nine and the second of twenty room playbooks (Chapters 31 to 50), following the instruction-centric template proven at the entryway in Chapter 31. The six-S method, Parts 1 to 8, is complete; from here the book points the finished loop at one real room at a time, and the format's heart is the detailed, step-by-step clean-and-shine instruction for each micro-zone, with the exact inputs to gather. If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most, the busiest space in the house and, minute for minute, the one where a proper 6S pays back the fastest and the hardest. It is also, by a distance, the dirtiest, because heat bakes grease onto surfaces, food leaves residue in every corner, and water and steam get everywhere, several times a day. That is exactly why this chapter, more than any other, is about the clean, and why it spends most of its length on precisely how to do it. Its freeing idea carries from the door: you never have to face a whole room, because the kitchen is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon. The kitchen is seven: the prep counter, the cooking zone, the sink, the upper cabinets, the lower cabinets and cookware, the utensil and utility drawers, and the fridge and freezer. Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain get a brisk pass; then the chapter slows right down for Shine. It recaps the universal nine rules from the door, work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix, and adds the kitchen's grease layer, four moves: soak first, work top to bottom, lift rather than wipe around, and switch to the degreaser the moment a surface is tacky, with the one input that changes everything here, the food-area degreaser, and food-safety cloth discipline throughout. Then, as the core of the chapter, "Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It" gives each of the seven zones the same three parts: the exact inputs to gather into the caddy, the numbered steps in the true order of work, and an inspect-and-flag list, because Shine finds the hazards and the safety pass fixes them. It lands the definition of a finished kitchen, one you could start cooking in with your eyes shut, names the room's genuinely hard calls, the appliance loved but rarely used, the spice rack no one is honest about, the sink as a staging area, and the leftovers moved forward three times, runs the kitchen's real hazards through the five safety questions, and closes with the Part 9 signature that retires the friction meter: the before-and-after pair, the before kept as the proof of how far the room came and the after taped up as the standard the kitchen has to match. Then a light hand-off to Chapter 33, the pantry, the kitchen's storeroom, where the same method meets a different problem.

## How to read this chapter

This is the second chapter of Part Nine, Room-by-Room Playbooks, and it is a PLAYBOOK, not a lesson. Read it differently from the thirty chapters that taught the method, because its centerpiece is a set of instructions: for each of the kitchen's seven small zones, the exact inputs to gather into your caddy and the precise, numbered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it to a professional standard, degreasing and all. So read the opening once for the frame, that the kitchen is a short list of finishable zones and not one overwhelming Saturday, take the recap of the nine-rule Shine method and the kitchen's grease layer once, and then treat the long central section, "Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It," as what it is, a procedure to follow with your sleeves rolled up, caddy gathered, one zone at a time. It is intentionally the longest room playbook so far, because the kitchen has seven zones and the clean is the whole point. Then go and do the one small, concrete thing it asks: gather the inputs for a single zone this weekend, the cooking zone or the sink, and clean it start to finish to the method, soak first.

The chapter rests on one method carried forward, one added layer, one core, and one closing ritual. The method is Shine, and this chapter USES the nine rules the door taught and does not re-teach them; it recaps them in a line and then applies them. The added layer is grease, the hardest opponent in the house, which does not behave like ordinary dirt: grease is the whole job and grease hides upward, so you soak the soakable parts first, work top to bottom, lift rather than wipe around, and reach for the food-area degreaser the moment a surface is tacky rather than dirty, following it with a clean damp pass so no residue is left where food will touch. The core is "Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It," the heart of the chapter, where each of the seven zones gets its inputs, its numbered steps in the true order of work, and its inspect-and-flag list, and where clean-to-inspect does its double duty: your hand passes over every surface, so your eye should too, and what it finds, a grease-soaked hood filter, a rocking grate, a slow leak under the sink, a slack fridge gasket, gets flagged, not fixed, because Shine finds it and the safety pass fixes it. The ritual is the before-and-after pair that closes every room in Part 9 and replaces the friction meter of the earlier parts: you shoot the before from the doorway before you touch a thing, framing the run of counter, the hob and hood, and the sink together, and keep it, and when the room is done you shoot the after from exactly the same spot and keep it as the standard. Around those sit the room's hard calls and its five safety questions, each named and answered. The register throughout is warm, practical, and expert, the voice of someone who cleans to a standard and will show you exactly how, and a little proud, because the kitchen is the biggest single win in the house: this is where the reader gets to spend the method they worked to build, so the feeling is capability, not instruction, and the before state is honoured, kept as proof and never mocked.

A word on the scope fence, so the chapter stays the playbook it is. This chapter USES the method the reader already owns and does not re-teach it. It recaps and applies the nine-rule Shine method and clean-to-inspect from the door rather than re-explaining the theory; the whole idea of deciding only whether a thing lives here belongs to the Sort teaching; the five safety questions it runs in the kitchen were taught in the hazard hunt; and the ideal-state photo it tapes up was taught in Standardize. It names product TYPES only and never a single brand name: the inputs listed for each zone are cleaners, cloths, brushes, and tools by type, so the reader can buy for the job, and the exhaustive product list is a separate appendix, not this chapter. It stays in the kitchen throughout and gives only a light one-line hand-off to the pantry. And it invents no statistics and no prices. The friction meter, which reached its goal at Chapter 30, is retired for Part 9; from here each room chapter closes with its own transformation instead, the before-and-after pair.

If you only do one thing: gather the inputs for one kitchen zone into a caddy this weekend and clean it start to finish, to the method. Start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime and give the biggest visible and hygienic win. Where the zone soaks, set the soak going first, the burner grates and the hood filter into hot degreasing water, then work top to bottom, degrease the tacky, lift rather than wipe around, and finish with the floor. That one zone is the smallest possible proof of the whole chapter, that a kitchen is not clean because the counters are clear but because you lifted the appliances, soaked the grease, and cleaned every surface to a method. You already know how. This is where you get to watch the room that matters most become genuinely, inspectably clean underneath.
