# Chapter 32 LinkedIn Comment Prompts: The Kitchen

Five prompts to start genuine discussion. Drop one in the comments of a post or article to invite replies. Warm, practical, expert voice, the friendly pro who shows you how. Foreground the how-to method without lecturing. No corporate framing, no hype, no force or shame language, no brand names, product types only.

---

1. Be honest about how you clean a kitchen: do you wipe around the toaster and the kettle and call the counter done, or do you lift them and clean underneath? The claim in this chapter is that a kitchen looks tidy and is actually hygienic are two different rooms, and the difference lives under the appliance feet, on top of the cabinets, and in the fold of the fridge gasket. Where do you think the crumbs and grease are quietly building up in your kitchen right now, in a spot a quick wipe never reaches?

2. Here is the rule the whole chapter turns on: grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward, so you soak the soakable parts first and work top to bottom. Most of us do it backwards, scrubbing the hob while the grates and the hood filter sit there baked-on. If you set the grates and the filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touched anything else, and let the degreaser sit for its dwell time instead of scrubbing, how much of the effort do you think you would actually save? Have you ever let time do the work for you?

3. The idea I keep coming back to is that the two things you never think to clean in a kitchen are the ones that matter most: the sponge is the dirtiest object in the room, and the dishwasher filter is why your glasses come out gritty. Be honest, when did you last clean the dishwasher filter, or is this the first you have heard it has one? The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, which means it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. Does that change what you would check first this weekend?

4. A quiet safety one worth surfacing: the chapter says never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the sink, because together they make a gas that will drive you out of the room. A lot of us reach for two bottles at once without thinking about what is in them. Did anyone ever actually tell you which cleaners must never meet, or did you pick it up by luck? And do you keep a separate cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, or does one cloth do everything?

5. The playbook works the kitchen as seven small zones, not one big job, and says to start with the cooking zone or the sink because those two carry the most grease and give the biggest win. Most of us look at "deep clean the kitchen" as a dreaded lost weekend and put it off for months. Does having the exact inputs to gather into a caddy and the numbered steps for a single zone, say the cooking zone with the soak going first, change whether you would actually start this weekend? Which zone would you clean first?
