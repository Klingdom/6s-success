### Slide 1 (hook)

**If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most. It is the busiest, the dirtiest, and the one a proper 6S pays back fastest. Watch the loop clean it, one zone at a time, to a method.**

The kitchen adds the hardest opponent in the house: grease. Grease bakes onto surfaces in the heat, hides in the folds and on the tops you never look at, and does not behave like ordinary dirt. So this playbook does not stop at telling you what to do. For every one of the seven kitchen zones it gives you the exact inputs to gather, the degreaser and the brushes included, and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it to a professional standard, soaking the grease and lifting the appliances rather than wiping around them, so the room that feeds you is genuinely, inspectably clean.

The book line, verbatim: "If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most." Swipe, and watch it change.

### Slide 3

**You do not 6S the whole kitchen. You 6S one zone of it, then the next. The kitchen is seven small zones, not one big job.**

Faced as one job, the kitchen is the most daunting room in the house. Faced as its zones, it is a series of finishable pieces you can do across a few sessions, or knock out the worst two on a Saturday morning. Start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime and give the biggest visible and hygienic win.

### Slide 4

**Four of the six passes are quick. Then you slow right down for Shine, which is where the kitchen stops being tidy and becomes clean.**

Sort each zone first, because a buried surface cannot be cleaned: clear the counter to a board-wide run, cap the pans at your busiest week plus one, keep one everyday set of dishes, bin the leftovers you have moved forward three times. That is the frame. The heart of it is Shine, and in the kitchen Shine is the whole point, because the kitchen adds grease, and grease does not behave like ordinary dirt.

### Slide 5

**Learn one method once and use it in every zone: the nine rules of Shine, carried from the door. The kitchen adds one thing, grease, and grease hides upward. This is the slide to photograph.**

Every step-by-step procedure in this chapter is these nine rules applied to a particular zone, plus the grease layer on top. Learn them once and they carry through all twenty room chapters.

The nine rules, frozen, in order:

- work top to bottom
- dry or clear before wet
- back to front and clean to dirty
- mist the cloth not the surface
- two cloths one to clean and one to dry
- colour-code your cloths
- match the cleaner to the surface
- give it dwell time
- clean to inspect and flag rather than fix

The grease rule, frozen, the kitchen Shine principle:

"Grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward. Set the soakable parts to soak before you do anything else, work top to bottom so what you loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner only smears a tacky film around."

### Slide 6

**The kitchen keeps the nine rules and adds four moves: soak first, work top to bottom, lift rather than wipe around, and switch to the degreaser the moment a surface is tacky. The one input that changes everything is the food-area degreaser.**

The most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time. Soaking is the single biggest time-saver in the room, and dwell time lets the chemistry lift baked grease while you work elsewhere. Where a surface feels tacky rather than dirty, your everyday cleaner will only smear that grease around, so switch to the degreaser and follow with a clean damp pass so no film is left where food will touch. And because this room handles raw food, keep a dedicated cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf.

The soak-and-dwell reframe, verbatim: "The most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time. Set the burner grates and the hood filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touch anything else, and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time. The chemistry lifts baked grease while you work elsewhere, so you scrub a fraction of what you otherwise would."

### Slide 7

**Now the core: each zone gets the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect as you go. Where a zone soaks, that step comes first so the soak works while you do the rest.**

Zone 1, the prep counter. Inputs: multi-surface cleaner, food-area degreaser, a surface cloth and a dry cloth, the caddy. Steps: clear the counter completely; do the backsplash first, top to bottom, and drag the cloth the length of the counter-to-backsplash seam; clean the knife block or strip and dry it at once; lift the toaster and kettle and clean under them, coiling the cords; clean the open run, switching to the degreaser where the surface is tacky; finish with the board and the front lip and underside overhang.

Zone 2, the cooking zone, the grease-heavy heart, so you soak first and work from the top down. Steps: soak the hood filter and the burner grates and caps in hot degreasing water first; degrease the hood, top down; degrease the splash wall, top to bottom and back to front; clean the hob and burner wells with the grates off; scrub and dry the soaked grates and refit the filter; do the oven door front and the control panel with a barely damp cloth; wipe the crock, the hook, and the greasy spice fronts; finish with the hot-pan landing space.

The book line, verbatim: "Here is the core of the chapter."

### Slide 8

**The same method, the reset point and the eye-level storage. The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, so it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed.**

Zone 3, the sink and dishwashing zone. Steps: clean the tap, base, and aerator; clean the basin, drain, and overflow slot; wipe the dispensers and the sealant bead around the rim; deal with the sponge, brush, and drying rack; clean the dishwasher gasket, pulling into the folds, and twist out and rinse the filter; clear under the sink and dry the base. The two things you never think to clean, the sponge and the dishwasher filter, are the ones that matter most.

Zone 4, the upper cabinets, worked shelf by shelf. Steps: do the cabinet tops first, vacuuming the felt of dust and grease; empty and wipe each interior shelf front to back with a second pass at the rear dust band; degrease the underside nearest the hob; do the glass fronts, misting the cloth not the glass; detail the doors, hinges, and shelf-pin holes.

The reset-point reframe, frozen: "The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, which means it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. The sponge is the dirtiest object in the room and the dishwasher filter is why your glasses come out gritty, so in this one zone the things you never think to clean are exactly the things that matter most."

### Slide 9

**The same method, the heavy storage and the hand tools. The prize in a low cabinet is the shelf floor, where oil creeping down from pan bases holds dust into a tacky film a dry cloth just slides over.**

Zone 5, the lower cabinets and cookware, everything out. Steps: empty the cabinet fully; degrease the shelf floor and let it dwell; wipe the walls and back top to bottom; do the door interior and lid rail; wipe each pan, lid, and sheet as it goes back, twisting each handle to find a loose one; clear the runners and vacuum the toe-kick and plinth vent.

In a kitchen, that difference is the difference between a room that looks tidy and a room that is actually hygienic."

### Slide 10

**The fridge and freezer: cold storage that has to keep food safe. The parts that keep food safe, the gasket, the vents, and the coils, are exactly the ones a quick wipe always misses.**

Zone 7, the refrigerator and freezer, with its own dedicated cloth colour for the interior and the raw-meat shelf. Steps: do the top of the unit first, vacuuming the greasy felt of dust; wipe the exterior doors and handle, following the grain; wipe the interior shelves and walls, top to bottom, so any drip lands on a surface still to be cleaned; pull and wash the crisper drawers in the sink and clear the sticky ring under them; do the door bins and run a damp cloth around the gasket, pulling gently into each fold where black grime and mold gather; clear the rear vents and the freezer; finish at the condenser coils, clearing the dust mat that makes the motor run hot.

A gasket usually fails from grime long before age, and a filter that has never come out is why things come out gritty. That is why, in this room, the things you never think to clean are exactly the things that matter most.

### Slide 11

**While your hand is on every surface, your eye is inspecting it. When you find the fraying cord or the mold in the gasket, you flag it and keep cleaning. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it.**

Each zone's inspect-and-flag list is exactly the set of things your hands are already near: the scorch bloom behind the toaster, the fraying cord as you coil it, the hood filter so grease-soaked it drips, the burner that lights slow, the slow leak staining the board under the trap, the soft black mold in the dishwasher gasket, the loose pan handle, the slack fridge gasket, the dusty coils making the motor run hot. You note each and keep going.

Then run the five safety questions once. Burn or fire: never leave oil heating unattended, tea towels and the paper roll away from a live burner. Fall, cut, or crush: no knife blade-up in the rack or hidden under suds, pan handles turned in. Water and electricity: the kettle, the sockets, and a charging phone out of splash distance of the basin. Poison, choke, or strangle: never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner, and raw meat sealed on the lowest shelf. The plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool.
