### Slide 2 · This chapter, more than any other, is about the clean

- If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most. It is the busiest space in the house, the one the whole family passes through and works in every day, and, minute for minute, the one where a proper 6S pays back the fastest and the hardest. It is also, by a distance, the dirtiest, because everything that makes a kitchen a kitchen also makes a mess: heat bakes grease onto surfaces, food leaves residue in every corner, water and steam get everywhere, and it all happens several times a day. No other room rewards a real clean like this one, and no other room punishes a fake one so quickly.
- The kitchen is where the difference between tidy and clean is starkest. Anyone can clear a counter. A kitchen that is genuinely clean is one where the crumbs under the toaster are gone, the grease is off the top of the cabinets, the filter in the dishwasher has been rinsed, and the mold is out of the fridge gasket. That is the standard this chapter holds you to, because it is the difference between a room that looks clean and a room that is safe to cook in. You already have the method from the door, the nine rules of Shine; the kitchen adds one big new element, grease, which behaves differently from ordinary dirt and needs a different approach.

### Slide 4 · Seven zones, not one room

- The kitchen looks like the most daunting room in the house to deep clean, and if you face it as one job it is. Faced as its zones, it is a series of finishable pieces, and you can do them across a few sessions or knock out the worst two on a Saturday morning. As at the door, the trick is to stop seeing the room and see the zones. Each has a clear edge and a clear finish: you do not clean "the kitchen"; you clean the cooking zone, which is done when the hood, the grates, the splash wall and the hob are degreased and the crock is wiped. If you want the highest payback for the least effort, start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime.

### Slide 5 · The quick passes, then slow down for Shine

- Before the deep clean, four of the other five passes are quick, so we move through them briskly and then slow right down for Shine, which is where the real work is and, in the kitchen, the whole point. Sort each zone before you clean it, because a buried surface cannot be cleaned: clear the counter to a board-wide run, cap the frying pans at your busiest week plus one, keep one everyday set of dishes, and bin the leftovers you have moved forward three times. Straighten the survivors by use: oil and turner within one reach of the hob, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, one named job per drawer. Those two passes clear the ground so the clean can happen.
- Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short. Safety acts on what the cleaning turned up: anchor a tall unit, turn pan handles in, get the loose blade out of the drawer. Standardize locks it: a date label on every leftover, a name inside each drawer's front edge, the eat-first bin where the door opens onto it. And Sustain hangs the upkeep on what already happens: when the dishwasher starts, the counter clears; when the coffee brews, the sink gets checked; on the way to write the shopping list, the fridge gets a look. That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

### Slide 6 · How to Shine the kitchen: the nine rules, and the grease rule

- You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one of them still holds. The kitchen simply adds the hardest opponent in the house, grease, and grease does not behave like ordinary dirt. Every step-by-step procedure in this chapter is these nine rules applied to a particular zone, with the grease layer on top. Learn the method once here and you use it in all twenty room chapters.
- Grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward, so you soak the soakable parts before you touch anything else, work top to bottom so what you loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner only smears a tacky film around. That is the principle the whole clean turns on, and it is what makes a kitchen clean go quickly instead of turning into an afternoon of scrubbing.
- The nine rules, frozen, in order: "work top to bottom / dry or clear before wet / back to front and clean to dirty / mist the cloth not the surface / two cloths one to clean and one to dry / colour-code your cloths / match the cleaner to the surface / give it dwell time / clean to inspect and flag rather than fix." ### Slide 7 · The kitchen Shine method and the kit

- Three things follow from the grease rule, and they are what make a kitchen clean go quickly. The first is soaking, the single biggest time-saver in the room: the most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time, so set the grates and the hood filter to soak before you touch anything else and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time. The second is the right input for the job: your neutral pH multi-surface cleaner handles everyday food residue, but where grease has built up it will just smear a tacky film around, so the moment a surface feels tacky rather than dirty, switch to the food-area degreaser and follow with a clean damp pass. The third is lifting, not wiping around. And one thing particular to the kitchen: food safety, so keep a dedicated cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf.
- The kit, by type. Cleaners: a food-area degreaser, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, and a streak-free glass cleaner for glass fronts. Cloths: a colour-coded microfiber set with one colour flagged for the fridge and raw-meat shelf. Tools: a small detail brush set, a compact vacuum with a brush attachment, and a portable caddy to carry it all. Types, not brands. Buy for the job.
- The soak-and-dwell reframe, verbatim: "The most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time. Set the burner grates and the hood filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touch anything else, and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time. The chemistry lifts baked grease while you work elsewhere, so you scrub a fraction of what you otherwise would."

### Slide 8 · Zone by zone: the prep counter, and the cooking zone

- Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Gather the inputs into your caddy first, and where a zone needs soaking, that step comes first so the soak works while you do the rest.
- Zone 1, the prep counter. Inputs: neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, food-area degreaser, a colour-coded set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth), the caddy. Steps: clear the counter completely, including the kettle, toaster, and knife block; do the backsplash first, top to bottom, then drag the cloth the full length of the counter-to-backsplash seam to lift the grease line; clean the knife block or strip and dry it at once; lift the toaster and kettle and clean under them, pulling the crumb tray, coiling the cords; clean the open run, switching to the degreaser where the surface is tacky and following with a clean damp pass; finish with the board and the front lip and underside overhang.
- Zone 2, the cooking zone, the grease-heavy heart, so you soak first and work from the top down. Inputs: food-area degreaser, multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set (surface, dry), a small detail brush set, hot water and dish soap for soaking, the caddy. Steps: soak the hood's metal grease filter and the burner grates and caps in hot degreasing water first; degrease the hood, top down; degrease the splash wall, top to bottom and back to front, so the loosened grease runs toward the hob; clean the hob and burner wells with the grates off, running a detail brush around the knob stems; scrub and dry the soaked grates and refit the rinsed filter; do the oven door front and glass, and the control panel with a barely damp cloth so nothing runs in behind the knobs; wipe the crock, the pot-holder hook, and the greasy spice fronts; finish with the hot-pan landing space so it is never slick.

### Slide 9 · Zone by zone: the sink, and the upper cabinets

- Zone 3, the sink and dishwashing zone, the washing point the whole kitchen resets from, so it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. Inputs: multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set (surface, dry), a small detail brush set, the caddy. Steps: clean the tap, base, and aerator, pushing a detail brush around the base collar; clean the basin, drain flange, and overflow slot, which grows its own slime unseen; wipe the dispensers, the bottles, and the sealant bead around the rim; deal with the sponge, brush, and drying rack, retiring the sponge the day it smells while dry; clean the dishwasher gasket, pulling gently into its folds, and twist the filter out and rinse the mesh; clear under the sink and dry the base. The two things you never think to clean, the sponge and the dishwasher filter, are the ones that matter most.
- Zone 4, the upper cabinets, eye-level storage worked shelf by shelf. Inputs: multi-surface cleaner, streak-free glass cleaner for glass fronts, food-area degreaser for the greasy underside nearest the hob, a colour-coded set (surface, glass, dry), a detail brush set, a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, the caddy. Steps: do the cabinet tops first, vacuuming the felt of dust and grease that gathers where no one looks, then wiping them; empty and wipe each interior shelf front to back, with a second pass at the rear dust band; degrease the underside nearest the hob; do the glass fronts, misting the cloth not the glass; detail the doors, hinges, and shelf-pin holes.

The sponge is the dirtiest object in the room and the dishwasher filter is why your glasses come out gritty, so in this one zone the things you never think to clean are exactly the things that matter most."

### Slide 10 · Zone by zone: the lower cabinets and cookware, and the drawers

- Zone 5, the lower cabinets and cookware, deep low storage where everything comes out, and the prize is the shelf floor itself, where oil creeping down from pan bases holds dust into a tacky film a dry cloth just slides over. Inputs: food-area degreaser, multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set (surface, dry), a detail brush set, a compact vacuum with the crevice tool, the caddy. Steps: empty the cabinet fully onto the floor or counter; degrease the shelf floor and let it dwell so it can cut the tacky film; wipe the side walls and back top to bottom, into the back corners; do the door interior and lid rail; wipe each pan, lid, and sheet as it goes back, twisting each handle so a loose one makes itself known; clear the runners and vacuum the toe-kick and plinth vent at the very base.
- Zone 6, the utensil and utility drawers, the shallow drawers of hand tools and wraps, where the crumbs that jam a drawer are not in the drawer but underneath the divider and down in the runner track. Inputs: multi-surface cleaner, food-area degreaser for a drawer near the hob, a colour-coded set (surface, dry), a detail brush set, the caddy. Steps: lift the whole divided insert right out and tip the grit into the bin, wiping each blade and peeler before it goes back; wipe the exposed drawer base into the corners; clean both runner tracks and clear the grit packed against the rollers; do the inner front and the label edge, checking it closes flush with one push; wipe the foil, cling film, and baking-paper boxes and stand them back on end.

### Slide 11 · Zone by zone: the fridge and freezer

- Zone 7, the refrigerator and freezer, cold storage that has to keep food safe and show you what you own before you shop. The parts that keep food safe, the gasket, the vents, and the coils, are exactly the ones a quick wipe always misses, so this zone earns its own dedicated cloth colour for the interior and the raw-meat shelf. Inputs: multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set with a dedicated colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, warm water and dish soap for washing the crisper drawers in the sink, the caddy.
- Steps, top down and back to the coils: do the top of the unit first, vacuuming the greasy felt of dust and wiping it; wipe the exterior doors and handle, following the grain on brushed steel; wipe the interior shelves and walls, top to bottom, so any drip lands on a surface still to be cleaned; pull the crisper drawers fully out and wash them in the sink, clearing the sticky ring in the well underneath; do the door bins, and run a damp cloth all the way around the door gasket, pulling gently into each fold where black grime and mold gather, because a gasket usually fails from grime long before age; clear the rear vents and the freezer so the compartment can hold temperature; finish at the condenser coils, behind or beneath the unit, clearing the dust mat that makes the motor run hot and work harder than it needs to.
- The book line, verbatim: "The kitchen simply adds the hardest opponent in the house, grease, and grease does not behave like ordinary dirt." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Give the degreaser its dwell time instead of scrubbing: spray it, leave it the time on the label, and it lifts the grease for you. Keep a separate cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, and never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the sink, because together they make a gas that will drive you out of the room."

### Slide 12 · Clean to inspect, and flag; the safety pass fixes

- Cleaning is how you inspect. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the thing you never normally see, a fraying cord, a grease-soaked filter, a slow leak, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Each zone's inspect-and-flag list is exactly the set of things your hands are already near: the scorch bloom behind the toaster, the fraying cord as you coil it, the hood filter so grease-soaked it drips, the burner that lights slow, the board darkening under the trap, the soft black mold in the dishwasher gasket, the loose pan handle, the slack fridge gasket, the dusty coils making the motor run hot.
- Then, after the shine, run the five safety questions once directly, because the kitchen holds more real hazards than any other room. Burn or fire: never leave oil heating unattended, and keep tea towels and the paper-towel roll away from a live burner. Fall, cut, or crush: never leave a knife blade-up in the rack or under suds, turn pan handles in over the hob, store blades sheathed rather than loose. Water and electricity: keep the kettle, the sockets, and a charging phone out of splash distance of the basin. Poison, choke, or strangle: never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner, and seal raw meat on the lowest shelf so its juices cannot drip onto ready-to-eat food. And the plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool, never a chair or a counter edge.
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Wiping around the appliances and the clutter instead of lifting and emptying, so the crumbs under the toaster, the dust felt on the cabinet tops, the oil film on the shelf floor, and the mold in the fridge gasket all stay. A kitchen cleaned only where you can see is tidy, not hygienic. Lift, empty, and pull the cloth into the fold."

### Slide 13 · The calls that matter here

- Cleaning to a method will make the kitchen shine, but a few genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and a playbook earns its keep by naming them. The first is the appliance you love but rarely use, the stand mixer, the bread machine, the espresso setup: count real uses over the past month, not the ones you intended; anything you reach for less than a couple of times a week comes off the counter, and anything you have not switched on in a year leaves the kitchen. The second is the spice rack you are not honest about: open the jars and smell them over the bin, and keep spices for the food you actually cooked in the last year, not the food you imagine cooking.
- The third is whether the sink is allowed to be a staging area, and it is settled with a deadline rather than a ban: a pan may soak, but only until the current dishwasher cycle ends or until you next leave the room. And the last call is the container of good leftovers you have moved to the front three times: name the meal, the day, and who eats it, and if you cannot, it is not food you are storing, it is the guilt of having cooked too much, so bin it today and cook a little less next time.
- The reset-point reframe, frozen: "The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, which means it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. The sponge is the dirtiest object in the room and the dishwasher filter is why your glasses come out gritty, so in this one zone the things you never think to clean are exactly the things that matter most." The Family Challenge, verbatim: "Give each person a zone to own on reset day, and put the fridge eat-first bin right where the door opens onto it, so the answer to what is for dinner comes from the bin rather than the cupboard, and far less food is thrown away."
