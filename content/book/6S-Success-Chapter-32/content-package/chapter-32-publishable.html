Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks

Chapter Thirty-Two

The Kitchen

The kitchen is the busiest room in the house and the one 6S pays back fastest, because it is worked hardest and gets dirtiest: grease, food, spills, and heat. This playbook points the whole loop at it one zone at a time, and gives you, for each of the seven zones, the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it to a professional standard, degreasing and all, so the room that feeds you is genuinely, inspectably clean.

If the entryway was the room to start with, the kitchen is the room that matters most. It is the busiest space in the house, the one the whole family passes through and works in every day, and, minute for minute, the one where a proper 6S pays back the fastest and the hardest. It is also, by a distance, the dirtiest, because everything that makes a kitchen a kitchen also makes a mess: heat bakes grease onto surfaces, food leaves residue in every corner, water and steam get everywhere, and it all happens several times a day. No other room rewards a real clean like this one, and no other room punishes a fake one so quickly.

That is why this chapter, more than any other, is about the clean. You already have the method from the door: the nine rules of Shine, and the habit of inspecting every surface your hand passes over. The kitchen takes all of that and adds one big new element, grease, which behaves differently from ordinary dirt and needs a different approach. So we will spend most of this chapter on exactly how to clean each of the seven kitchen zones, with the precise inputs to gather and the numbered steps in the order that actually works, degreasing and soaking and all.

A word before we start, because the kitchen is where the difference between tidy and clean is starkest. Anyone can clear a counter. A kitchen that is genuinely clean is one where the crumbs under the toaster are gone, the grease is off the top of the cabinets, the filter in the dishwasher has been rinsed, and the mold is out of the fridge gasket. That is the standard this chapter holds you to, and it is worth it, because it is the difference between a room that looks clean and a room that is safe to cook in.

What You Will Learn

  • Break the kitchen into its seven small zones and run them one at a time.
  • Gather the exact inputs, including the degreaser and brushes, each kitchen zone needs.
  • Clean and shine every zone with a precise, professional method, soaking the grease first.
  • Handle grease, dwell time, and food-safety cloth discipline the way a professional does.
  • Inspect each surface as you clean it, flag what needs fixing, and hold the room with a before-and-after pair.

The principleSeven Zones, Not One Room

The kitchen looks like the most daunting room in the house to deep clean, and if you face it as one job it is. Faced as its zones, it is a series of finishable pieces, and you can do them across a few sessions or knock out the worst two on a Saturday morning. As at the door, the trick is to stop seeing the room and see the zones.

The kitchen is seven small zones, not one big job: the prep counter, the cooking zone, the sink, the upper cabinets, the lower cabinets and cookware, the utensil and utility drawers, and the fridge and freezer. Do them one at a time and the busiest room in the house comes back to you a zone at a time.

Each of those has a clear edge and a clear finish. You do not clean "the kitchen"; you clean the cooking zone, which is done when the hood, the grates, the splash wall and the hob are degreased and the crock is wiped. If you want the highest payback for the least effort, start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime and give the biggest visible and hygienic win. Here is the standard the whole room is working toward.

A finished kitchen

A finished kitchen is one you could start cooking in with your eyes shut: a clear run of prep counter, oil and your turner within one reach of a clean hob, an empty dry sink the room resets from, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, drawers that close on one push, and a fridge where the eat-first bin sits right where the door opens onto it.

The quick passesThe Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

As at the door, four of the passes are quick, so we move through them and then slow right down for Shine. Sort each zone before you clean it, because a buried surface cannot be cleaned: clear the counter to a board-wide run, cap the frying pans at your busiest week plus one, keep one everyday set of dishes, and bin the leftovers you have moved forward three times. Straighten the survivors by use: oil and turner within one reach of the hob, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, one named job per drawer.

Safety, Standardize and Sustain come after the shine and each is short. Safety is where you act on what the cleaning turned up: anchor a tall unit, turn pan handles in, get the loose blade out of the drawer. Standardize locks it: a date label on every leftover, a name inside each drawer's front edge, the eat-first bin where the door opens onto it. And Sustain hangs the upkeep on what already happens: when the dishwasher starts, the counter clears; when the coffee brews, the sink gets checked; on the way to write the shopping list, the fridge gets a look. Now the heart of it.

The methodHow to Shine the Kitchen: The Method

You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door: work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix. Every one of those still holds. The kitchen simply adds the hardest opponent in the house, grease, and grease does not behave like ordinary dirt.

Grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward. Set the soakable parts to soak before you do anything else, work top to bottom so what you loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner only smears a tacky film around.

Three things follow from that, and they are what make a kitchen clean go quickly instead of turning into an afternoon of scrubbing.

The first is soaking, which is the single biggest time-saver in the room.

The most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time. Set the burner grates and the hood filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touch anything else, and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time. The chemistry lifts baked grease while you work elsewhere, so you scrub a fraction of what you otherwise would.

The second is the right input for the job. Your neutral pH multi-surface cleaner is for everyday food residue, and it is all most surfaces need. But where grease has built up, on the hood, the splash wall, the shelf floor of a low cabinet, it will just smear that grease into a tacky film. The moment a surface feels tacky rather than dirty, switch to the food-area degreaser, which is formulated to cut grease, and follow it with a clean damp pass so no residue is left where food will touch.

The third is lifting, not wiping around.

The crumbs and grease you never see are the ones living under the toaster's feet, on top of the cabinets, and in the fold of the fridge gasket. A wipe around the clutter is not a clean; lifting the appliance, emptying the shelf, and pulling the cloth into the fold is. In a kitchen, that difference is the difference between a room that looks tidy and a room that is actually hygienic.

One more thing, particular to the kitchen: food safety. Because this room handles raw food, keep a dedicated cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf so you never carry that onto a prep surface, treat the sink sponge as the dirtiest object in the room, and never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the basin, because the two together release a gas that will drive you out of the room. With that, here is the kit, and then the zones.

The centrepieceZone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag on the way. Gather the inputs into your caddy first. Where a zone needs soaking, that step comes first so the soak works while you do the rest.

Zone 1 · The Primary Prep Counter

The stretch of counter where food is cut, mixed, and plated. The crumbs you never see are the ones living under the appliance feet, so this zone earns a real wipe, not a wave around the clutter.

Inputs

  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Food-area degreaser
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth)
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Clear the counter completely. Take everything off, including the kettle, the toaster, and the knife block, so you are cleaning a bare surface and not around obstacles.
  2. Do the backsplash first, top to bottom. Spray the backsplash with multi-surface cleaner and wipe downward, so anything you loosen falls onto the counter you have not cleaned yet, then lay the cloth into the counter-to-backsplash seam and drag it the full length to lift the grease line that collects there.
  3. Clean the knife block or strip. Tip a block upside down over the bin to shake the crumbs out of the slots, wipe the block or the magnetic strip with a barely damp cloth, and dry it at once so no moisture sits against the steel.
  4. Lift the toaster and kettle and clean under them. Pull the toaster's crumb tray out, shake it over the bin, and wipe it dry. Wipe each appliance's cool exterior with a damp cloth, keeping water off the plug and element, coil the cord, and wipe the bare stretch of counter where the feet stood before you set it back.
  5. Clean the open run of counter. For everyday residue, use the multi-surface cleaner on the cloth. Where oil or a greasy splatter has built up and the surface feels tacky, switch to the degreaser, let it sit its dwell time, then wipe and follow with a clean damp cloth so no film is left where food will touch.
  6. Finish with the board and the edges. Wash the cutting board on both faces and stand it on edge to dry, then run the cloth along the front lip and the underside overhang of the counter, where hands grip and splashes dry into a band you only see from below.

Inspect and flag as you go: a scorch mark or heat bloom on the counter or wall behind the toaster or kettle; a cracked, brittle, or fraying cord as you lift and coil an appliance; the sealant bead along the counter-to-backsplash seam lifting, crumbling, or gone dark; a soft, swollen, or lifting patch in the counter near that seam; a loose rivet or wobbling handle on the knife, or a hairline split in the cutting board. Flag each and keep cleaning.

Zone 2 · The Cooking Zone

The space either side of the burners, and the hood above. Grease is the whole job here, and it hides upward, so you soak first and work from the top down.

Inputs

  • Food-area degreaser
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth)
  • A small detail brush set
  • Hot water and dish soap, for soaking, in the sink or a basin
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Soak the soakable parts first. Lift out the hood's metal grease filter and the burner grates and caps, and set them to soak in hot water with degreasing dish soap. Leave them while you do everything else; the soak does what scrubbing cannot.
  2. Degrease the hood, top down. Spray the underside and face of the hood with degreaser, give it the label's dwell time, wipe it down with a cloth, and finish with a clean damp pass.
  3. Degrease the splash wall, top to bottom and back to front. Spray it, let it sit, then wipe from the top down so the loosened grease runs toward the hob and not onto clean wall. Give any baked-hard spatter a second pass.
  4. Clean the hob and burner wells. With the grates off, degrease the exposed hob and work the burner wells where boil-overs bake into a ring. Pull the knobs off if they lift, wash them, and run a detail brush around the knob stems where sticky grime collars them.
  5. Scrub and dry the soaked grates. Come back to the grates and caps, scrub the softened baked rings with a detail brush, rinse them, and dry them fully before they go back so they do not rust onto the hob. Rinse and dry the hood filter and refit it.
  6. Do the oven door front and the panel. Wipe the oven door front and its glass window with a multi-surface cloth, then a clean damp buff so no film clouds the glass you check a roast through. Wipe the handle, and clean the control panel and dials with a barely damp cloth, not a wet one, so nothing runs in behind the knobs.
  7. Wipe the crock, the hook, and the spice fronts. Empty the utensil crock, wash and dry it, and wipe each tool before it goes back. Wipe the pot-holder hook and the fronts and shoulders of the spice jars, since airborne grease settles into a tacky coat on everything standing near the burners.
  8. Finish with the landing space. Degrease, wipe, and dry the stretch of counter you set hot pans down on, so the one surface a hot pan lands on is never slick.

Inspect and flag as you go: a hood filter so grease-soaked it stays soft and drips, and a fan behind it that sounds strained or barely moves air; a burner that lights slow or burns yellow and sooty, or a grate that rocks on its seat; scorching, discolouring, or bubbling paint on the splash wall or hood underside; a chip, crack, or spidered line in the hob surface, found once the baked ring is cleared; a frayed or heat-stiffened cord on any appliance on the burner side. Flag each.

Zone 3 · The Sink and Dishwashing Zone

The washing point the whole kitchen resets from, so it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. The two things you never think to clean, the sponge and the dishwasher filter, are the ones that matter most.

Inputs

  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth)
  • A small detail brush set
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Clean the tap, base, and aerator. Wipe the tap and handle top to bottom, then push a detail brush around the base collar where limescale and grime ring it. If the flow has slowed, brush the aerator tip, where scale builds.
  2. Clean the basin, drain, and overflow. Clean the whole basin with multi-surface cleaner, then scrub the drain flange and its underside lip with a detail brush where a dark film grips, and run the brush into the overflow slot, which grows its own slime unseen.
  3. Wipe the dispensers and the rim. Lift the soap pump and the dish-soap bottle, wipe the tacky ring of dried soap under each, wipe the bottles and the surround they stand on, and run a damp cloth along the sealant bead around the sink rim.
  4. Deal with the sponge, brush, and rack. Wring the sponge out hard and stand it on edge where it drains, and retire it the day it smells while dry. Stand the brush bristles up. Empty the drying rack and wash its tray and the channel that carries water back to the sink.
  5. Clean the dishwasher gasket and filter. Wipe the rubber door gasket all the way around, pulling gently into its folds where black specks hide. Then twist the filter out of the floor of the machine and rinse it under the tap to clear the mesh, because a filter that has never come out is why things come out gritty.
  6. Clear under the sink. Take the cleaning tray out and wipe the cabinet floor into the back corners, wipe the trap and the visible pipes, and dry the base before the tray goes back so nothing sits on a damp board.

Inspect and flag as you go: a damp patch, water stain, or warped, darkening board on the cabinet floor under the trap, the quiet sign of a slow leak; soft black mold in the folds of the dishwasher gasket or the sealant around the sink rim; rust, a green crust, or a bead of moisture on a pipe joint or compression fitting; a sponge or brush that smells while dry; swelling or dark staining along the front edge of the cabinet base. Flag each.

Zone 4 · The Upper Cabinets

Eye-level storage for the dishes you use every day. Take each shelf completely empty before the cloth touches it, because the band of dust at the back is how you learn how long it has really been.

Inputs

  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Streak-free glass and mirror cleaner (for glass fronts)
  • Food-area degreaser (for the greasy underside nearest the hob)
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a glass cloth, a dry cloth)
  • A small detail brush set
  • A compact vacuum with the brush attachment
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Do the cabinet tops first. Vacuum the flat tops of the cabinets with the brush attachment to lift the felt of dust and grease that gathers where no one looks, then wipe them with a multi-surface cloth.
  2. Empty and wipe each interior shelf. Take a shelf completely empty, wipe front to back, and give the rear lip and back wall a second pass where the dust band sits. Let the shelf dry before the stacks return. Do the top shelf, the once-a-month storage, the same way.
  3. Degrease the underside nearest the hob. Look up at it from an angle to find the greasy film, wipe it with the degreaser, and finish with a clean damp pass so nothing tacky is left overhead.
  4. Do the glass fronts. Mist the glass cleaner onto the glass cloth, not the glass, wipe, and buff dry so no lamplight streak shows. Do the inside face too if the door is glazed.
  5. Detail the doors and hinges. Wipe the door fronts and handles where finger grease darkens the timber, then run a detail brush around the hinges and into the shelf-pin holes and seams where dust cakes into corners a cloth skips.

Inspect and flag as you go: a shelf that flexes or bows under its stack, or a shelf pin sitting loose in its hole; a hinge screw pulling out of the cabinet side, or a door that no longer sits square; a water stain or ring on a shelf under a rarely moved item, hinting at a leak above; a greasy film that keeps returning fast, a sign the extractor is not clearing the air; a soft, swollen patch on the cabinet bottom above the kettle, where steam has been rising into it. Flag each.

Zone 5 · The Lower Cabinets and Cookware

Deep, low storage for the heavy pieces. Everything comes out for this one, and the prize is the shelf floor itself, where oil creeping down from pan bases holds dust into a tacky film a dry cloth just slides over.

Inputs

  • Food-area degreaser
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth)
  • A small detail brush set
  • A compact vacuum with the crevice tool
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Empty the cabinet fully. Take every pan, lid, and sheet out onto the floor or the counter, so you can see and reach every surface.
  2. Degrease the shelf floor. Attack the shelf floor with the degreaser or a strong multi-surface pass, let it sit its dwell time so it can cut the tacky film, then wipe and follow with a clean damp cloth. A dry cloth alone will only skate across it.
  3. Wipe the walls and back, top to bottom. Wipe the side walls and back panel so grit falls to the floor you will do last, reaching right into the back corners where a dropped lid or dust drift hides.
  4. Do the door interior and lid rail. Wipe the inside of the door and the lid rack or rail mounted on it, since splashes carry onto the door every time a pan goes in wet, and dry the rail so lids do not sit against a damp edge.
  5. Wipe each piece as it goes back. While everything is out, wipe each pan, lid, and sheet, stand baking sheets on end, and twist each pan handle as you wipe it, so grease comes off the grip and rivets and a loose one makes itself known under your hand.
  6. Clear the runners and the toe-kick. Wipe the runners of any pull-out shelf and clear grit from the track with a detail brush so it glides, then vacuum the toe-kick and the plinth vent at the very base of the units, where floor dust and crumbs pack in.

Inspect and flag as you go: a loose rivet, wobbling handle, or cracked helper handle on a pan as you twist it; the tacky oil film returning to the shelf floor faster than it should, telling you a pan is going away greasy; a damp patch or water stain on the cabinet floor along the plumbing wall; rust or orange bloom on cast iron, a carbon-steel pan, or a bare baking sheet; a pull-out runner that binds or drops, or a mounting bracket working loose. Flag each.

Zone 6 · The Utensil and Utility Drawers

The shallow drawers of hand tools and wraps. The crumbs that jam a drawer are not in the drawer, they are underneath the divider and down in the runner track, so you lift the insert right out.

Inputs

  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Food-area degreaser (for a drawer near the hob)
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth)
  • A small detail brush set
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Lift the insert out and tip the grit. Take the whole divided insert out and tip the grit into the bin. Wash or wipe the insert compartments, and wipe each blade, spatula, and peeler before it goes back so nothing goes home sticky.
  2. Wipe the drawer base. With the insert out, wipe the exposed base into the corners where flour, seeds, and crumbs settle. If grease has built up on a drawer near the hob, use the degreaser here and finish with a clean damp pass.
  3. Clean the runner tracks. Wipe both tracks along their length and clear any grit packed against the rollers with a detail brush, since that grit is what makes a drawer grind and stick. Wipe them dry rather than leaving them damp.
  4. Do the front and the label edge. Wipe the inner front edge where the drawer's name is written so the label stays readable, then clean the outer front and the handle where finger grease gathers, and check it closes flush with one push before you refill it.
  5. Wipe the wrap boxes. Wipe the tops and sides of the foil, cling film, and baking-paper boxes, which pick up a greasy dust in a kitchen drawer, and stand them back on end so you can read them from above.

Inspect and flag as you go: a runner that binds, drops, or grinds even once cleared, or a runner bracket screw backing out; a drawer front that no longer closes flush or sits racked, a sign the box is going out of square; rust or pitting starting on a peeler edge, a paring knife, or a mandoline blade; crumbs or grease gone tacky under the insert, pointing to a spill from the surface above; a cracked or splitting plastic insert leaving a sharp edge where fingers rummage. Flag each.

Zone 7 · The Refrigerator and Freezer

Cold storage that has to keep food safe and show you what you own before you shop. The parts that keep food safe, the gasket, the vents, and the coils, are exactly the ones a quick wipe always misses.

Inputs

  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • A colour-coded microfiber cloth set, with a dedicated colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf
  • A compact vacuum with the brush attachment
  • Warm water and dish soap, for washing the crisper drawers in the sink
  • Your portable caddy

Steps

  1. Do the top of the unit first. Vacuum the dust off the top with the brush attachment, then wipe it, since the warm air rising off the back turns that dust into a greasy felt.
  2. Wipe the exterior doors and handle. Follow the grain on brushed steel so no swirl shows, keep any abrasive off a stainless or painted front, and give the handle a second pass where hands pull it dozens of times a day.
  3. Wipe the interior shelves and walls, top to bottom. Take out and wipe the glass or wire shelves so any drip lands on a surface still to be cleaned, wipe the back and side walls, and dry glass shelves so they do not streak.
  4. Pull and wash the crisper drawers. Take the crisper drawers fully out and wash them in the sink with warm soapy water, then dry them. Wipe the runners and the well they sat in, clearing the sticky ring underneath where old produce has wept and dried.
  5. Do the door bins, seals, and gasket. Empty and wash the door bins and wipe the door liner, then run a damp cloth all the way around the door gasket, pulling gently into each fold where black grime and mold gather, because a gasket usually fails from grime long before age.
  6. Clear the vents and the freezer. Wipe the rear vents and back wall clear of anything packed against them so the compartment can hold temperature. In the freezer, wipe the interior walls and door seal and clear any loose frost so you can see the wall behind it.
  7. Vacuum the condenser coils. Finish at the coils, behind or beneath the unit, clearing the dust mat with the brush attachment, because that mat makes the motor run hot and work harder than it needs to.

Inspect and flag as you go: soft black mold seated deep in the folds of the door gasket; water pooling under a crisper drawer or in the base of the compartment, the sign of a blocked defrost drain; a gasket gone slack, torn, or no longer springing back; thick frost building on the freezer wall, telling you the door is not sealing; a shelf thermometer reading warm, or a motor that runs long and hot once you reach the dusty coils. Flag each.

Quick Win

Do the cooking zone's soak-first trick. Before anything else, lift the burner grates and caps and the hood filter into hot degreasing water and leave them. While they soften on their own, degrease the splash wall and the hood from the top down. Come back, scrub the loosened grates in a fraction of the time, dry them fully, and the dirtiest corner of the kitchen is done.

6S Tip

Give the degreaser its dwell time instead of scrubbing: spray it, leave it the time on the label, and it lifts the grease for you. Keep a separate cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, and never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the sink, because together they make a gas that will drive you out of the room.

The hard callsThe Calls That Matter Here

Cleaning to a method makes the kitchen shine, but a few genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean. The kitchen has several, and here are the ones people dodge longest.

The first is the appliance you love but rarely use: the stand mixer, the bread machine, the espresso setup. Count real uses over the past month, not the ones you intended. Anything you reach for less than a couple of times a week comes off the counter and into a low cabinet; anything you have not switched on in a year leaves the kitchen. And if the honest reason it lives on the counter is that lifting it from a cabinet would stop you using it, you have just proved it is not part of your cooking.

The second is the spice rack you are not honest about. Open the jars and smell them, one by one, over the bin. If a jar smells of dust rather than of itself, it is no longer seasoning anything, and keeping it does not recover what you paid. Then make the harder call: keep spices for the food you actually cooked in the last year, not the food you imagine cooking.

The third is whether the sink is allowed to be a staging area.

The sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, which means it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed. The sponge is the dirtiest object in the room and the dishwasher filter is why your glasses come out gritty, so in this one zone the things you never think to clean are exactly the things that matter most.

Settle soaking with a deadline rather than a ban: a pan may soak, but only until the current dishwasher cycle ends or until you next leave the room, whichever comes first. And the last call is the container of good leftovers you have moved to the front three times. Name the meal, the day, and who eats it. If you cannot, it is not food you are storing, it is the guilt of having cooked too much, so bin it today and cook a little less next time.

Family Challenge

Give each person a zone to own on reset day, and put the fridge eat-first bin right where the door opens onto it, so the answer to what is for dinner comes from the bin rather than the cupboard, and far less food is thrown away.

The hazardsWatch for in the Kitchen

The kitchen holds more real hazards than any other room, and cleaning to inspect will surface most of them, but run the five safety questions once directly. For burn or fire, never leave oil heating unattended, and keep tea towels and the paper-towel roll away from a live burner; a toaster shoved under a low cabinet is heat with nowhere to go. For fall, cut, or crush, never leave a knife blade-up in the drying rack or hidden under suds in a full sink, turn pan handles in over the hob, keep glassware off shelves above head height, and store blades sheathed rather than loose among the utensils. For water and electricity, keep the kettle, the sockets, and a charging phone out of splash distance of the basin. For poison, choke, or strangle, never mix bleach with an acidic or ammonia cleaner, keep loose button batteries out of the utility drawer, and seal raw meat on the lowest shelf so its juices cannot drip onto ready-to-eat food. And for the plain fall, reach the top shelf from a stable step stool, never a chair, a counter edge, or an open drawer.

Common Mistake

Wiping around the appliances and the clutter instead of lifting and emptying, so the crumbs under the toaster, the dust felt on the cabinet tops, the oil film on the shelf floor, and the mold in the fridge gasket all stay. A kitchen cleaned only where you can see is tidy, not hygienic. Lift, empty, and pull the cloth into the fold.

The ritualBefore and After

When the seven zones are done and cleaned to the method, stand at the doorway and take the second photograph.

This is the kitchen the whole loop was for: the same room you started with, cleared, degreased, and genuinely clean, from the hood down to the toe-kick. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard, because a kitchen this clean is one you will actually want to cook in, and the photo is what it has to match from now on.

Shoot it as a matched pair from the room's entrance, framing the run of counter, the hob and hood, and the sink together, because that is the working heart and where the most change shows. The before is the valuable one, the proof of how far the room came, and the after is the ideal-state standard, the picture the kitchen has to match. That is the kitchen: seven zones, each sorted, homed, and cleaned to a professional method, the grease soaked off and the appliances lifted, its hazards flagged and fixed. It is the biggest single win in the house, and now it is done.

One Idea to Keep

A kitchen is not clean because the counters are clear. It is clean because you lifted the appliances, soaked the grease, pulled the cloth into the gasket, and cleared the filter, cleaning to a method zone by zone and inspecting as you went. Do one zone this weekend, the sink or the cooking zone, and cook in a room that is finally clean underneath.
