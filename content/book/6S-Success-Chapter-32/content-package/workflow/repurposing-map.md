# Chapter 32 · Repurposing Map

How the one chapter fans out into every channel, and which idea leads on each. This chapter carries the instruction-centric room-playbook format proven at the entryway in Chapter 31, so the spine leads with the how-to-clean value: the exact inputs and the exact, ordered steps to clean and shine each of the kitchen's seven zones to a professional standard, grease and all.

## The spine (reused across channels)
- The how-to-clean value, first (the whole point of the format): a playbook does not stop at telling you what to do, it gives you, for every small zone of the room, the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, done the way a professional would rather than the way most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better; that distinction is the whole game, because a tidy room and a genuinely clean room are not the same thing, and the difference is method, the order you work in, the cloth you use, the cleaner you match to the surface, and, in the kitchen, whether you soaked the grease and lifted the appliance rather than wiping around it
- The universal nine-rule Shine method (carried from Ch 31, stated once, governs every zone): work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix; you already know it from the door, so the kitchen does not re-teach it
- The kitchen grease layer (frozen grease rule, the new material this chapter adds): grease is the whole job in a kitchen, and grease hides upward, so set the soakable parts to soak before you do anything else, work top to bottom so what you loosen falls onto what you have not cleaned yet, and reach for the degreaser the moment your everyday cleaner only smears a tacky film around; three moves follow, soaking (the single biggest time-saver in the room), the right input for the job (the food-area degreaser the moment a surface feels tacky rather than dirty), and lifting rather than wiping around; plus food safety, a dedicated cloth colour for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf, the sponge treated as the dirtiest object in the room, and never bleach mixed with an acidic or ammonia cleaner in the basin
- The centerpiece, Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It: each zone gets the same three things, the exact INPUTS to gather into the caddy before you start, the NUMBERED steps in the true order of work (soak first where the zone needs it, then clear, dust or degrease high and dry, glass, surfaces, details, floor and toe-kick last), and an INSPECT-AND-FLAG list, because Shine finds the hazard and the safety pass fixes it; the seven zones and their signature jobs, Zone 1 the primary prep counter (lift the toaster and kettle because the crumbs live under the appliance feet; the counter-to-backsplash seam), Zone 2 the cooking zone (grease is the whole job; soak the hood filter and burner grates and caps first, then degrease the hood and splash wall top-down), Zone 3 the sink and dishwashing zone (the point the whole kitchen resets from; the sponge and the dishwasher filter are the ones that matter most), Zone 4 the upper cabinets (the cabinet tops furred with greasy dust, and the dust band at the back that tells you how long it has really been), Zone 5 the lower cabinets and cookware (everything comes out; the prize is the shelf floor and its tacky oil film a dry cloth just skates over), Zone 6 the utensil and utility drawers (lift the insert and tip the grit, because the crumbs that jam a drawer live under the divider and in the runner track), Zone 7 the refrigerator and freezer (the gasket folds, the vents, and the condenser coils a quick wipe always misses)
- Clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix (the oldest idea in the book, from the very first Shine chapter): your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too, and when you find the scorch mark behind the toaster, the grease-soaked hood filter, the slack fridge gasket, or the motor running hot at the dusty coils, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning, Shine finds it, the safety pass fixes it, so do not stop your clean to chase a repair
- A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name (the freeing rule carried from Ch 31): you do not 6S a whole kitchen, you 6S one of its seven zones, then the next, until the room is done, and if you want the biggest win for the least effort you start with the cooking zone or the sink, because those two carry the most grease and the most grime
- The seven zones (frozen, structural): the kitchen is seven small zones, not one big job, the prep counter, the cooking zone, the sink, the upper cabinets, the lower cabinets and cookware, the utensil and utility drawers, and the fridge and freezer, do them one at a time and the busiest room in the house comes back to you a zone at a time
- The quick passes, moved through briskly before the deep clean: Sort each zone first because a buried surface cannot be cleaned (clear the counter to a board-wide run, cap the pans at your busiest week plus one, keep one everyday set of dishes, bin the leftovers you have moved forward three times); Straighten survivors into a home chosen by use (oil and turner within one reach of the hob, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, one named job per drawer); Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine and are each short
- The definition of a finished kitchen (frozen definition box): one you could start cooking in with your eyes shut, a clear run of prep counter, oil and your turner within one reach of a clean hob, an empty dry sink the room resets from, daily dishes between shoulder and eye height, pans that come out in one motion, drawers that close on one push, and a fridge where the eat-first bin sits right where the door opens onto it
- The four calls that matter here: the appliance you love but rarely use (count real uses over the past month; under a couple of times a week comes off the counter, not switched on in a year leaves the kitchen), the spice rack you are not honest about (smell each jar over the bin; keep spices for the food you actually cooked in the last year), whether the sink is allowed to be a staging area (settle soaking with a deadline rather than a ban), and the guilt-leftover container you have moved to the front three times (name the meal, the day, and who eats it, or bin it today and cook a little less next time)
- The soak-and-dwell reframe (frozen pull-quote): the most powerful tool in a kitchen clean is not a scrubbing arm, it is time, set the burner grates and the hood filter to soak in hot degreasing water before you touch anything else, and let every degreaser sit for its dwell time, the chemistry lifts baked grease while you work elsewhere; the lift-do-not-wipe-around reframe (frozen pull-quote): the crumbs and grease you never see live under the toaster's feet, on top of the cabinets, and in the fold of the fridge gasket, and a wipe around the clutter is not a clean; the reset-point reframe (frozen pull-quote): the sink is the point the whole kitchen resets from, so it has to be genuinely clean, not just rinsed, and the sponge and the dishwasher filter are the things you never think to clean that matter most
- The Shine kit for the kitchen (product TYPES grouped by job, never brands): the cleaners (a food-area degreaser, the kitchen's key input, for the hood, grates, splash wall, and the oil film on shelf floors; a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for everyday food residue; a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner for glass cabinet fronts and the oven door window), the cloths (a colour-coded microfiber set with a dedicated colour flagged for the fridge interior and the raw-meat shelf), the tools (a small detail brush set for burner wells, knob stems, drain flange, hinges, and runner tracks; a compact vacuum with attachments for cabinet tops, condenser coils, and the toe-kick; a portable caddy), plus dish soap and hot water for soaking grates and washing crisper drawers; gather the inputs into the caddy before you start, types only, buy for the job
- Watch for in the kitchen (the room's real hazards as the five safety questions, where the safety pass fixes everything the clean flagged): burn or fire (oil left heating unattended, a tea towel or paper-towel roll near a live burner, a toaster under a cabinet), fall/cut/crush (a knife blade-up in the drying rack or under suds, a pan handle turned out over the front edge, glassware above head height, blades stored unsheathed), water and electricity (the kettle, sockets, or a charging phone within splash of the basin), poison/choke/strangle (bleach mixed with an acidic or ammonia cleaner, loose button batteries in the utility drawer, raw-meat juices dripping onto ready-to-eat food), and the plain fall (reaching the top shelf from a stable step stool, never a chair, a counter edge, or an open drawer)
- The before-and-after (frozen caption, the Part 9 signature that replaces the friction meter): this is the kitchen the whole loop was for, the same room you started with, cleared, degreased, and genuinely clean, from the hood down to the toe-kick, take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard, because a kitchen this clean is one you will actually want to cook in, and the photo is what it has to match from now on
- The One Idea to Keep (frozen close): a kitchen is not clean because the counters are clear, it is clean because you lifted the appliances, soaked the grease, pulled the cloth into the gasket, and cleared the filter, cleaning to a method zone by zone and inspecting as you went, do one zone this weekend, the sink or the cooking zone, and cook in a room that is finally clean underneath

## Cross-chapter / reuse cautions
- This is the SECOND chapter of Part Nine and the second of twenty room playbooks (Chapters 31 to 50, one per room in the Micro Zone Manual). The six-S method, Parts 1 to 8, is complete, and Part Nine points the finished loop at one real room at a time. Chapter 32 owns the kitchen; the room's own zone-level clean/shine detail and inputs, and the grease layer, are the new material, and it does NOT teach the method again.
- CRITICAL fence (Chapters 1 to 30, the whole six-S method): Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain are already taught.
