## Hero device (frozen): the visual control (the home that speaks)
A home can be well chosen (Ch 12) and well placed (Ch 13) and still be silent: only the person who built it knows what belongs there. A visual control is the cue that makes the home speak for itself.
> **The rule (frozen):** A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word.

## The Glance Test (frozen check, set as a pull-quote)
> Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent.

## The three simple controls (frozen framing; teach these, home-scaled, not factory jargon)
- **The label** names what belongs in a spot (the bin says "batteries"). Label the spot, not just the thing.
- **The outline** shows the shape or the place a thing goes (a drawn or taped silhouette, a hook with the item's outline, a marked rectangle where the bin sits). Its quiet power: an empty outline shows at a glance when the thing is missing.
- **The boundary** shows the edges of a home and when it is overfull (a tray, a basket, a divider, a line): everything inside belongs, and when it will not close or the line is crossed, the home is telling you it is full.
- Two lighter controls to fold in: **seeing in** (a clear jar or bin shows the contents and the level without opening) and **color** (one color per person or per kind, read instantly).

## The reframe (frozen)
> You were the label. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it. A visual control moves that knowledge out of your head and onto the shelf, where everyone can read it, including you on a tired day.

## Worked example (the entryway keepers, then scaling up)
Every entryway keeper has a home now, well placed; Chapter 14 makes each one speak:
- **Keys** get a small labeled hook or a painted key silhouette on the hook, so a house-sitter knows at a glance.
- **Leash** gets a labeled hook or a little drawn outline, so anyone can return it.
- **Umbrella** is the easy one: an umbrella stand is a visual control by its shape, its form already says what it holds, no label needed. Use it to show that the best control is sometimes built into the object's home.
- **Reusable bags** get a labeled basket or hook ("bags"), so they stop migrating.
- **Outgoing mail** gets a tray marked "out" or "to post," so the go-mail is visually separate from the keep-mail.
Then scale up in one paragraph: the pantry (labeled bins, clear jars that show the level, a simple line that says "buy more when you can see it"); the kids' zone (one color of hook and cubby per child, read before they can read words); the junk drawer (a divider tray so every small thing has a bounded slot); the linen closet (a labeled shelf edge so folded stacks land in the right place).

## One Idea to Keep (frozen)
> A good home does not need you to explain it. Put the knowledge where the thing lives, a label, an outline, a clear jar, and the room starts telling everyone what belongs where, so keeping order stops being your job alone.

## Definition box (frozen)
> A visual control is any simple cue, a label, an outline, a color, a boundary, a clear container, that makes it obvious at a glance what belongs in a spot and whether it is there. It moves the rule out of your memory and onto the thing itself, so the home works even when you are not there to run it.

## Callouts (frozen wording)
- **Quick Win:** Pick one bin, drawer, or shelf whose contents are a mystery from the outside, and give it one label or one clear container. Then ask someone else in the house to put a thing away there. If they get it right without asking, the home just started speaking.
- **Family Challenge:** Walk the house together and find the three spots where people always ask "where does this go?" Add one visual control to each, a label, an outline, or a bin, and let the room answer the question from now on.
- **6S Tip:** Label the spot, not just the thing. The bin, the shelf edge, the hook, that is where a guest looks. A label on the item travels away with the item; a label on the home stays to greet the next thing that belongs there.
- **Common Mistake:** Making the cue for you instead of for a stranger. Codes, abbreviations, and "I will remember" markers keep the system in your head. If a guest could not read it and act on it, it is decoration, not a control.
