# Your Home Runs on Rules Only You Can Read. Write Them Down.

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Stand at the door one final time. Every keeper has a home now, and every home sits where your hand actually wants it. The keys hang first thing, the leash beside them, the bag a step to the side, the umbrella in its corner, the mail in its tray. You built all of it, and it works. You could return any one of these things in the dark, because the whole arrangement lives in your head.

Now watch someone else. A friend picks up your keys to lock up while you are away and stands in the entryway, looking at a row of hooks that all appear the same. A weekend guest sets the leash on the counter, because nothing near the door told them it lived on a hook. Your own child calls out the oldest question in any household: where does this go?

The home is right there, a foot away, correct in every respect. It simply never told anyone.

That gap is the whole of this chapter. A home can be well chosen and well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself, because the rule for what belongs there sits in exactly one place: your memory. Let me fence this off plainly. We are not re-choosing what to keep, and we are not re-deciding where a home sits; both are done. We are not yet drawing the single map of the whole room, which is the next chapter, and we are not building the routines that keep a place in order over weeks, which is a later S. Today has one narrow task: give each home a way to explain itself.

## What a visual control is

There is a well-worn idea from the workshops where this thinking began. On a good workbench, each tool has its own painted shape on the board behind its hook. Reach for the hammer and you know precisely where it goes back, and if the hammer is gone, the empty shape tells you so from across the room. Nobody has to hold the layout in mind. The board holds it for them.

That is the whole idea of a visual control, shrunk to fit a home. It is any simple cue attached to a spot that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list.

**A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word.**

You are not adding storage or buying a system; you are marking the storage you already arranged, so it can answer for itself.

## The Glance Test

How do you know whether a home really explains itself, or whether you are just so used to it that you cannot tell the difference? There is one plain test, and it is the center of this chapter.

**Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent.**

Every clause does work. At a glance means the answer arrives in the time it takes to look. Without asking is the heart of it: if the honest answer is that they would text you a photo and wait, then the home is still keeping its secret, however tidy it looks.

Run this test on your own house and it stings a little at first, because most spots fail it, and they fail it invisibly. You never notice the ones that only work because of you. None of this is anyone failing. It is a design gap.

## Three simple controls

Three cues cover almost everything in a house, and each answers a slightly different question.

- The label · names what belongs in a spot: the bin that says batteries, the hook that says leash. It turns a blank container into one that announces its contents before you open it.
- The outline · shows the shape a thing goes in. Because it draws a thing's absence as clearly as its presence, an empty outline tells you at a glance that the thing is missing. The label says what lives here; the outline also says whether it is home right now.
- The boundary · shows the edges of a home and when it is overfull: a tray, a divider, a painted line. When the lid will not close, the home is telling you it has taken all it can hold.

Two lighter cues round out the set: seeing in (a clear jar or bin shows the contents and the level without opening) and color (one shade per person or per kind, read faster than any word). Match one cue to the one confusion a spot keeps producing, and stop there. One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces, is nearly always plenty.

**Label the spot, not just the thing.** The bin, the shelf edge, the hook, that is where a guest looks. A label on the item travels away with the item; a label on the home stays to greet the next thing that belongs there.

## You were the label

Step back and notice what has been happening in your home all this time. Every spot that only you understand has had a label on it the whole while. The label was you.

**You were the label. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it. A visual control moves that knowledge out of your head and onto the shelf, where everyone can read it, including you on a tired day.**

This is meant to lift a weight off you. You have been the living index of the house, consulted a dozen times a day, on call even when you are worn out, or away, or simply trying to read in peace. A cue on the shelf takes that job off you. It never leaves, never tires, and answers everyone the same.

## Giving the keepers a voice

Back to the door, where every keeper already has its well-placed home. Nothing moves in this pass. The keys get a labeled hook or a drawn key shape, so the outline sits empty whenever the keys are out. The leash gets a hook marked leash. The umbrella teaches the best lesson: a stand needs no label at all, because its shape already tells you what it holds. Whenever you can choose a container whose form declares its job, you get the control for free. The reusable bags get a basket that says bags; the outgoing mail gets a tray marked out.

The same three cues scale to every room. The pantry gets labeled bins and clear jars, with a buy-more line drawn inside the rice jar. The kids' zone gets one color of hook and cubby per child. The junk drawer gets a divider tray so every small thing has a bounded slot. The linen closet gets a labeled shelf edge, sheets here, towels there. None of this is a project. It is an afternoon with a marker, a roll of tape, and a few containers you very likely already own.

## Nothing left, and the room stopped leaning on you

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves once more, and nothing had to leave the house for it to move. The room holds exactly what it held this morning. All that changed is that the homes learned to explain themselves, and the room stopped leaning on a single person's memory to run. Every "where does this go" that no longer gets asked is a small interruption removed. So the needle takes the third step of Straighten, honestly earned, and stops well short of the end, which is exactly what makes it worth trusting.

**A good home does not need you to explain it. Put the knowledge where the thing lives, a label, an outline, a clear jar, and the room starts telling everyone what belongs where, so keeping order stops being your job alone.**

## The assignment

Pick one bin, drawer, or shelf whose contents are a mystery from the outside, and give it one label or one clear container. Then ask someone else in the house to put a thing away there. If they get it right without asking, the home just started speaking.
