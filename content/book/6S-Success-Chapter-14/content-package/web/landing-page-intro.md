# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 14, Visual Controls for the Home

Stand at the door one final time. Every keeper has a home, and every home now sits where your hand actually wants it. The keys hang first thing by the door, the leash beside them, the bag a step to the side, the umbrella in its corner, the mail in its tray on the way out. You built all of it, and it works. You could return any one of these things in the dark, without looking, because the whole arrangement lives in your head. That last part is the whole of this chapter, because the room runs beautifully when you are there to run it, and quietly comes undone when you are not.

Now watch someone else. A friend picks up your keys to lock up while you are away and stands in the entryway, looking at a row of hooks that all appear the same. A weekend guest sets the leash on the counter, because nothing near the door told them it lived on a hook. Your own child drops the reusable bag wherever, then calls out that oldest question in any household: where does this go? The home is right there, a foot away, correct in every respect. It simply never told anyone. None of this is anyone failing. A home can be well chosen and well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself, because the rule for what belongs there sits in exactly one place: your memory. It is a design gap, not a fault, and this chapter closes it.

Welcome to the third step of Straighten. Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and placing each home by how often your hand goes to it was the right second one, and neither unwinds here. But a home you chose well and placed well is still only half finished if the sole record of what belongs in it is you. So the work turns once more, from placing homes well to making each placed home explain itself, and it does so without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is take the rule that has always lived in your head and set it down on the shelf, where anyone can read it.

The tool for that is the visual control, and the idea comes almost unchanged from the workshops where this thinking began. On a good workbench you sometimes see a board behind the tools where each tool has its own painted shape, so a reach tells you exactly where a thing goes back, and an empty shape tells you from across the room that a thing is out. That board has lifted the rule off one person's memory and onto the wall, where anyone can read it. Shrink it to fit a hallway and you have the whole of this chapter: a visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word. It is any simple cue, a label, an outline, a color, a boundary, a clear container, that makes it obvious at a glance what belongs in a spot and whether it is there.

There is one plain test for whether a home really explains itself, and it is the center of the chapter. Picture handing your house to someone who has never set foot in it, a pet sitter, a new babysitter, a friend stopping by to water the plants, and ask of any spot: could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent. Run this Glance Test on your own rooms and it stings a little at first, because most spots fail it, and they fail it invisibly. You never notice the ones that only work because of you, since you are the reason they do.

Three simple cues cover almost everything in a house. The label names what belongs in a spot, and the one rule that matters most is to label the spot, not just the thing, because the hook, the bin, and the shelf edge are where a guest looks. The outline shows the shape or place a thing goes, and its quiet power is that an empty outline reveals at a glance when the thing is missing. The boundary shows the edges of a home and, without a word, when it is overfull. Two lighter cues round out the set: seeing in, a clear jar or bin that shows the contents and the level without opening, and color, one shade per person or per kind, so a three-year-old who cannot yet read still knows which hook is theirs. The small art of it is choosing the one cue that answers the particular question a spot keeps raising, and then stopping there.

Step back and this lands as relief, not as one more chore. Every spot that only you understand has had a label on it the whole while, and the label was you. You have been the living index of the house, consulted a dozen times a day, on call even when you are worn out, or away, or simply trying to read in peace. A cue on the shelf takes that job off you. It knows no less than you do; it just never leaves, never tires, and answers everyone the same. Give the entryway keepers a voice, keys a labeled hook or a painted silhouette, leash a hook marked leash, the umbrella a stand whose shape already says what it holds, bags a labeled basket, mail a tray marked out, and the same three cues scale to the pantry, the kids' zone, the junk drawer, and the linen closet. None of this is a project. It is an afternoon with a marker, a roll of tape, and a few containers you very likely already own.

Read Chapter 14 free in the online book, then take the smallest possible first step. Pick one bin, drawer, or shelf whose contents are a mystery from the outside, and give it one label or one clear container. Then ask someone else in the house to put a thing away there, and watch whether they get it right without asking. If they do, the home just started speaking. Then, in Chapter 15, every home you just made self-explaining gets one shared map, so the whole room can be read in a single look and Straighten is complete.
