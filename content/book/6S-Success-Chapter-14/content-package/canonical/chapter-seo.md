## Search intent
Making the home self-explaining, not removing or re-placing. Readers arriving here have already decluttered, given their things homes, and placed those homes by how often they use them, but the room still only works when they are personally there, because the rule for what goes where lives in one person's head. They want a way to make each spot answer for itself, so guests, kids, and house-sitters can find a thing and put it back without asking. The chapter answers with a device and a test: a visual control is any simple cue that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list, and the Glance Test asks whether someone who has never lived here could put a thing away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking. It teaches three simple cues (the label, the outline, the boundary) plus seeing-in and color, delivers the reframe that you were the label, gives the entryway keepers a voice, and scales the cues to the pantry, kids' zone, junk drawer, and linen closet.

## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Reframes organizing as making the home speak for itself, moving the rule out of one person's head and onto the shelf, instead of generic labeling tips
- Gives one plain, testable check, the Glance Test (could a stranger put this away correctly, at a glance, without asking?), rather than vague "keep it tidy" advice
- Distinguishes three cues by the question each answers: the label (what belongs here), the outline (is it here now), and the boundary (how much fits), which most guides blur together
- Teaches the counterintuitive rule to label the spot, not just the thing, because a label on the item travels away with the item
- Uses the shadow board's empty outline to show that a control can reveal a missing item at a glance, not just name a location
- Delivers the anti-shame reframe directly (a silent home is a design gap, not a failing; you were the label) instead of implying the reader is disorganized
- Warns against over-cueing (one cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion it produces), which most maximalist labeling guides ignore
- Keeps scope tight: this makes each home self-explaining; it does not re-assign homes (Chapter 12), re-place by frequency (Chapter 13), map the whole room (Chapter 15), or build routines for keeping order (the later Standardize S)
