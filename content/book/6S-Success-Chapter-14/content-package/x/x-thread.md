2/
A home can be placed perfectly and still leave everyone guessing. Your child drops the bag and calls out the oldest question in any house: where does this go?

"The home is right there, a foot away, correct in every respect. It simply never told anyone."

(approx. 250 chars)

3/
Why does the room work for you? Because you are the one standing in it.

"A home can be well chosen and well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself, because the rule for what belongs there sits in exactly one place: your memory."

(approx. 233 chars)

---

4/
None of this is anyone failing. It is a design gap. The knowledge exists; it has just never been written down anywhere the thing itself can be seen. So we move the rule out of your head and onto the shelf, where anyone can read it.

(approx. 227 chars)

5/
Picture the workshop board where each tool has a painted shape behind its hook. Reach for the hammer, and if it is gone the empty shape says so from across the room. Nobody holds the layout in mind. The board holds it for them. That is a visual control, home-scaled.

(approx. 262 chars)

---

6/
The rule the whole chapter rests on:

"A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word."

It is any simple cue attached to a spot that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list.

(approx. 214 chars)

---

7/
The hero check. Set as one plain test of any spot:

"Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent."

(approx. 261 chars)

---

8/
Run it on your own house and it stings a little, because most spots fail it, and they fail invisibly. You never notice the ones that only work because of you. Walk your rooms as a polite stranger, and the failures show themselves, each one a rule trapped in your head.

(approx. 264 chars)

---

9/
Three simple cues cover almost everything. First, the label. It names what belongs in a spot: the bin that says batteries, the hook that says leash. It turns a blank container into one that announces its own contents before you open it.

(approx. 233 chars)

---

10/
Second, the outline: the shape a thing goes, drawn where it sits.

"The label says what lives here; the outline also says whether it is home right now."

An empty key shape behind the hook tells you at a glance the keys are out with you.

(approx. 232 chars)

---

11/
Third, the boundary: a tray, a basket, a divider, a line.

"A boundary is how a spot says enough on its own, so nobody has to stand guard over it or decide, again and again, how much is too much."

(approx. 197 chars)

---

12/
Two lighter cues round it out. Seeing in: a clear jar shows the contents and the level without opening. Color: one shade per person, read faster than any word.

"One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces, is nearly always plenty."

(approx. 249 chars)

---

13/
The reframe at the heart of it:

"You were the label. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it. A visual control moves that knowledge out of your head and onto the shelf, where everyone can read it, including you on a tired day."

(approx. 268 chars)

---

14/
Back to the door. The keys get a labeled hook, or a little key shape drawn behind it, so a pet sitter sees where they live and whether they are home. The leash gets the same. The umbrella stand needs no label: its shape already says what it holds.

(approx. 245 chars)

---

15/
"Whenever you can choose a container whose form declares its job, you get the control for free, with nothing to print and nothing to draw."

The bags get a labeled basket; the mail a tray marked out. The same cues scale to every room.

(approx. 237 chars)
