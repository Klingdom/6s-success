## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
The homes are assigned (Ch 12) and well placed (Ch 13), but silent: only the person who built them knows what belongs where, so things drift back and the family keeps asking "where does this go?" A **visual control** makes each home explain itself. **A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word.** The check is the **Glance Test**: could a guest put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? Three simple cues cover most of a house, the label (names what belongs), the outline (shows whether it is home; an empty outline reads as missing), and the boundary (shows when a spot is full), plus seeing-in (clear jars) and color. The reframe: **you were the label**, the living index of the house; a cue on the shelf takes that job off you. The entryway keepers each get a voice, then the same cues scale to the pantry, the kids' zone, the junk drawer, and the linen closet. Nothing leaves the house, yet the friction meter takes its third Straighten step, because the room stops leaning on one person's memory. Hands off to Chapter 15 (The Room Map), which completes Straighten.

## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule: **A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word.**
- The Glance Test: **Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent.**
- The reframe: **You were the label. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it. A visual control moves that knowledge out of your head and onto the shelf, where everyone can read it, including you on a tired day.**
- One Idea to Keep: **A good home does not need you to explain it. Put the knowledge where the thing lives, a label, an outline, a clear jar, and the room starts telling everyone what belongs where, so keeping order stops being your job alone.**
