### Slide 2 · The home never told anyone

- Stand at the door one final time. Every keeper has a home, and every home now sits where your hand actually wants it. You could return any one of these things in the dark, because the whole arrangement lives in your head.
- Now watch someone else. A friend picks up your keys to lock up while you are away and stands looking at a row of hooks that all appear the same. A guest sets the leash on the counter. Your own child drops the reusable bag and calls out, from the next room, the oldest question in any household: where does this go?
- The frozen line, verbatim: "The home is right there, a foot away, correct in every respect. It simply never told anyone."

### Slide 3 · The home that does not speak

- The last two chapters did the heavy lifting of Straighten: first a single home for every keeper, then each home placed by how often your hand goes to it. None of that unwinds here.
- But look closely at why it works and you find something fragile. It works because you are the one standing in it. Every rule, the keys on the first hook, the leash a hook thing not a counter thing, the tray for outgoing mail, is real, and every one is stored in a single place nobody else can open: the inside of your head.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "A home can be well chosen and well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself, because the rule for what belongs there sits in exactly one place: your memory." None of this is anyone failing. It is a design gap. Keep the fences honest: not re-choosing what to keep, not re-deciding where a home sits, not yet drawing the map of the whole room (Chapter 15), not building routines for keeping order (a later S).

### Slide 4 · What a visual control is

- There is a well-worn idea from the workshops where this thinking began, and it carries over to a hallway almost unchanged. On a good workbench, a board behind the tools gives each tool its own painted shape. Reach for the hammer and you know exactly where it goes back, and if the hammer is gone, the empty hammer-shape tells you so from across the room. The board holds the layout so nobody has to.
- That board took the rule for where each tool belongs and lifted it out of one person's memory and onto the wall, where anyone can read it. The frozen line, verbatim: "It is any simple cue attached to a spot that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list." You are not adding storage or buying a system; you are marking the storage you already arranged, so it can answer for itself.
- The rule, verbatim: "A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word."
- Definition, verbatim: "A visual control is any simple cue, a label, an outline, a color, a boundary, a clear container, that makes it obvious at a glance what belongs in a spot and whether it is there. It moves the rule out of your memory and onto the thing itself, so the home works even when you are not there to run it."

### Slide 5 · The Glance Test

- How do you know whether a home really explains itself, or whether you are just so used to it that you cannot tell the difference? There is one plain test, and it is the center of this chapter. Picture handing your house to someone who has never set foot in it, a pet sitter, a new babysitter, a friend watering the plants, and ask a single question of any spot.
- Read it slowly, because every clause does work. At a glance means the answer arrives in the time it takes to look. Without opening it rules out the cue that only appears once you have already pulled the drawer. Without reading a list keeps the knowledge on the thing, not on a laminated sheet on the fridge. And without asking is the heart of it.
- The Glance Test, verbatim: "Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent."

### Slide 6 · Three simple controls

- You do not need a workshop full of techniques. Three simple cues cover almost everything in a house, and all three read in a look.
- The label names what belongs in a spot: the bin that says batteries, the shelf edge that says towels, the hook that says leash. The outline shows the shape or place a thing goes; because it draws a thing's absence as clearly as its presence, an empty outline tells you at a glance that the thing is missing. The frozen line, verbatim: "The label says what lives here; the outline also says whether it is home right now." The boundary shows the edges of a home and, without a word, when it is overfull. The frozen line, verbatim: "A boundary is how a spot says enough on its own, so nobody has to stand guard over it or decide, again and again, how much is too much."
- Two lighter cues round out the set: seeing in (a clear jar or bin, so the contents and the level both read without opening) and color (one shade per person or per kind, so a three-year-old who cannot yet read knows which hook is theirs).

### Slide 7 · Match the cue to the question

- Matching the cue to the question is most of the skill, and it is easier than it sounds. Ask what the spot keeps getting wrong. If people cannot tell what belongs in a closed container, it wants a label or a clear front. If a thing keeps wandering off and nobody notices until you need it, it wants an outline, so its absence shows. If the spot keeps overflowing onto the counter, it wants a boundary that says when it is full.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces, is nearly always plenty." Piling three cues onto a drawer that only ever needed a label does not make it clearer; it just makes it busy, and a busy cue is one more thing to read past.
- 6S Tip, verbatim: "Label the spot, not just the thing. The bin, the shelf edge, the hook, that is where a guest looks. A label on the item travels away with the item; a label on the home stays to greet the next thing that belongs there."
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Making the cue for you instead of for a stranger. Codes, abbreviations, and \"I will remember\" markers keep the system in your head. If a guest could not read it and act on it, it is decoration, not a control."

### Slide 8 · You were the label

- Step back and notice what has actually been happening in your home all this time. The frozen line, verbatim: "Every spot that only you understand has had a label on it the whole while. The label was you."
- This is meant to lift a weight off you, not to add one. The frozen line, verbatim: "You have been the living index of the house, consulted a dozen times a day, on call even when you are worn out, or away, or simply trying to read in peace." A cue on the shelf takes that job off you. It knows no less than you do; it just never leaves, never tires, and answers everyone the same.
- The reframe, verbatim: "You were the label. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it. A visual control moves that knowledge out of your head and onto the shelf, where everyone can read it, including you on a tired day."

### Slide 9 · Giving the keepers a voice

- Back to the door, where every keeper already has its well-placed home. Nothing moves in this pass; we only give each spot a way to explain itself.
- The keys get the smallest cue that works: a labeled hook, or better, a little key shape drawn behind the hook, so the outline sits empty whenever the keys are out and a pet sitter learns two things in one look. The leash gets the same idea, a hook marked leash or a drawn outline. The umbrella is the easy one and teaches the best lesson. The frozen line, verbatim: "Whenever you can choose a container whose form declares its job, you get the control for free, with nothing to print and nothing to draw." The reusable bags get a labeled basket or a hook that says bags; the outgoing mail gets a tray marked out, or to post.
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Pick one bin, drawer, or shelf whose contents are a mystery from the outside, and give it one label or one clear container. Then ask someone else in the house to put a thing away there. If they get it right without asking, the home just started speaking."

### Slide 10 · The same cues scale to the whole house

- That is one small hallway. The same three cues scale to every room in the house, and the busier the room, the more they repay.
- In the pantry, labeled bins and clear jars let you read the whole shelf without touching it, and a single buy-more line inside the rice jar tells you to restock. In the kids' zone, one color of hook and cubby per child sorts coats and bags before anyone is old enough to read the words. In the junk drawer, a cheap divider tray gives every small thing a bounded slot. In the linen closet, a labeled shelf edge, sheets here, towels there, means a folded stack lands in the right place even for a person who has never once put laundry away in this house.
- None of this is a project. It is an afternoon with a marker, a roll of tape, and a few containers you very likely already own.
- Family Challenge, verbatim: "Walk the house together and find the three spots where people always ask \"where does this go?\" Add one visual control to each, a label, an outline, or a bin, and let the room answer the question from now on."

### Slide 12 · A home that answers for itself

- Walk the entryway one last time, but not as yourself. Walk it as the friend who is locking up while you are away. The keys hang behind their painted shape, present or plainly gone. The hook marked leash takes the leash back without a word from you. The umbrella stand needs no explaining, and the tray marked out holds exactly what is heading out the door. The frozen line, verbatim: "The room did the explaining. You were somewhere else entirely, and it held together anyway."
- You did not buy an organizing system or add an ounce of storage. You took the rules that had always lived in your head and set them down on the shelves, the hooks, and the bins. The frozen line, verbatim: "A home that carries its own instructions asks less of everyone, and it keeps asking less tomorrow. It also survives you being human."
- One Idea to Keep, verbatim: "A good home does not need you to explain it. Put the knowledge where the thing lives, a label, an outline, a clear jar, and the room starts telling everyone what belongs where, so keeping order stops being your job alone."

### Slide 13 · Each home now speaks (close + soft CTA)

- Each home now answers for itself, which is exactly what this chapter set out to do. You did not add storage or buy a system; you moved the rule out of your head and onto the shelf, where it waits for whoever needs it next, yourself included, on a day too tired to remember much of anything.
- But notice the gap that remains. A stranger can put any single thing away correctly and still have no idea how the whole house fits together, because they meet each home alone, in the moment they happen to need it. There is no one picture yet. Giving the household that shared map, so the whole room can be read in a single look, is where Straighten finishes.
