# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Visual Controls for the Home

*Chapter 14, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter draws a line between two acts: giving a thing the right home, and making that home speak. Chapters 12 and 13 did the first; Chapter 14 does the second. Where in your own home is a spot well chosen and well placed and yet still silent, obvious only to the person who built it?

2. "A home can be well chosen and well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself, because the rule for what belongs there sits in exactly one place: your memory." Which rules about where things go live only in your head right now, so the room runs beautifully when you are there and quietly comes undone when you are not?

3. The chapter insists that "None of this is anyone failing. It is a design gap. The knowledge exists; it has just never been written down anywhere the thing itself can be seen." How does reading a silent home as a design gap, rather than a family's failing, change how it feels to notice the spots that only work because of you?

4. The rule, in one line, is: "A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word." Where in your home would you most like the right thing to be obvious without a word, so the asking simply stops?

5. Run the hero check on a real spot: "Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent." Pick a house-sitter or a weekend guest in your mind and walk three spots. Which pass, and which keep their secret however tidy they look?

6. Every clause of the Glance Test does work: at a glance, without opening it, without reading a list, and without asking. Which clause does the most spots in your home fail on, and what would it take to move the answer onto the thing itself?

7. The label names what belongs; the outline shows whether it is home right now; the boundary shows when the spot is full. "The label says what lives here; the outline also says whether it is home right now." Name a spot in your home for each cue, one that wants a label, one that wants an outline, and one that wants a boundary.

8. "One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces, is nearly always plenty." Have you ever piled several cues onto a spot that only needed one, and found it busier rather than clearer? Ask what a troublesome spot keeps getting wrong, and pick the single cue that answers that one confusion.

9. The umbrella teaches the easiest lesson in the chapter: a stand needs no label because its shape already says what it holds. "Whenever you can choose a container whose form declares its job, you get the control for free, with nothing to print and nothing to draw." Where in your home could a container's own shape do the explaining, so there is nothing to print and nothing to draw?

10. The reframe is meant to lift a weight off you: "You were the label. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it." Where have you been the living index of the house, consulted a dozen times a day, and how would it feel to hand that job to a cue on the shelf?

11. "A home that carries its own instructions asks less of everyone, and it keeps asking less tomorrow. It also survives you being human." Think of a day you were sick, away, or too tired to think straight. Which spots in your home kept answering questions without you, and which fell silent the moment you were not there to run them?

12. The entryway keepers each get a voice with the smallest cue that works. Walk your own most-used doorway, drawer, or shelf and name the one cue each keeper there would need to speak for itself. Which is a label, which an outline, and which is already a container whose shape says its job?

13. The same three cues scale to the pantry, the kids' zone, the junk drawer, and the linen closet, and "the busier the room, the more they repay." Which busy room in your home would gain the most from one afternoon with a marker, some tape, and a few containers you likely already own?

14. Nothing left the house in this chapter, yet the friction meter still stepped toward calm, because the room stopped leaning on one person's memory. Each home now answers for itself, but a newcomer still meets each one alone, with no single picture of how the house fits together. Where would a shared map of where everything lives, the work of the next chapter, help most in your home?
