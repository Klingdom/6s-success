# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

You finished the last chapter with every keeper in a home and every home placed where your hand actually wants it, and the room looked settled. Then you watched someone else use it. A friend picks up your keys to lock up and stands at a row of hooks that all look the same; your own child drops the reusable bag wherever and calls out, from the next room, the oldest question in any household: where does this go? The home is right there, a foot away, correct in every respect. It simply never told anyone. Chapter 14 of *6S Success: Home Edition* closes that gap.

"Visual Controls for the Home" is the third chapter of Part Four, and it keeps working inside Straighten, the second of the six S's. Chapter 12 assigned the homes; Chapter 13 placed them by how often you reach for each thing; this chapter keeps the same keepers and the same room and gives each already-placed home a way to explain itself. The chapter names its fence plainly: it does not re-assign homes, does not re-place them by frequency, does not map the whole room, does not build routines for keeping order, and does not re-open Sort. Today has one narrow task, take each home you have already placed and give it a way to explain itself, so the rule inside your head ends up out here, where anyone can read it.

The trouble is that the room only works because you are the one standing in it. "A home can be well chosen and well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself, because the rule for what belongs there sits in exactly one place: your memory." So the room runs beautifully when you are there and quietly comes undone when you are not. "None of this is anyone failing. It is a design gap. The knowledge exists; it has just never been written down anywhere the thing itself can be seen." The fix is a visual control, and the workshop shadow board is the classic picture of it: each tool has its own painted silhouette, so a glance shows where everything goes and one empty shape shows what is missing. The board holds the layout so nobody has to. "It is any simple cue attached to a spot that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list."

The rule, shrunk to fit a home, is one line: "A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word." And the hero check is the Glance Test: "Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent." Every clause does work. At a glance means the answer arrives in the time it takes to look; without opening it rules out the cue you only see after you have pulled the drawer; without reading a list keeps the knowledge on the thing; and without asking is the heart of it.

Three simple cues cover almost everything in a house, and all three can be read in a look. The label names what belongs in a spot, the bin that says batteries, the shelf edge that says towels, the hook that says leash. The outline shows the shape or the place a thing goes, and because it draws a thing's absence as clearly as its presence, an empty outline shows at a glance when the thing is missing: "The label says what lives here; the outline also says whether it is home right now." The boundary shows the edges of a home and, without a word, when it is overfull, a tray, a basket, a divider, a line: "A boundary is how a spot says enough on its own, so nobody has to stand guard over it or decide, again and again, how much is too much." Two lighter cues round out the set, seeing in (a clear jar or bin shows the contents and the level without opening) and color (one shade per person or per kind, so a three-year-old who cannot yet read knows which hook is theirs). The small art is matching the cue to the question the spot keeps raising: "One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces, is nearly always plenty."

Then the chapter lands its reframe. "Every spot that only you understand has had a label on it the whole while. The label was you." This is meant to lift a weight off you, not add one: "You have been the living index of the house, consulted a dozen times a day, on call even when you are worn out, or away, or simply trying to read in peace." A cue on the shelf takes that job off you, and it survives you being human, the sick day, the week a relative stays, the late night when you cannot think straight.

The entryway keepers each get a voice, and nothing moves in the pass. The keys get a labeled hook or a little painted key shape, so an empty outline shows whenever they are out. The leash gets a hook marked leash or a drawn outline. The umbrella teaches the best lesson in the chapter: an umbrella stand needs no label, because its shape already tells you what it holds, so "Whenever you can choose a container whose form declares its job, you get the control for free, with nothing to print and nothing to draw." The reusable bags get a labeled basket or hook, and the outgoing mail gets a tray marked out. Then the same three cues scale to every room: the pantry (labeled bins, clear jars, a buy-more line drawn inside the rice jar), the kids' zone (one color of hook and cubby per child, read before they can read words), the junk drawer (a divider tray so every small thing has a bounded slot), and the linen closet (a labeled shelf edge, sheets here, towels there). None of it is a project, just an afternoon with a marker, some tape, and a few containers you likely already own.

Nothing leaves the house in this chapter, yet the friction meter takes its third Straighten step, a little further toward calm, because the room stopped depending on one person's memory. Chapter 13's position becomes the faint dashed ghost just behind the live needle, the earlier step and the Sort-complete milestone sit further back, and the goal crosshair on the calm side is still ahead, because one more Straighten chapter and four more S's remain. Each home now answers for itself, but a newcomer still has to walk the whole house meeting them one at a time; there is no one picture yet. That single shared map of where everything lives, so the whole room can be read at once, is where Chapter 15 takes Straighten to its finish.
