# B-Roll and Visual Notes: Visual Controls for the Home

**Chapter 14, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** visual-controls-for-the-home
**Use:** Shot list, on-screen text moments, and lower-third suggestions for the YouTube, short-form, and any companion video cuts.

**Visual vocabulary to keep consistent (from the chapter):**
- The hero device is the visual control: any simple cue attached to a spot that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list. Show it as a cue on the home itself, a label, an outline, a boundary, a clear jar, so the rule moves out of one person's head and onto the shelf.
- The starting point is the settled, well-placed entryway from Chapters 12 and 13. A neat row of homes by the door: hooks, a small shelf, a couple of lidded bins, a tray. Each is clearly a good spot, and each is completely blank. A person stands in the middle holding a set of keys, looking from hook to hook. A soft floating question hangs above their head: where does this go?
- The homes are already assigned and already well placed. The only action this chapter is giving each placed home a cue so it explains itself. Do NOT stage re-assigning a home, re-placing by frequency, re-Sorting, or weighing what stays.
- The shadow board is the classic image: a workbench board where each tool has its own painted silhouette, so a glance shows where everything goes AND one empty outline shows a missing tool. Home-scale it; reference lightly, do not overclaim.
- The friction meter MOVES this chapter, but nothing leaves the house. The live needle steps past Chapter 13's position and a little further toward the calm side, the third step of Straighten. Chapter 13's position becomes a faint dashed ghost just behind the live needle; the earlier Chapter 12 step and the Sort-complete milestone sit further back. The GOAL crosshair stays out on the green calm side, because one more Straighten chapter and four more S's remain.
- Warm daylight, lived-in homes, real households. Not a styled catalog look.
- Keep the whole thing calm and unhurried. No force or violence framing. Nothing is purged or attacked. The tone is anti-shame: a silent home is a design gap, not a family's failing.

## CORE SHOT LIST

### Shot 1: The home that does not speak (hero opener)
- The settled entryway. A neat row of well-placed homes: hooks, a small shelf, a couple of lidded bins, a tray. Every one is a good spot, and every one is completely blank. A person stands in the middle holding keys, looking from hook to hook, unsure. A soft floating question hangs above their head: where does this go?
- Critical detail: the arrangement is finished and correct, but every home is mute, so the only person who can use the room without guessing is the person who built it.
- Coverage: one wide of the blank, tidy row; one slow push across the unmarked hooks and bins; one on the hesitating hand with the keys.
- **On-screen text moment:** "The home is right there. It never told anyone."
- **Lower third:** Chapter 14 · Visual Controls for the Home

### Shot 2: The system lives in your head (the turn)
- Split or dissolve: on the left, the owner moving through the entryway with easy certainty, putting things away in the dark; on the right, a visitor in the same spot, frozen, holding the leash, unsure whether it is a counter thing or a hook thing.
- The point: the room runs beautifully when you are there to run it, and quietly comes undone when you are not. The rule sits in exactly one place, your memory.
- **On-screen text moment:** "A home can be well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself."
- **Lower third:** A silent home: a design gap, not a failing

### Shot 3: What a visual control is (the shadow board)
- A workbench shadow board. Each tool has its own painted silhouette on the wall behind its hook: the hammer, the wrench, the pliers. One shape is empty, its tool out. A glance reads the whole layout, and the one gap where the wrench should be. Then dissolve to a home-scaled echo: a hook with a painted key shape behind it.
- The point: the board lifts the rule out of one person's memory and onto the wall, where anyone can read it. That is a visual control, shrunk to fit a home.
- **On-screen text moment:** "A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word."
- **Lower third:** The board holds the layout, so nobody has to

### Shot 4: The Glance Test (hero check)
- A house key handed to a pet sitter at the door. Then a single spot in soft focus, a drawer, with a question mark hovering over it. The question resolves into on-screen words. The sitter reads a small "cables" label and drops a stray cable in without pausing.
- The point: could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking?"
- **Lower third:** The Glance Test: could a guest put it away without asking?

### Shot 5: Three simple controls
- Three calm, home-scaled vignettes in a row, with a fourth smaller one. First, a lidded bin with a clear label reading "batteries" (the label). Second, a hook with a drawn key silhouette behind it, the hook empty, so the outline shows a thing is out (the outline). Third, a divider tray, each compartment holding one kind of small thing, with a marked fill line near the top (the boundary). Off to the side, a clear glass jar of pasta with the level plainly visible (seeing in). Each vignette titled with its cue name.
- The point: each cue answers a slightly different question. The label names what belongs; the outline shows whether it is home; the boundary shows when the spot is full.
- Cue-to-question inserts (readable, using the middot): "label · what belongs here", "outline · is it home right now?", "boundary · is the spot full?", "clear jar · what and how much, without opening".
- **On-screen text moment:** "One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces."
- **Lower third:** Match the cue to the question, then stop there

### Shot 6: You were the label (the reframe)
- A person moving through the house being asked, again and again, where the tape is, where the spare batteries hide, an invisible thread of little interruptions reaching for them all day. Then the thread goes slack as a small label and a painted outline appear on the shelves, and the next question gets answered by the shelf instead.
- The point: you have been the living index of the house. A cue on the shelf takes that job off you, and it never leaves, never tires, and answers everyone the same, including you on a tired day.
- **On-screen text moment:** "You were the label." then "A visual control moves that knowledge onto the shelf."
- **Lower third:** Stop being the room's memory

### Shot 7: Giving the keepers a voice (worked example)
- Each entryway home gaining its own cue: keys behind a little painted key shape, so the outline sits empty when they are out; a hook marked leash; an umbrella dropped into a stand whose shape already declares its job; a labeled basket claiming the reusable bags; a tray marked out holding the outgoing mail. Then scale up: a pantry of clear jars and labeled bins, a kids' zone of one color per child, a divider tray in the junk drawer, a labeled shelf edge in the linen closet.
- The point: nothing moves in this pass. Each already-placed home just learns to explain itself. The umbrella teaches the best lesson: its stand is a control by its own shape, so you get the cue for free.
- Home-to-cue inserts (readable, using the middot): "keys · a painted key shape behind the hook", "leash · a hook marked leash", "umbrella · a stand that declares itself, no label", "reusable bags · a labeled basket", "outgoing mail · a tray marked out".
- **On-screen text moment:** "Give each placed home a way to explain itself."
- **Lower third:** Whenever a container's form declares its job, the cue is free

## SUPPORTING / TRANSITION B-ROLL

- A friend at the door turning a set of keys over in their hand, looking from hook to hook, none of which says a thing (sells the silent home).
- A strip of tape reading "cables" going onto the front of a drawer, then a guest reading it and dropping a stray cable in without pausing (sells the label and the Glance Test together).
- A hook with a painted key shape behind it, empty while the keys are out, obviously "gone" at a glance (sells the outline's quiet power).
- A divider tray filling compartment by compartment until the marked line is reached, the spot plainly saying enough (sells the boundary).
- A clear jar of pasta or rice with a small buy-more line drawn partway down, the level readable from across the kitchen (sells seeing in).
- An umbrella dropped into a stand whose shape obviously declares its job, no label anywhere near it (sells the control built into the object's home).
- A pantry, a kids' zone of colored hooks, a junk-drawer divider tray, and a labeled linen shelf edge in quick succession (sells the same three cues scaling up).
- A soft rack-focus from the settled, now-speaking entryway to the whole calm house, each home explaining itself, with a stranger still walking room to room meeting them one at a time (sets up the Chapter 15 room-map teaser without drawing the map).
