## SECTION 1: THE HOME THAT DOES NOT SPEAK

[HOST]
Let me name the turn we are making, because it is easy to miss.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: A home can be well placed and still keep its knowledge to itself.]

[HOST, voice over]
The last two chapters did the heavy lifting of Straighten. First we gave every keeper a home. Then we placed each home by how often your hand goes to it. None of that unwinds here. But look closely at why it works and you find something a little fragile. It works because you are the one standing in it. You know the keys go on the first hook because you decided they would. Every one of those rules is real, and every one is stored where nobody else can open it: the inside of your head.

[HOST, on camera]
So the room runs beautifully when you are there to run it, and quietly comes undone when you are not. A visitor guesses wrong. A family member calls out that old question and waits, because you are the only copy of the answer. None of this is anyone failing. It is a design gap. The knowledge exists; it has just never been written down anywhere the thing itself can be seen.

## SECTION 2: WHAT A VISUAL CONTROL IS

[HOST]
There is a well-worn idea from the workshops where this thinking began, and it carries over to a hallway almost unchanged.

[VISUAL: A workbench shadow board. Each tool has its own painted silhouette on the wall behind its hook: the hammer, the wrench, the pliers. One shape is empty, its tool out. A glance reads the whole layout, and the one gap where the wrench should be.]

[HOST, voice over]
On a good workbench you sometimes see a board where each tool has its own painted shape drawn on the wall behind its hook. Reach for the hammer and you know exactly where it goes back, and if the hammer is gone, the empty shape tells you so from across the room. Nobody has to hold the layout in mind. The board holds it for them. It has taken the rule for where each tool belongs and lifted it out of one person's memory and onto the wall, where anyone can read it.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: A visual control makes the right thing obvious without a word.]

[HOST, on camera]
That is the whole idea of a visual control, shrunk to fit a home. It is any simple cue attached to a spot that makes the answer plain without a person, a conversation, or a list. You are not adding storage or buying a system. You are marking the storage you already arranged, so it can answer for itself.

## SECTION 3: THE GLANCE TEST

[HOST]
How do you know whether a home really explains itself, or whether you are just so used to it that you cannot tell? There is one plain test, and it is the center of this chapter.

[VISUAL: A house key handed over to a pet sitter at the door. Then a single spot in soft focus, a drawer, with a question mark hovering over it. The question resolves into on-screen words.]

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking?]

[HOST, voice over]
Picture handing your house to someone who has never set foot in it, and ask a single question of any spot. Could someone who has never lived here put this away correctly, at a glance, without opening it, reading a list, or asking? If they can, the home speaks for itself. If they would have to ask, it is still silent.

[HOST, on camera]
Every clause is doing work. At a glance means the answer arrives in the time it takes to look. Without opening it rules out the cue that only appears once you have already pulled the drawer. Without reading a list keeps the knowledge on the thing. And without asking is the heart of it. Run this test on your own house and it stings a little at first, because most spots fail it, and they fail it invisibly. You never notice the ones that only work because of you.

## SECTION 4: THREE SIMPLE CONTROLS

[HOST]
You do not need a workshop full of techniques. Three simple cues cover almost everything in a house, and all three can be read in a look.

[VISUAL: Three calm, home-scaled vignettes in a row. First, a lidded bin with a clear label reading "batteries." Second, a hook with a drawn key silhouette behind it, the hook empty. Third, a divider tray, each compartment holding one kind of small thing, with a marked fill line near the top. Off to the side, a clear glass jar of pasta with the level plainly visible.]

[HOST, voice over]
The first is the label. It names what belongs in a spot: the bin that says batteries, the shelf edge that says towels, the hook that says leash. The second is the outline: a shape drawn or taped where the item sits. Because it draws a thing's absence as clearly as its presence, an empty outline tells you at a glance that the thing is missing. The label says what lives here; the outline also says whether it is home right now. The third is the boundary: a tray, a basket, a divider, a line. Everything inside belongs, and the moment the lid will not close, the home is telling you it has taken all it can hold.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: One cue per spot, aimed at the one confusion that spot produces.]

[HOST, on camera]
Two lighter cues round out the set. Seeing in means choosing a clear jar, so the contents and the level both read without opening anything. Color means one shade per person, so a three-year-old who cannot yet read knows which hook is theirs. Most homes need only one of these. Ask what the spot keeps getting wrong, add the one cue that answers it, and stop there. Piling three cues onto a drawer that only needed a label does not make it clearer. It just makes it busy, and a busy cue is one more thing to read past.

## SECTION 5: YOU WERE THE LABEL

[HOST]
Step back and notice what has actually been happening in your home all this time.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: You were the label.]

[HOST, voice over]
Every spot that only you understand has had a label on it the whole while. The label was you. The system lived in your head, so the room only worked when you were there to run it. A visual control moves that knowledge out of your head and onto the shelf, where everyone can read it, including you on a tired day.

[HOST, on camera]
This is meant to lift a weight off you, not to add one. You have been the living index of the house, consulted a dozen times a day, on call even when you are worn out, or away, or simply trying to read in peace. A cue on the shelf takes that job off you. It knows no less than you do; it just never leaves, never tires, and answers everyone the same.

## SECTION 6: GIVING THE KEEPERS A VOICE

[HOST]
Back to the door, where every keeper already has its well-placed home. Nothing moves in this pass. We only give each spot a way to explain itself.

[B-ROLL: The keys hung behind a painted key shape; a hook marked leash taking the leash back; an umbrella dropped into its stand; a labeled basket claiming the reusable bags; a tray marked out holding the outgoing mail. Then a soft cut to a pantry shelf of clear jars and labeled bins.]

[HOST, voice over]
The keys get a little key shape painted behind the hook, so the outline sits empty and obvious whenever they are out with you. The leash gets a hook marked leash. The umbrella is the easy one, and it teaches the best lesson in the chapter: an umbrella stand needs no label at all, because its shape already tells you what it holds. Whenever you can choose a container whose form declares its job, you get the control for free. The bags get a labeled basket, and the outgoing mail gets a tray marked out.

[HOST, on camera]
That is one small hallway, and the same three cues scale to every room. The pantry gets labeled bins and clear jars, with a single buy-more line drawn inside the rice jar. The kids' zone gets one color of hook and cubby per child. The junk drawer gets a divider tray. The linen closet gets a labeled shelf edge, sheets here, towels there. None of this is a project. It is an afternoon with a marker, a roll of tape, and a few containers you very likely already own.

## CLOSE AND SOFT CTA

[HOST]
Walk the entryway one last time, but not as yourself. Walk it as the friend who is locking up while you are away.

[B-ROLL: The friend moving through the entryway, putting each thing away without hesitation, never once reaching for the phone. The keys behind their painted shape, the hook marked leash, the umbrella stand, the tray marked out.]

[HOST, voice over]
The keys hang behind their painted shape, present or plainly gone. The hook marked leash takes the leash back. The umbrella stand needs no explaining. Your friend puts everything where it belongs and never once reaches for the phone to ask. The room did the explaining. You were somewhere else entirely, and it held together anyway. You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage. You took the rules that had always lived in your head and set them down on the shelves.

[HOST, on camera]
So here is your job, and it takes almost nothing. Pick one bin, drawer, or shelf whose contents are a mystery from the outside, and give it one label or one clear container. Then ask someone else in the house to put a thing away there. If they get it right without asking, the home just started speaking.
