## Search intent
Keeping a good home good, after the reader has built it and taken on the keeping mindset from Chapter 26. Readers arriving here have a home that is cleared, arranged, clean, safe, and standardized, and they want a concrete, repeatable way to keep it from drifting back, but the obvious word for it, audit, makes them flinch, because it sounds like a test with an inspector and a grade. The chapter answers with one hero device and one rule: a home audit is not an inspection, it is a check-in, and an audit checks the home, not the household, so what it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame. It names why the word makes people flinch (it arrives carrying tax inspectors, clipboards, and grades, but that is inspection, and it is not what a home audit is); it delivers the inspection-versus-check-in reframe (an inspection is done to people to catch them out, a check-in is done for a home to see how it is holding up, and it can be the same walk through the same rooms and feel like the opposite thing); it delivers the definition (a 6S audit at home is a quick, regular, friendly walk through the house, a health check, not a test) and the four things that turn an inspection into a check-in (the home not the people, light and quick, regular and expected, notices what works); it delivers the no-blame move (keep it about the shelf, because blame makes people hide the mess before audit day); and it delivers the timing logic (a drift caught this week is a two-minute fix, the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend, so look often and lightly, reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit, and let each person check their own zone). It reads the friction meter as a small FIRST Sustain step, not a milestone: the needle takes its first move since Standardize, a short step along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26, because a checked home needs a little less minding. Five of six S's are done; the goal is reached only at Chapter 30.

## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Names why the word audit makes people flinch and takes it apart: it arrives carrying tax inspectors, clipboards, and grades, but that is inspection, and a home audit is the opposite, a check-in that is care rather than judgment
- Draws the exact line between an inspection and a check-in: the mechanical act can be identical, someone walks the house and looks, but an inspection points its attention at people and asks who failed, while a check-in points it at the home and asks how the house is holding up
- Gives the four things that turn an inspection into a check-in plainly: it looks at the home not the people, it is light and quick, it is regular and expected, and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped
- Makes blamelessness the practical core, not a nicety: the moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip it dies, because blame makes a household defensive and teaches people to hide the mess before audit day, so keeping it about the shelf is what keeps the audit honest and useful
- Reframes the point of a regular check as timing: a drift caught this week is a two-minute fix, the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend, so you look often and lightly to spare the exhausting overhaul
- Keeps the check short enough to survive: a five-minute check every week or two beats a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening, and it can reuse the simple snapshot from the baseline audit
- Turns being inspected into taking pride in your own patch: let each person check their own zone, and people turn out to be honest about their own drift when nobody is grading them
- Reads the friction meter honestly as a small first Sustain step, not a milestone: the needle moves a little for the first time since Standardize because a checked home needs less minding, though the audit clears nothing on its own
- Keeps the register warm, kind, and anti-shame, never punitive or surveillant: drift is normal and expected, finding drift is a success of the audit, not a failure of the household, and an audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family
