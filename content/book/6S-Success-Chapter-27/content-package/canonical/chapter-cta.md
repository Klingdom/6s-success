# Chapter 27 Calls to Action: 6S Audits That Do Not Feel Like Inspections

This is the second chapter of Part Eight, and it gives the first concrete way to keep the gains that Chapter 26 promised: a regular check-in on the home. Its strongest CTA is the smallest possible proof of the whole idea: do one two-minute audit right now, walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, what has drifted since we set this up, and fix the first small thing you find, because that is a whole audit and it was not frightening at all. The CTAs match the chapter's tone, warm and kind and de-frightening, never corporate and never punitive: an audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family, so it checks the home and never the household. The move costs about two minutes, because the whole point is that the check-in is light, regular, and owned, small enough to actually keep happening.

## In-chapter actions a reader can take (from the callouts)
- **Quick Win:** Do one two-minute audit right now. Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, "what has drifted since we set this up?" Fix the first small thing you find. That is a whole audit, and it was not frightening at all.
- **Family Challenge:** Let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's. A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent, and people turn out to be honest about their own drift when nobody is grading them.
- **6S Tip:** Keep it short and regular. A five-minute check every week catches far more than a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening. You can reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit; you do not need a new instrument.

## CTA writing notes
- The action is a small, provable move that demonstrates the whole chapter: do one two-minute audit of a single room, ask what has drifted of the room and not of anyone in it, and fix the first small thing. Lead with it, which is concrete and doable in about two minutes.
- Keep it non-prescriptive and anti-shame, in a register of warmth and kindness. Drift is normal and expected, which is the whole reason for a regular check-in; finding drift is a success of the audit, not a failure of the household. Never scold the reader or imply their family is being judged.
- The rule (an audit checks the home, not the household, what it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame), the inspection-versus-check-in reframe, and the catch-it-early reframe are the most memorable, shareable lines. Lead social with them.
- Avoid corporate framing, hype, and any force, control, or surveillance language (no "crack down," "hold people accountable" in the punitive sense, "keep tabs on," "no hiding," unlock, leverage, elevate, "audit-proof"). Use "inspection" only as the negated thing the audit is not. The audit is care, not inspection or surveillance or judgment. Avoid brand names.
- Scope note: this gives the first concrete way to keep the gains, the recurring, drift-catching check-in, and its work is the REFRAME (not an inspection) and the routine. It does not re-teach the one-time baseline audit from Chapter 5 (the 6S Snapshot radar, the 0 to 5 scoring, the out-of-30 baseline), which it references in one light line only; it does not build the family problem-solving method (Chapter 28), the daily-times system (Chapter 29), or the recurring 6S event (Chapter 30); and it does not re-teach Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, or Standardize. Because a home with a regular, kindly check-in needs a little less minding, the friction meter takes its FIRST Sustain step, a small move along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26; the goal is reached only at Chapter 30, when all six S's are done.
