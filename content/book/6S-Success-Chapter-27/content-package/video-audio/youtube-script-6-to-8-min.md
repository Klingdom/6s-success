## SECTION 1: WHY "AUDIT" MAKES EVERYONE FLINCH

[HOST]
It is worth naming precisely what the word carries, because until you disarm it, everything else here will land wrong.

[VISUAL: The word audit arriving with its suitcases, tax inspectors and clipboards and grades, someone official deciding whether you passed. Then a hand feeling a child's forehead for a temperature, warm and unworried.]

[HOST, voice over]
When most people hear that they should audit their home, some part of them braces, as if they are about to be tested on it, and that bracing is the enemy. Here is the thing to see: the mechanical act can be identical. Someone walks through the house and looks at how things are. What changes everything is the orientation of the looking. An inspection points its attention at people and asks who has failed to keep this up, so it produces defensiveness, hiding, and resentment. A check-in points its attention at the home and asks how the house is holding up, so it produces small, cheerful fixes and a household that does not mind at all.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: One is looking for fault. The other is only looking for drift.]

[HOST, on camera]
Here is the line that holds the whole reframe. An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift. A home audit is closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit. It is an act of care, not an act of judgment.

## SECTION 2: A HEALTH CHECK FOR THE HOUSE

[HOST]
So here, stated plainly and with the dread removed, is what you are actually doing.

[VISUAL: A short, warm walk through the house at ease. Then the four small things drawn as four clean warm items, each with an icon: a small house, a little clock, a calendar, a green tick beside a note. "INSPECTION" greyed on the left, an arrow, "CHECK-IN" warm on the right.]

[HOST, voice over]
A 6S audit at home is a quick, regular, friendly walk through the house to see how the gains are holding up: what is still working, and where small drift has crept in, so you can fix it while it is small. It checks the home, not the people, and it is a health check, not a test. You already own an instrument for it, if you want one, in the simple snapshot you took at the start to get your baseline. But the instrument matters far less than the spirit, and the spirit is four small things.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The home, not the people. Light and quick. Regular and expected. Notices what works.]

[HOST, on camera]
What turns an inspection into a check-in: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped. Each one defuses a different part of the dread. It looks at the home, so no one is on trial. It is light and quick, so it is never a burden. It is regular and expected, so it is never an ambush. And it notices what works, so people leave it encouraged rather than scolded. Miss any one, and the audit tips back toward inspection. Hold all four, and a frightening word becomes an easy, welcome habit of care.

## SECTION 3: LOOK AT THE HOME, NOT THE HOUSEHOLD

[HOST]
Of those four, the first is the one everything else hangs on, and it is where good intentions most often go wrong.

[VISUAL: The defensive household going quiet, the mess hidden before audit day, a show of order put on for the inspection. Then the attention turning off the roommate and onto the shelf, and a drifted shelf topped up like a low tyre.]

[HOST, voice over]
The whole practice lives or dies on keeping the attention on the room and off the roommate. The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected. Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right. And this is not soft-heartedness, it is what works. The instant you assign blame, people get defensive and go quiet about drift, so you lose the very information the audit exists to gather. And worse, they start hiding the mess before audit day, so you train your family to fake a clean house on a schedule.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Keep it about the shelf.]

[HOST, on camera]
Keep it about the shelf, and none of that happens. A drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, like a low tyre, and you simply top it up. The blamelessness is not a nicety. It is the thing that keeps the audit honest, and therefore useful.

## SECTION 4: CATCH IT EARLY, KEEP IT LIGHT

[HOST]
The last thing to understand is why you do this regularly and keep it small, and the answer is entirely about timing.

[VISUAL: Drift compounding, one thing in the wrong place inviting a second. Then the same drift at two ages: a two-minute fix this week, beside a lost weekend six months on. Then the light audit walk through the zones, green "holding" ticks and one honey "drifted, quick fix" note, a few minutes on the clock.]

[HOST, voice over]
In a home the cost of a problem depends almost completely on how long it has been left. The whole value of a regular check-in is timing. A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. You do not audit the home to judge it. You audit it so that it never again has to be rescued. Because drift compounds: one thing left in the wrong place invites a second, and a system used slightly wrong quietly becomes used wrong by default. So you look often and lightly.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix.]

[HOST, on camera]
A five-minute check every week or two catches drift while it is still trivial, and a household that does that never again meets the exhausting overhaul. And keep it genuinely light, because only the light version survives. A short, low-key walk people can do forever beats a solemn reckoning they manage twice and then avoid. And if you want, let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's. That turns being inspected into taking quiet pride in your own patch, and people are remarkably honest about their own drift when nobody is standing over them with a pen.

## CLOSE AND SOFT CTA

So that is the first concrete way to keep the gains. And it is worth pausing on.

[B-ROLL: The friendly check-in as the first practice of keeping, the home catching its own drift while it is small. Then a soft look at the recurring problem the audit keeps turning up, the same thing drifting in the same place. Then a warm return to the home, kept and holding the household up.]

[HOST, voice over]
You started this part with only the mindset, that a home is kept and not finished. Now you have the first practice that puts it to work: a regular, friendly check-in that looks at the home, catches drift while it is small, and never once puts the household on trial. Run kindly and often, that single habit will spare you more dreaded overhauls than any amount of heroic cleaning. What the audit does not do, by design, is solve the bigger things it finds. Every so often it turns up a real, recurring problem, the same thing drifting in the same place month after month, and fixing those together, as a household, is a practice of its own.

[HOST, on camera]
So here is your job, and it is one of the smallest in the whole book. Do one two-minute audit right now. Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, "what has drifted since we set this up?" Fix the first small thing you find. That is a whole audit, and it was not frightening at all. And do not make yourself the household inspector. Let each person check their own zone. A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent.
