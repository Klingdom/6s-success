## Post 1 · A home audit is not an inspection

The word audit makes almost everyone tense up. It arrives carrying suitcases: tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed. If that is what checking on your home meant, you should absolutely skip it.

"A home audit is not an inspection."

It is a check-in instead, and the difference is everything.

"An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift."

## Post 2 · Check the home, not the people

Here is the one thing the whole practice lives or dies on: keep your attention on the room and off the roommate. The moment a check-in becomes about who let the shelf slip, it is over.

"An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame."

A shelf that drifted is not a flaw in anyone's character. It is a small system that needs a small nudge.

"Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right."

And the payoff is real: nobody gets defensive, nobody hides the mess before check-in day, so the home stays honest and easy to keep. An audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family. Read the free chapter.

## Post 3 · Catch it early, while it is still tiny

The entire reason for a regular check-in comes down to one thing: timing.

"The whole value of a regular check-in is timing. A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. You do not audit the home to judge it. You audit it so that it never again has to be rescued."

Drift compounds. One thing left in the wrong place invites a second. A system used slightly wrong quietly becomes used wrong by default. Caught in its first week, almost any drift is a shrug and a small correction. Left for half a year, it has hardened into the new normal.

So you look often and lightly, and the dreaded overhaul simply never has to happen. More in Chapter 27 of 6S Success: Home Edition, free to read online.

## Post 4 · Keep it short, and let everyone check their own patch

If a check-in sounds like a chore, here is the good news: the only version that works is the tiny one.

"Keep it short and regular. A five-minute check every week catches far more than a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening. You can reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit; you do not need a new instrument."

And it is not meant to rest on one person walking around judging everyone else. The kindest version hands each person their own zone.

"Let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's. A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent, and people turn out to be honest about their own drift when nobody is grading them."

Taking quiet pride in your own patch beats being inspected, every time. This is from Chapter 27 of 6S Success: Home Edition. Read it free online.

One quiet note to close on. Go and look at the friction meter. For the first time since Standardize, the needle actually moves.

"The needle takes its first Sustain step, a small move along the dashed path to the goal that appeared last chapter. It is honestly small. A check-in scrubs no surface and removes no clutter on its own. What it does is make the home a little more able to keep itself.

"a home that catches its own drift early is a home that needs less minding to stay good."
