### Slide 2 · A home audit is not an inspection

- You have the keeping mindset now, from the last chapter: a home is kept rather than finished, on small shared habits, until keeping becomes second nature. That is the ground. This chapter lays the first stone on it, the first concrete, repeatable thing you actually do to keep the gains, and it is the humble, powerful practice of looking. A home holds its shape when someone, now and then, simply looks at it with clear eyes and notices what has begun to slip, while the slipping is still small and a two-minute matter.
- The trouble is only the name, which in 6S is the audit, and audit is a word that makes almost everyone tense up. It arrives carrying suitcases: tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed. If that were what an audit meant for your home you should skip it, because a household will not, and should not, submit itself to regular judgment, and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial gets quietly abandoned within a month, with relief.
- So the first and most important job of this chapter is to take that word apart and hand it back meaning something completely different. Verbatim: "A home audit is not an inspection." It is a check-in, closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit, an act of care rather than judgment. Verbatim: "Get that distinction right and the audit becomes something a whole household will happily do. Get it wrong and it dies on contact."

### Slide 4 · Why "audit" makes everyone flinch

- Name precisely what the word carries, because until you disarm it, everything else in the chapter will land wrong. When most people hear that they should audit their home, some part of them braces, as if they are about to be tested on it, and that bracing is the enemy. The mechanical act can be identical: someone walks through the house and looks at how things are. What changes everything is the orientation of the looking.
- An inspection points its attention at people and asks who has failed to keep this up, so it produces defensiveness, hiding, and resentment, which is exactly why inspections make homes worse over time, not better. A check-in points its attention at the home and asks how the house is holding up, so it produces small, cheerful fixes and a household that does not mind at all. It is the difference between a doctor examining you kindly to keep you well and an examiner grading you to decide if you are good enough, and your body knows the difference the instant it walks in the room. Your family knows it too.
- The inspection-versus-check-in reframe, frozen: "An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift."

### Slide 5 · A health check for the house

- Stated plainly and with the dread removed, here is what you are actually doing: a short, warm, regular look at the house. You already own an instrument for it, if you want one, in the simple snapshot you took right at the start to get your baseline; you can walk the same quick pass now, not to score where you began but to notice what has moved since. But the instrument matters far less than the spirit, and the spirit is captured in four small things that are the whole difference between an audit people run happily for years and one that dies after two tries.
- Take those four in turn, because each one defuses a different part of the dread. It looks at the home, not the people, so no one is on trial. It is light and quick, so it never becomes a burden anyone avoids. It is regular and expected, so it carries no ambush and no fear. And it notices what is working, so people leave it encouraged rather than scolded. Miss any one and the audit tips back toward inspection; hold all four and it becomes an easy, welcome habit of care.

### Slide 6 · Look at the home, not the household

- Of those four, the first is the one everything else hangs on, and it is worth its own moment, because it is where good intentions most often go wrong. The whole practice lives or dies on keeping the attention on the room and off the roommate. This is not soft-heartedness; it is what actually works. The instant an audit starts assigning blame, two things happen, and both of them wreck it.
- First, people become defensive, and a defensive household stops being honest about drift, so the audit loses the very information it exists to gather. Second, and worse, people learn that mess is punished, and respond not by keeping the home better but by hiding the mess before audit day and putting on a show of order for the inspection, so you train your family to fake a clean house on a schedule, precisely the opposite of what you wanted. Keep it about the shelf and none of that happens. A drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, like a low tyre, and you simply top it up. The blamelessness is not a nicety; it is the thing that keeps the audit honest and therefore useful.
- The no-blame reframe, frozen: "The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected. Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right."

### Slide 7 · Catch it early, keep it light

- The last thing to understand is why you do this regularly and keep it small, and the answer is entirely about timing, because in a home the cost of a problem depends almost completely on how long it has been left. Drift compounds: one thing left in the wrong place invites a second, and a system used slightly wrong a few times quietly becomes used wrong by default. Caught in its first week, almost any drift is a shrug and a small correction; left for half a year, the same drift has recruited friends and hardened into the new normal, and undoing it is a whole dreaded project of the kind Sustain exists to make unnecessary.
- So you look often and lightly, because a five-minute check every week or two catches drift while it is still trivial, and a household that does that never again meets the exhausting overhaul. And keep it genuinely light, because only the light version survives: a short, low-key walk people can do forever beats a thorough, solemn reckoning they manage twice and then avoid. If you want, let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's, which turns the whole thing from being inspected into taking quiet pride in your own patch.
- The catch-it-early reframe, frozen: "The whole value of a regular check-in is timing. A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. You do not audit the home to judge it. You audit it so that it never again has to be rescued." Quick Win, verbatim: "Do one two-minute audit right now. Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, "what has drifted since we set this up?" Fix the first small thing you find. That is a whole audit, and it was not frightening at all." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Keep it short and regular. A five-minute check every week catches far more than a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening. You can reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit; you do not need a new instrument."

### Slide 8 · The rule

- That is the whole chapter, and it fits in a single line. Point the looking at the home, treat every drift as a small system to top up, keep it light and regular and owned, and a frightening word becomes an easy, welcome habit of care. The blamelessness is not softness; it is the thing that keeps the audit honest, because a household that is not on trial will actually tell you where the drift is.
- Held rightly, the audit is care, not judgment. It is not a test you pass or fail; it is the kind, ordinary looking that keeps a good home good, so that it never again drifts far enough to need rescuing. And the surest way to keep it kind is to make the check-in a shared thing the household owns together, not one person quietly grading the rest.
- The rule, frozen, the hero: "An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame."

Fixing those, together, as a household, instead of one person forever patching them alone, is a practice of its own, and it is the next chapter.
- Past that lies folding the keeping into the ordinary shape of a day, and then the recurring event that renews the whole thing and completes the book. For now, hold the one idea this chapter turns on, which is also the warmest way to think about the whole practice: an audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family.
