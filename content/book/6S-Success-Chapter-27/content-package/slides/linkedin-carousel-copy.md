### Slide 1 (hook)

**The same walk through the same rooms, twice. One feels like a trial. One feels like care. Nothing about the house changed.**

You have the keeping mindset now: a home is kept, not finished, on small shared habits. Here is the first concrete thing you actually do to keep it, and it is the humblest practice of all. Someone, now and then, simply looks at the home with clear eyes and notices what has begun to slip, while the slipping is still small and a two-minute matter.

The trouble is only the name. In 6S it is called the audit, and audit is a word that makes almost everyone brace, as if they are about to be tested on their own house. But an audit and an inspection can be the exact same walk through the exact same rooms. The only difference, and it is everything, is whether it looks for fault in the people or drift in the home. Swipe.

### Slide 2

**A home audit is not an inspection.**

The line the whole chapter turns on:

"A home audit is not an inspection."

It is a check-in: a quick, regular, warm look at the house, not the people, to see how the good things you built are holding up and to catch the small drift before it becomes a large mess. It is closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit, an act of care rather than an act of judgment. And the distinction is not a nicety, it is the whole game: get it right and the audit becomes something a whole household will happily do; get it wrong and it dies on contact.

The book line, verbatim:

"Get that distinction right and the audit becomes something a whole household will happily do. Get it wrong and it dies on contact."

### Slide 3

**The word audit is the whole obstacle. So the first job is to disarm it.**

Audit arrives carrying suitcases: tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed, the cold feeling of being examined and found wanting. If that were what an audit meant for your home, you should skip it, because a household will not, and should not, submit itself to regular judgment, and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial gets quietly abandoned within a month, with relief.

So take the word apart and hand it back meaning something completely different. The mechanical act can be identical: someone walks through the house and looks at how things are. What changes everything is the orientation of the looking. An inspection points its attention at people and asks who has failed, so it produces defensiveness, hiding, and resentment, which is exactly why inspections make homes worse over time. A check-in points its attention at the home, so it produces small, cheerful fixes and a household that does not mind at all.

The book line, verbatim:

"A check-in points its attention at the home and asks how the house is holding up, so it produces small, cheerful fixes and a household that does not mind at all."

### Slide 4

**An inspection is done to people. A check-in is done for a home. It can be the same walk, and feel like the opposite thing.**

The reframe the whole chapter holds, frozen:

"An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift."

Read that carefully, because it holds the whole reframe. One is looking for fault in the people; the other is only looking for drift in the home. Everything else in the chapter is a way of keeping the second and never sliding into the first.

### Slide 6

**Four small things turn a dreaded inspection into a welcome check-in.**

The four things, frozen:

"What turns an inspection into a check-in: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped."

Each one defuses a different part of the dread. The home, not the people, so no one is on trial. Light and quick, so it never becomes a burden anyone avoids. Regular and expected, so it carries no ambush and no fear. And it notices what is working, so people leave it encouraged rather than scolded. Miss any one and the audit tips back toward inspection; hold all four and it becomes an easy, welcome habit of care.

### Slide 7

**Of the four, one is everything: look at the home, not the household.**

The whole practice lives or dies on keeping the attention on the room and off the roommate. This is not soft-heartedness; it is what actually works. The instant an audit starts assigning blame, two things happen, and both wreck it: people become defensive and stop being honest about drift, so the audit loses the very information it exists to gather; and people learn that mess is punished, so they hide it before audit day and put on a show of order, and you have trained the household to fake a clean house on a schedule.

The no-blame reframe, frozen:

"The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected. Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right."

The book line, verbatim:

"A drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, like a low tyre, and you simply top it up."

### Slide 8

**A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix. The same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend.**

The catch-it-early reframe, frozen:

"The whole value of a regular check-in is timing. A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. You do not audit the home to judge it. You audit it so that it never again has to be rescued."

Drift compounds: one thing left in the wrong place invites a second, and a system used slightly wrong quietly becomes used wrong by default. Caught in its first week, almost any drift is a shrug and a small correction; left for half a year, it has hardened into the new normal. So you look often and lightly, because only the light version survives.

Quick Win: "Do one two-minute audit right now. Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, "what has drifted since we set this up?" Fix the first small thing you find. That is a whole audit, and it was not frightening at all."

6S Tip: "Keep it short and regular. A five-minute check every week catches far more than a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening. You can reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit; you do not need a new instrument."

### Slide 9

**An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame.**

The rule, frozen, the hero of the chapter:

"An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame."

### Slide 10

**Let each person check their own zone. A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent.**

The gentlest way to keep the looking off the people is to hand it to them. Let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's, and the whole thing turns from being inspected into taking quiet pride in your own patch. People are remarkably honest about their own drift when nobody is standing over them with a pen, and a household that owns its own check-ins never feels graded at all.

Family Challenge: "Let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's. A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent, and people turn out to be honest about their own drift when nobody is grading them."

### Slide 12 (soft CTA)

**Keeping has begun. You have the mindset, and now the first mechanism: a check-in, not an inspection.**

You have taken the first real step of keeping. You started with the mindset, that a home is kept and not finished, and now you have the first practice that puts it to work: a regular, friendly check-in that looks at the home, catches drift while it is small, and never once puts the household on trial. Run kindly and often, that single habit spares you more dreaded overhauls than any amount of heroic cleaning. What the audit does not do, by design, is solve the bigger recurring problems it turns up, and that is the next chapter, then a daily rhythm, then a recurring event that completes the book.

Common Mistake: "Turning the audit into a performance review of the people, with scores held over someone's head. The instant it feels like judgment, everyone starts hiding the mess before the audit instead of fixing it, and you have taught the household to fake a clean home for inspection day. Check the home, kindly, and never the person."

One Idea to Keep, frozen: "Keep the gains with a light, regular check-in that looks at the home and never at the people. A drift caught small is a small fix; a drift left to grow is an overhaul. Audit the house kindly and often, and it will never again need rescuing."
