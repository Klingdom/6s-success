## 1. The first thing you actually do to keep it

You built the good home, and with Chapter 26 you took on the mindset that keeps it: a home is kept, not finished.

Now here is the first concrete thing you actually do to keep it. You look.

A home holds its shape when someone, now and then, simply looks at it with clear eyes and notices what has begun to slip, while the slipping is still small.

In 6S we call that looking an audit. And there is the problem, because the word makes almost everyone flinch.

So before anything else, one promise. A home audit is not an inspection. It is a check-in, closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit.

## 2. Why "audit" makes everyone flinch

Say the word audit and watch people brace.

It arrives carrying suitcases: tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed. The cold feeling of being examined and found wanting.

If that is what an audit meant for your home, you should skip it. A household will not, and should not, submit itself to regular judgment, and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial gets quietly abandoned within a month, with relief.

So the first job is to take the word apart and hand it back meaning something completely different. Not a test with a grade. A check-in: a quick, warm look at how the house is holding up.

A home audit is not an inspection. It is a check-in: a quick, regular, warm look at the house, not the people. Read the free chapter.

## 3. The same walk, two feelings

Picture the same short walk through the same three or four rooms, made twice.

The first time it is an inspection: a stern figure with a clipboard and a red pen, ticking and crossing, while the family stands stiffly to attention and a child half-hides a toy.

The second time it is a check-in: a warm figure giving small green ticks to what is holding up, quietly jotting one small drift, the family loose and even helping.

Nothing about the rooms changed. Everything about the feeling did.

An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up. Same walk, opposite thing, because one looks for fault and the other only for drift.

## 4. Check the home, not the household

Of everything in this chapter, one line carries the rest.

An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame.

Keep the attention on the room and off the roommate. The instant an audit starts assigning blame, two things go wrong. People go defensive, and a defensive household stops being honest about drift, so the audit loses the very thing it exists to gather. And people learn that mess is punished, so they tidy it away before audit day, and you have taught the family to fake a clean house on a schedule.

Keep it about the shelf, and none of that happens.

An audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family. Read the free chapter.

## 5. Keep it about the shelf

A shelf drifted. A couple of things landed there that do not belong.

You have two ways to see that. As someone's failure, or as a low tyre.

Choose the tyre. A drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, and you simply top it up. It is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right.

The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected.

The blamelessness is not a nicety. It is the thing that keeps the audit honest, and therefore useful.

Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment. Read the free chapter.

## 6. Four things turn an inspection into a check-in

What turns a frightening word into an easy, welcome habit? Four small things.

What turns an inspection into a check-in: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped.

Each one defuses a different part of the dread. Look at the home, so no one is on trial. Keep it light and quick, so it never becomes a burden anyone avoids. Make it regular and expected, so it carries no ambush and no fear. And notice what is working, so people leave it encouraged rather than scolded.

Miss any one and the audit tips back toward inspection. Hold all four and it becomes a habit of care.

## 7. Catch it early

Why look regularly, and why keep it small? It is all about timing.

In a home, the cost of a problem depends almost entirely on how long it has been left. Drift compounds: one thing left in the wrong place invites a second, and a system used slightly wrong quietly becomes used wrong by default.

Caught in its first week, almost any drift is a shrug and a small correction. Left for half a year, it has recruited friends and hardened into the new normal, and undoing it is a whole dreaded project.

So you look often and lightly, and the exhausting overhaul never arrives.

A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. Read the free chapter.

## 8. Keep it short and regular

The thorough, solemn audit you manage twice and then dread keeps nothing.

Only the light version survives. A short, low-key walk that people can do forever beats a two-hour reckoning once a year, because the small one actually keeps happening.

A five-minute check every week or two catches drift while it is still trivial. And you do not need a new instrument for it: you can reuse the simple snapshot you took at the start, walking the same quick pass, not to score where you began but to notice what has moved since.

Keep it genuinely light. The dull, small, regular version wins, every time, because it is the one that lasts.

## 9. Let each person check their own zone

Here is the move that takes the last of the sting out.

Let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's.

A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent. It turns the whole thing from being inspected into taking quiet pride in your own patch, and people are remarkably honest about their own drift when nobody is standing over them with a pen.

Nobody is grading anyone. Each person just glances at their own corner, notices the one thing that has slipped, and sets it right. That is the difference between a household that dreads audit day and one that barely notices it happened.
