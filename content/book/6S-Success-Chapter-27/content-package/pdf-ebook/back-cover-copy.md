# Back Cover Copy: 6S Audits That Do Not Feel Like Inspections

## Back-cover text

You have the keeping mindset now. Chapter 26 gave it: a home is kept and not finished, held good over the years by small, shared habits, until keeping becomes second nature. What it did not give you was a single concrete thing to actually do. This chapter lays the first stone on that mindset, the first repeatable practice of keeping, and it is the humblest one imaginable, which is simply looking. A home holds its shape when someone, now and then, looks at it with clear eyes and catches what has begun to slip while the slipping is still small and a two-minute matter. In 6S the name for that looking is the audit, and there is the trouble, because the word makes almost everyone flinch: it arrives carrying tax inspectors and clipboards and grades, and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial gets quietly abandoned within a month. So the whole work of this chapter is to take the word apart and hand it back meaning something completely different. "A home audit is not an inspection."

Chapter 27 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the second chapter of Part Eight, and it gives the first concrete way to keep the gains that Chapter 26 promised: a regular check-in on the home that catches drift while it is still small. Its whole work is a reframe. It names why the word makes people flinch, then draws the exact line between the two things it could mean: "An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift." Then it delivers the hero, a health check for the house, in a plain definition: "A 6S audit at home is a quick, regular, friendly walk through the house to see how the gains are holding up: what is still working, and where small drift has crept in, so you can fix it while it is small. It checks the home, not the people, and it is a health check, not a test." Four small things turn an inspection into a check-in: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped. The one everything hangs on is the first, and the chapter states it as the rule of the whole practice: "An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame." Blame does not just feel bad; it wrecks the audit, because the moment it becomes about who let the shelf slip, people learn to hide the mess before audit day instead of fixing it. And the reason you look often and keep it light is entirely timing: "A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend." Run the check-in without it ever feeling like an inspection with **The Home Check-In card**, and keep it short enough to actually keep happening with **The Quick Audit Walk sheet**. Then go and look at the friction meter, which moves again, just a little, for the first time since Standardize: the needle takes its first Sustain step, a short move along the dashed last-gap path that appeared last chapter, because a home with a regular, kindly check-in needs a little less minding. It is honestly small, because an audit clears nothing on its own; what it does is make the home a little more able to keep itself. Next, Chapter 27 hands off to Chapter 28 · Family Problem Solving, where the household gets a simple, shared way to solve the bigger, recurring problems the audit keeps turning up, together, instead of one person forever patching them alone.
