# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: 6S Audits That Do Not Feel Like Inspections

*Chapter 27, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter turns on a single reframe: "A home audit is not an inspection." When you first read the word audit, what did it make you brace for, and how does it feel to have that word handed back meaning a kindness rather than a test?

2. The inspection-versus-check-in reframe draws the line exactly: "An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early." Where in your life has the same act felt completely different depending only on whether it was aimed at you or done for you?

3. The chapter says the mechanical act can be identical, and only the aim changes: "A check-in points its attention at the home and asks how the house is holding up, so it produces small, cheerful fixes and a household that does not mind at all." Think of a time someone checked on your home or your work. Was it pointed at the house or at you, and how could you tell?

4. The definition box strips the dread off the word: "A 6S audit at home is a quick, regular, friendly walk through the house to see how the gains are holding up: what is still working, and where small drift has crept in, so you can fix it while it is small. It checks the home, not the people, and it is a health check, not a test." What would it take, in your home, for a walk-through to feel like a health check rather than a test?

5. Four small things turn an inspection into a check-in: "it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped." Which of the four do you think you would find hardest to hold, and which would matter most in your household?

6. The rule of the whole practice is short: "An audit checks the home, not the household. What it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame." Where do you catch yourself turning a drifted shelf into a question about who let it slip, and what would it change to keep it about the shelf?

7. The no-blame reframe explains why blame is fatal, not just unkind: "The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected. Keep it about the shelf." Have you ever watched a household or a team start hiding problems because finding them got someone in trouble?

8. The chapter offers a plain image for a drifted system: "A drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, like a low tyre, and you simply top it up." What is one drift in your home you could treat as a low tyre rather than a failing, and does seeing it that way make it easier to fix?

9. The catch-it-early reframe is all about timing: "A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend." Which drift in your home was once a two-minute fix and became a lost weekend because it was left, and what might have caught it early?

10. The 6S Tip keeps it survivable: "Keep it short and regular. A five-minute check every week catches far more than a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening." What is the shortest, most regular check-in you could imagine actually keeping up, and does making it smaller feel like a relief or a compromise?

11. The Family Challenge points at ownership rather than oversight: "Let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's. A self-audit you own beats an inspection you resent." Who in your home has quietly been the one checking on everyone else, and what might change if each person checked their own patch?

12. The Common Mistake names the exact way it goes wrong: "The instant it feels like judgment, everyone starts hiding the mess before the audit instead of fixing it, and you have taught the household to fake a clean home for inspection day." Where have you seen a check turn into a performance, with the real state of things hidden just before it?

13. The Quick Win is the smallest possible proof: "Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, "what has drifted since we set this up?" Fix the first small thing you find." Which room would you walk, and could you do it before this discussion ends?

14. The chapter promises the deeper payoff of catching drift small: "a home that catches its own drift early is a home that needs less minding to stay good." Where in your home would catching things early mean less minding overall, not more, and what would that free you from?

15. The friction meter takes its first Sustain step: "The needle takes its first Sustain step, a small move along the dashed path to the goal that appeared last chapter. A home with a regular, kindly check-in needs a little less minding." With five of the six S's done and keeping only just begun, how does it feel that the first mechanism moves the needle only a little, and honestly so?

16. The One Idea to Keep closes the chapter: "an audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family." What would it take for a check-in on your home to feel, to everyone in it, like a kindness rather than a judgment?
