# 6S Audits That Do Not Feel Like Inspections

**Part Eight, Sustain**
Chapter 27 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

Subtitle · The word audit makes everyone flinch, because it sounds like a test with an inspector and a grade. A home audit is the opposite: a quick, regular, friendly look at the house, not the people, to see what is still working and catch small drift before it grows. It checks the home, never the household, so a drift it finds is a system to fix, not a person to blame.

---

## Chapter descriptor

The second chapter of Part Eight, and it gives the first concrete way to keep the gains that Chapter 26 promised. Chapter 26 opened Sustain, the sixth and final S, with its concept and its mindset: a home is kept and not finished, held over the years by small, shared habits, until keeping becomes second nature. This chapter lays the first stone on that mindset, the first repeatable practice of keeping, and it is the humblest one imaginable, which is simply looking. A home holds its shape when someone, now and then, looks at it with clear eyes and catches what has begun to slip while the slipping is still small. In 6S the name for that looking is the audit, and there is the whole obstacle, because the word makes almost everyone tense up: it arrives carrying tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed, and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial gets quietly abandoned within a month. So the first and most important job of this chapter is a reframe, taking the word apart and handing it back meaning something completely different: a home audit is not an inspection, it is a check-in, closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit, an act of care rather than judgment. It draws the exact line between them, because the mechanical act can be identical, someone walks the house and looks, but an inspection points its attention at people and asks who has failed, while a check-in points it at the home and asks how the house is holding up, so one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift. Then it delivers the hero, a health check for the house, in a plain definition box, and the four small things that turn an inspection into a check-in, each of which defuses a different part of the dread: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped. The one everything hangs on is the first, and it becomes the rule of the whole practice: an audit checks the home, not the household, and what it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame, because the moment the audit becomes about who let the shelf slip it dies, and blame teaches people to hide the mess before audit day instead of fixing it. And the reason you look often and keep it light is entirely timing, because drift compounds: a drift caught this week is a two-minute fix, and the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend, so you look often and lightly, keep it genuinely light because only the light version survives, reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit rather than build a new instrument, and let each person check their own zone. Then the chapter reads the friction meter as a small first Sustain step, not a milestone: the needle moves for the first time since Standardize, a short move along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26, because a home with a regular, kindly check-in needs a little less minding, though the move is honestly small, because an audit clears nothing on its own; it scrubs no surface and removes no clutter, but it makes the home a little more able to keep itself. It hands off to the rest of Part Eight before Chapter 28. Five of the six S's are done, Sustain has begun and keeping is under way, and the goal, its dashed path a little shorter now, is reached only at the very end of the book, when all six S's are done.

## How to read this chapter

This is the second chapter of Part Eight, Sustain, and it gives the first concrete way to keep the gains. It picks up exactly where Chapter 26 left off: you have the keeping mindset, that a home is kept and not finished, held by small, shared habits until keeping becomes second nature. What you did not yet have was a single concrete thing to do, and this chapter hands you the first, the humble, powerful practice of looking. Read it through once for the reframe, that a home audit is not an inspection but a check-in, a kindness you do for your home rather than a judgment you pass on your family, then do the one small, concrete thing it asks: walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, what has drifted since you set it up, and fix the first small thing you find.

The chapter rests on a plain reframe. Audit is a word that makes almost everyone flinch, because it arrives sounding like a test with an inspector and a grade, but that is inspection, and it is not what a home audit is. An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them; a check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early, and it can be the same walk through the same rooms and feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift. The register throughout is warm, kind, and de-frightening: the audit is care, and nobody is on trial. There is no force in it and nothing to enforce, no surveillance and no grading, only a little affectionate attention to the home, so drift is normal and expected, finding drift is a success of the audit and never a failure of the household, and the chapter never once scolds.

A word on the scope fence, so the chapter stays the recurring, drift-catching check-in and the reframe that makes it feel like care, rather than drifting into the mechanisms that follow or back into the earlier S's. It is distinct from the one-time baseline audit of Chapter 5: that chapter, The 6S Home Audit, introduced the audit as a single starting snapshot, the 6S Snapshot radar, each S scored and totalled to fix where you began. This chapter is the recurring, drift-catching check-in you run again and again to keep the gains, so it references that baseline in one light line only, that you can reuse its simple snapshot if you want one, and it does not re-teach the radar, the scoring, or the out-of-30 baseline. It uses the keeping mindset Chapter 26 built without re-teaching it. And it fixes the small drift it finds on the spot, but it does not build the mechanisms that come next: the audit turns up bigger, recurring problems, and solving those together as a household is Chapter 28, Family Problem Solving; folding the keeping into the ordinary shape of a day is Chapter 29, Daily 6S Times; and the recurring event that resets and renews the whole thing, and completes the book, is Chapter 30, Your Next 6S Event. It does not re-open Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, or Standardize; a one-line callback is fine, but the earlier S's are not re-taught here. And because a home with a regular, kindly check-in needs a little less minding, the friction meter takes its first Sustain step, a small move along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26, from where the needle held toward the goal. This is a small move, not a milestone, and honestly so, because an audit clears nothing on its own; what it does is make the home a little more able to keep itself, because drift is now met while it is small instead of discovered when it is large. The goal is reached not in this chapter but at the very end of the book, at Chapter 30, when all six S's are done.

If you only do one thing: do one two-minute audit right now. Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, what has drifted since you set this up, and fix the first small thing you find. That is a whole audit, and it was not frightening at all. Not a solemn reckoning you dread and avoid, but a short, kind look you could do forever. That is the smallest possible proof of the whole chapter, and it is how keeping begins in practice. An audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family.
