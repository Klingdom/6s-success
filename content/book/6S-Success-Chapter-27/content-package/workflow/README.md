## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
You have the keeping mindset now, from Chapter 26, that a home is kept and not finished, on small shared habits, and here is the first concrete way to do the keeping: a regular check-in on the home that catches drift while it is still small. The obvious word for it is audit, and that word makes almost everyone tense up, because it arrives carrying tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed, the cold feeling of being examined and found wanting; and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial gets quietly abandoned within a month, with relief, so the first and most important job is to take the word apart and hand it back meaning something completely different. A home audit is not an inspection, it is a check-in, closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit, an act of care rather than judgment: an inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them, a check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early, and it can be the same walk through the same rooms and feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift. The hero is a health check for the house, a short, warm, regular look at the home, not a report card for the family; you already own an instrument for it, if you want one, in the simple snapshot you took right at the start to get your baseline, but the instrument matters far less than the spirit, and the spirit lives in four small things that turn an inspection into a check-in, each of which defuses a different part of the dread: it looks at the home, not the people, it is light and quick, it is regular and expected, and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped. The first of the four, the one everything hangs on, is the no-blame move: keep the attention on the room and off the roommate, because the instant an audit starts assigning blame the household becomes defensive and stops being honest about drift, and worse, people learn to hide the mess before audit day and put on a show of order for the inspection, so you train your family to fake a clean house on a schedule, precisely the opposite of what you wanted; keep it about the shelf and none of that happens, a drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, like a low tyre, and you simply top it up. The timing logic is catch it early and keep it light: drift compounds, so a drift caught this week is a two-minute fix and the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend, so you look often and lightly, a five-minute check every week or two, keep it genuinely light because only the light version survives, reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit rather than building a new instrument, and let each person check their own zone, because people are remarkably honest about their own drift when nobody is standing over them with a pen. The rule holds the whole chapter together: an audit checks the home, not the household, what it finds is always a system to fix, never a person to blame. The friction meter moves again for the first time since Standardize, but only a little and honestly so, a short step along the dashed last-gap path, because an audit does not clear a single thing on its own, it scrubs no surface and removes no clutter; what it does is subtler and, over years, larger, it makes the home a little more able to keep itself, because drift is now met while it is small instead of discovered when it is large. Hands off to Chapter 28 · Family Problem Solving: the friendly audit finds the drift and fixes the small things on the spot, and the next chapter gives the household a simple, shared way to solve the bigger, recurring problems the audit keeps turning up, together, instead of one person forever patching them alone.

- The definition box (frozen): **A 6S audit at home is a quick, regular, friendly walk through the house to see how the gains are holding up: what is still working, and where small drift has crept in, so you can fix it while it is small. It checks the home, not the people, and it is a health check, not a test.**
- The four things (frozen): **What turns an inspection into a check-in: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped.**
- The inspection-versus-check-in reframe (frozen): **An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift.**
- The no-blame reframe (frozen): **The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected. Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right.**
- The catch-it-early reframe (frozen): **The whole value of a regular check-in is timing. A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. You do not audit the home to judge it. You audit it so that it never again has to be rescued.**

A home with a regular, kindly check-in needs a little less minding, because drift is caught while it is small instead of found when it is large. The gap is shorter now, and the rest of it, the last of the whole journey, is what the chapters ahead close.**
- One Idea to Keep: **Keep the gains with a light, regular check-in that looks at the home and never at the people. A drift caught small is a small fix; a drift left to grow is an overhaul. Audit the house kindly and often, and it will never again need rescuing.**
