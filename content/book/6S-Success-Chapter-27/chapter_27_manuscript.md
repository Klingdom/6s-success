## Chapter Opening

You have the mindset now, from the last chapter: that a home is kept rather than finished, on small shared habits, until keeping becomes second nature. That is the ground. This chapter lays the first stone on it, the first concrete, repeatable thing you actually do to keep the gains, and it is the humble, powerful practice of looking. A home holds its shape when someone, now and then, simply looks at it with clear eyes and notices what has begun to slip, while the slipping is still small and a two-minute matter.

The trouble is the name for this, which in 6S is the audit, and audit is a word that makes almost everyone tense up. It arrives carrying suitcases: tax inspectors, surprise checks, someone official with a clipboard deciding whether you passed, the cold feeling of being examined and found wanting. If that is what an audit meant for your home, I would tell you to skip it, because a household will not, and should not, submit itself to regular judgment, and any keeping-practice that feels like being put on trial is one that quietly gets abandoned within a month, with relief.

So the first and most important job of this chapter is to take that word apart and hand it back to you meaning something completely different. A home audit is not an inspection. It is a check-in: a quick, regular, warm look at the house, not the people, to see how the good things you built are holding up and to catch the small drift before it becomes a large mess. It is closer to feeling a child's forehead for a temperature than to a tax audit, an act of care rather than an act of judgment. Get that distinction right and the audit becomes something a whole household will happily do. Get it wrong and it dies on contact.

## Why "Audit" Makes Everyone Flinch

It is worth naming precisely what the word carries, because until you disarm it, everything else in this chapter will land wrong. When most people hear that they should audit their home, some part of them braces, as if they are about to be tested on it, and that bracing is the enemy.

> An inspection is something done to people, to catch them out and grade them. A check-in is something done for a home, to see how it is holding up and help it early. It can be the same walk through the same rooms, and it will feel like the opposite thing, because one is looking for fault and the other is only looking for drift.

Read that carefully, because it holds the whole reframe. The mechanical act can be identical: someone walks through the house and looks at how things are. What changes everything is the orientation of the looking. An inspection points its attention at people and asks, in effect, who has failed to keep this up, so it produces defensiveness, hiding, and resentment, which is exactly why inspections make homes worse over time, not better. A check-in points its attention at the home and asks how the house is holding up, so it produces small, cheerful fixes and a household that does not mind at all. It is the difference between a doctor examining you kindly to keep you well and an examiner grading you to decide if you are good enough, and your body knows the difference the instant it walks in the room. Your family knows it too.

## A Health Check for the House

So here, stated plainly and with the dread removed, is what you are actually doing.

> A 6S audit at home is a quick, regular, friendly walk through the house to see how the gains are holding up: what is still working, and where small drift has crept in, so you can fix it while it is small. It checks the home, not the people, and it is a health check, not a test.

That is the entire practice: a short, warm, regular look at the house. You already own an instrument for it, if you want one, in the simple snapshot you took right at the start to get your baseline; you can walk the same quick pass now, not to score where you began but to notice what has moved since. But the instrument matters far less than the spirit, and the spirit is captured in four small things that, between them, are the whole difference between an audit people run happily for years and one that dies after two tries.

> What turns an inspection into a check-in: it looks at the home, not the people; it is light and quick; it is regular and expected; and it notices what is working, not only what has slipped.

Take those in turn, because each one defuses a different part of the dread. It looks at the home, not the people, so no one is on trial. It is light and quick, a few minutes and not a formal reckoning, so it never becomes a burden anyone avoids. It is regular and expected, part of the ordinary rhythm rather than a surprise sprung on the household, so it carries no ambush and no fear. And it notices what is working, not only what has slipped, so it feels like appreciation as much as correction, and people leave it encouraged rather than scolded. Miss any one of these and the audit tips back toward inspection; hold all four and it becomes what it should be, an easy, welcome habit of care.

## Look at the Home, Not the Household

Of those four, the first is the one everything else hangs on, and it is worth its own moment, because it is where good intentions most often go wrong. The whole practice lives or dies on keeping the attention on the room and off the roommate.

> The moment an audit becomes about who let the shelf slip, it dies, because nobody volunteers to be inspected. Keep it about the shelf. A shelf that drifted is not a failure of anyone's character; it is a small system that needs a small adjustment, and the audit's only job is to find it kindly and set it right.

This is not soft-heartedness; it is what actually works. The instant an audit starts assigning blame, two things happen, and both of them wreck it. First, people become defensive, and a defensive household stops being honest about drift, so the audit loses the very information it exists to gather. Second, and worse, people learn the real lesson you are teaching, which is that mess is punished, and they respond the way anyone responds to that: not by keeping the home better, but by hiding the mess before audit day and putting on a show of order for the inspection. You will have trained your family to fake a clean house on a schedule, which is precisely the opposite of what you wanted. Keep it about the shelf and none of that happens. A drifted shelf is a neutral fact about a system, like a low tyre, and you simply top it up. The blamelessness is not a nicety. It is the thing that keeps the audit honest and therefore useful.

## Catch It Early, Keep It Light

The last thing to understand is why you do this regularly and keep it small, and the answer is entirely about timing, because in a home the cost of a problem depends almost completely on how long it has been left.

> The whole value of a regular check-in is timing. A drift caught this week is a two-minute fix; the same drift caught in six months is a lost weekend. You do not audit the home to judge it. You audit it so that it never again has to be rescued.

Drift compounds. One thing left in the wrong place invites a second, because a surface that is already a little cluttered signals that clutter is allowed here; a system used slightly wrong a few times quietly becomes used wrong by default. Caught in its first week, almost any drift is a shrug and a small correction. Left for half a year, the same drift has recruited friends and hardened into the new normal, and undoing it is a whole dreaded project of the kind Sustain exists to make unnecessary. So you look often and lightly, because a five-minute check every week or two catches drift while it is still trivial, and a household that does that never again meets the exhausting, morale-crushing overhaul. And keep it genuinely light, because only the light version survives: a short, low-key walk that people can actually do forever beats a thorough, solemn reckoning that they manage twice and then avoid. If you want, let each person check their own zone rather than one person checking everyone's, which turns the whole thing from being inspected into taking quiet pride in your own patch, and people are remarkably honest about their own drift when nobody is standing over them with a pen.

> **Quick Win**
> Do one two-minute audit right now. Walk a single room and ask, of the room and not of anyone in it, "what has drifted since we set this up?" Fix the first small thing you find. That is a whole audit, and it was not frightening at all.

> **6S Tip**
> Keep it short and regular. A five-minute check every week catches far more than a two-hour reckoning once a year, and only the short one actually keeps happening. You can reuse the simple snapshot from your baseline audit; you do not need a new instrument.

## Keeping Has Begun

You have taken the first real step of keeping. You started with the mindset, that a home is kept and not finished, and now you have the first practice that puts it to work: a regular, friendly check-in that looks at the home, catches drift while it is small, and never once puts the household on trial. Run kindly and often, that single habit will spare you more dreaded overhauls than any amount of heroic cleaning, because it means your home never again gets far enough from good to need rescuing.

What the audit does not do, by design, is solve the bigger things it finds. A check-in is brilliant at catching drift and fixing the small stuff on the spot, but every so often it will turn up a real, recurring problem, the same thing drifting in the same place month after month, which no amount of quick correction will settle because the system itself is wrong. Fixing those, together, as a household, instead of one person forever patching them alone, is a practice of its own, and it is the next chapter. Past that lies folding the keeping into the ordinary shape of a day, and then the recurring event that renews the whole thing and completes the book. For now, hold the one idea this chapter turns on: an audit is a kindness you do for your home, not a judgment you pass on your family.

> **Common Mistake**
> Turning the audit into a performance review of the people, with scores held over someone's head. The instant it feels like judgment, everyone starts hiding the mess before the audit instead of fixing it, and you have taught the household to fake a clean home for inspection day. Check the home, kindly, and never the person.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> Keep the gains with a light, regular check-in that looks at the home and never at the people. A drift caught small is a small fix; a drift left to grow is an overhaul. Audit the house kindly and often, and it will never again need rescuing.
