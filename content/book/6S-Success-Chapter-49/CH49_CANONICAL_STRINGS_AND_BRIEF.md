## The room in one line
The stair landing is not a room at all but a route, the only space in this book you never stop in, and the one place where a small oversight, a book left on a tread or a bulb nobody replaced, stops being untidiness and becomes a real injury.

## The stair landing Shine layer (the nine rules from Ch 31 PLUS)
- **This is a route, not a room**: every other space in the book is judged by what it holds. This one is judged by whether you can pass through it, at night, with your arms full, and the correct amount of storage here is very nearly none.
- **Here a small oversight becomes a real injury, and it arrives unattended**: a workshop hazard hurts you while you are paying attention to it. A stair hazard waits in the dark for somebody who is thinking about something else.
- **The trap is a reasonable sentence**: "I'll take it up next time I go" is how a console fills and how the bottom three steps become a shelf. One basket, emptied nightly, is the only version of that promise that survives a busy week.
- **The most touched and least washed surface in the house is the handrail**: wipe it end to end weekly, underside included, and vacuum the crevice where each tread meets its riser, because that trapped grit is what wears a runner smooth and slick.

## Three zones (start at the Stair and Floor Path, though it looks the least cluttered)
1. **Stair and Floor Path** - the treads, runner, handrail and lighting that carry every person in the house between floors, several times a day, often with their arms full.
2. **Landing Surface or Console** - a small surface that holds a lamp and a landing spot without becoming the house's holding pen for things headed to another floor.
3. **Wall and Display Zone** - the frames, mirror and sconces on the stairway wall, hung so they stay put and never crowd the shoulder of someone climbing.

## FROZEN STRINGS (byte-verbatim in manuscript AND final.html)

### Three zones
The stair landing is three small zones, not one big job: the stair and floor path, the landing surface or console, and the wall and display zone. Do them one at a time, and start with the stair and floor path even though it looks like the least cluttered of the three, because clearing the treads and sorting the lighting sets the limit on what the console and the wall are allowed to hold.

### The rule (a route, not a room)
The stair landing is not a room. It is a route, and it is the only space in this book that nobody ever stops in. Every other space you have worked is judged by what it holds; this one is judged solely by whether a person can pass through it at night, with their arms full, without anything going wrong. Which means the correct amount of storage here is very nearly none, and every question you ask on this flight has the same shape: does this obstruct the passage, or the hand that needs the rail, or the light that shows the step.

### The definition (defbox)
A finished stair landing is one anybody can take in the dark with their arms full: every tread bare from nose to riser including the bottom three steps that usually stage things, the runner flat with no lifted corner at the turn, the handrail unbroken and unobstructed from the top newel post to the bottom with no bag or lead hung on either post, both switches working so the top step and the bottom step are lit before you take them, three permanent things on the console with one basket that sleeps empty, and every frame hung from two points with nothing projecting more than a hand's width at shoulder height.

### Unattended-hazard reframe (why this room's stakes are different)
The workshop was dangerous too, and the difference matters. A workshop hazard hurts you while you are paying attention to it: you are holding the tool, you are looking at the blade, you know what you are doing is sharp. A stair hazard does the opposite. It waits, in the dark, for somebody who is thinking about something else entirely, carrying a basket they cannot see over, at the end of a long day. Nobody approaches a staircase carefully, because a staircase is not a task. That is precisely why the small oversights here matter so much more than they would anywhere else in the house.

### Reasonable-sentence reframe (how a landing fills)
The trap in this room is not laziness. It is a completely reasonable sentence: I will take it up next time I go. Every person in your house says it, several times a day, and each time it is true in principle and false in practice. That single sentence is how a landing console fills, and it is how the bottom three steps quietly become a shelf. So you do not try to defeat it, because you will not. You give it a container instead. One basket, on the floor at the end nearest the top step, emptied every night whether it is full or not, is the only version of that promise that survives a busy week.

### Handrail reframe (most touched, least washed)
The handrail is one of the most touched and least washed surfaces in the whole house. Every hand that has ever used this staircase has left something on it, and almost nobody wipes it, because it does not look dirty the way a counter looks dirty. So it gets cleaned end to end every week, and the underside your fingers actually wrap around gets cleaned too, since that is the part carrying the oil. And while you are on the flight, vacuum the crevice where each tread meets its riser, because that trapped grit is exactly what polishes a runner smooth and slick under a stocking foot.

### Safety reframe (the room where safety is not a pass but the point)
Fall is not one of five questions here, it is the whole chapter, and it has three answers that tend to arrive together and at night: a lifted runner corner, a loose handrail bracket, and a dead bulb over the top step. Fall, cut, or crush is anything set near the stair-side edge of a console, which a passing sleeve knocks onto the top tread to wait in the dark, a glass vase falling further than furniture height onto treads people walk in socks, and a picture coming off a stairway wall to shatter across several steps at once. Water and electricity is a stairwell bulb changed while balancing on a tread rather than working from the landing on a properly footed ladder.

### Before-and-after caption
This is the stair landing the whole loop was for: the same flight you started with, every tread bare from nose to riser including the bottom three, the runner flat and fixed, the rail clear from newel to newel, both ends lit, and a console holding three things and a basket that sleeps empty. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then do what this room asks: stand at the bottom in the dark and look up at the top step, and stand at the top and look down at the bottom, because that is the view the fall would have.

### One Idea to Keep
A staircase is not safe because it looks tidy. It is safe because nothing obstructs the passage, the hand that needs the rail, or the light that shows the step. And it stays safe because the reasonable sentence, I will take it up next time I go, was given a basket that empties every night instead of being argued with. Do one zone this week, and make it the flight itself, because bare treads and working light at both ends set the limit on everything else this landing is allowed to hold.

## Four callouts (frozen, budgeted ~250)
- **Quick Win (win)**: Stand at the bottom of the flight in the dark and look up at the top step. Then stand at the top and look down at the bottom. A dead or dim bulb at either end is the single most dangerous thing this room can hold, it takes ten minutes to fix, and you cannot see it from anywhere except those two positions.
- **6S Tip (tip)**: Wipe the handrail end to end every week, underside included, because it is one of the most touched and least washed surfaces in the house. Then vacuum the crevice where each tread meets its riser and where a runner tucks against the stringer, since that trapped grit is what wears a runner smooth and slick.
- **The Nightly Basket Challenge (family)**: Put one basket on the landing floor at the end nearest the top step, and agree out loud that whoever switches off the landing light carries it up or down and brings it back empty. It sleeps empty or the rule has failed. And if an item rides that basket three nights running, it has no home on the destination floor: give it one tonight.
- **Common Mistake (mistake)**: Doing the console and the picture wall first because they look like the untidy parts, when it is the flight that sets the limit; pressing a lifted runner corner back down and carrying on; and straightening a high frame while standing on a tread instead of working from the flat landing.

## The calls that matter here
The runner that has already lifted once (you have almost certainly pressed it back down and carried on, and that is the wrong shape of decision, because a runner that has lifted once has already told you what it will do again, so choose today between refixing it properly along its whole length, with rods or full-width tape on every tread rather than a patch on the one failed corner, or taking it up entirely and living with bare treads, since bare wood you know is slippery beats a runner you have quietly stopped trusting, because you adjust your step for the first and not the second, and the one thing you may not do is press it down again and wait); the going-upstairs-anyway pile (give it a container, exactly one basket, and then enforce the part that hurts, which is that it empties nightly whether it is full or not, and if an item rides that basket three nights running without landing anywhere, it is not waiting for a trip, it has no home on the destination floor, so stop carrying it back and forth and either give it a home tonight or route it out of the house); the heavy mirror you would rather not touch (make this call with your hands rather than your eye: grip it and push up and out, and if it lifts off the wall with any play in the hook it is being held by friction and gravity on a surface that shakes every time someone runs down, so rehang it today on two rated anchors or take it down and leave the wall bare until the right hardware arrives, because a bare stairway wall is not a failure, it is safer than heavy glass hanging over steps).

## Watch for on the stair landing (five safety questions)
- **Fall**: a lifted runner corner, a loose handrail bracket, or a dead bulb over the top step, each one a fall on the hardest surface in the house, and they tend to arrive together and at night; anything set near the stair-side edge of the console, a book, a phone, a set of keys, caught by a passing sleeve and knocked onto the top tread where it waits in the dark; reaching sideways to level a frame while standing on a tread, which puts your weight outside your feet on the least forgiving surface in the house.
- **Fall, cut, or crush**: a glass vase or ceramic bowl on a console beside an open stairwell, which falls further than furniture height and breaks across treads that people walk in socks; a framed picture coming off a stairway wall, landing on treads and shattering across several steps at once, in the one place you cannot sweep from a standing position; carrying a laundry basket or a box you cannot see over, which means going down blind with no free hand for the rail, with anything parked on the bottom three steps invisible to that person.
- **Water and electricity**: changing the bulb over the stairwell while balancing on a tread with a fitting overhead, rather than switching the light off at the wall, letting a hot bulb cool, and working from the landing on a properly footed ladder; a lamp cord run across the walking side of a console rather than clipped down its back leg.
- **Burn or fire**: a sconce or lamp shade running hot enough to scorch, seen as a brown mark on the shade lining or around the bulb, which says the bulb is running too hot for the fitting.
- **Poison, choke, or strangle**: **nothing meaningful in this room, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than inventing something.** A stair landing holds no chemistry, no medication, and no small parts. Following the precedent set in Chapter 47, an honest negative is recorded rather than a manufactured hazard, because filling the slot would make the other four easier to skim, and on a staircase the other four are the whole point.

## Kit (product TYPES only)
This is the shortest kit in the book, which is itself the argument: a route needs almost nothing. Cleaning: a compact vacuum with attachments and its brush and crevice tools, a colour-coded microfiber cloth set, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a floor-material-compatible cleaner, a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner for the frames and the mirror at the turn, and a portable cleaning caddy. Holding, and there is deliberately very little: one woven basket for the stair basket, a remote control tray or jewelry and valet tray for the console top, and a cord label set with a cable management clip for the lamp cord down the back leg. Safety and fixing: anti-tip anchors and rated wall hardware for the mirror and every frame, a stable step ladder used from the flat landing and never from a tread, impact-rated safety glasses for overhead work at the fitting, and floor boundary tape only if the basket's footprint needs marking. Nothing else belongs here, and that is the point.
