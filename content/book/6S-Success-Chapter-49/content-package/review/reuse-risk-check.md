# Chapter 49 Reuse Risk Check: The Stair Landing

What could go wrong when this chapter's material is cut up and republished, and what stops it.

## Highest risk in this chapter: depiction

This is the first Part Nine chapter where **the images are riskier than the words.** Three depiction fences apply to every visual asset, and they are stated in the README, the image prompts, the b-roll notes, the storyboard, and the repurposing map:

1. **Never depict anyone working from a tread.** The chapter tells readers to reach the stairway light and the high frames from a ladder footed on the flat landing. A single stock photo of someone standing on a step to change a bulb would contradict the chapter's central safety instruction more loudly than any sentence could correct.
2. **Never depict a fall, a stumble, or a near-miss**, staged or otherwise, and never a person carrying a load they cannot see over on stairs. The hazard is described in words; illustrating it would turn care into spectacle.
3. **No fall statistics or injury data anywhere.** The chapter's argument is structural (the hazard is unattended) and does not need a number. A number would date, would invite challenge, and would shift the register from care to alarm.

## Second risk: the dead-bulb passage detached from its fix

The most quotable passage in the chapter describes somebody coming down at night with their arms full and taking the top step without being able to see where it ends. In the manuscript, the podcast, the short video and the X thread it is always followed immediately by "ten minutes and a bulb".

**Cut loose from the fix, it is a fear post.** Any future reuse must carry the remedy in the same asset. This is recorded as a hard condition rather than a preference.

## Third risk: repair creep

The chapter flags a lifted runner, a loose tread, a loose bracket and a picture on a single nail. It decides what to do about each and never explains how to fix any of them. There is no structural, electrical, or carpentry instruction anywhere in the package.

The pressure to add it is real, because a reader who has just been told a runner is dangerous will want to know how to refix it. **Do not answer that in this book.** The call the chapter makes is refix it properly along its whole length or take it up entirely, and "properly" is deliberately left to a trade.

## Fourth risk: the storage claim read as absolute

"The correct amount of storage here is very nearly none" is the chapter's most repeated line and its most quotable. Read in isolation it can be heard as a rule against ever putting anything on a landing, which is not what the chapter says: it permits a console holding three things and a basket that sleeps empty.

Every asset that carries the line also carries the basket, which is the qualifier. Keep that pairing.

## Fifth risk: household negotiation

The nightly basket requires somebody to do it, and the chapter is honest that this is a household agreement rather than a purchase. Assets that present the basket as a product solution would misrepresent it. The discussion questions ask directly who would carry it and whether they would agree, which is the correct framing.

## Standing fences, held

- Product **types** only, never brand names. Twenty-one types, none new.
- No invented prices.
- No medical content. Nobody's balance, mobility, or age is diagnosed anywhere; the chapter describes the space, not the people on it.
- Never advise mixing cleaners.
- Disposal is never down a drain.
- The kit stays sparse. Padding it in a reused asset contradicts the chapter's argument.

## Verdict
Cleared for reuse subject to the three depiction fences and the dead-bulb pairing rule, all of which are written into the package rather than held here.
