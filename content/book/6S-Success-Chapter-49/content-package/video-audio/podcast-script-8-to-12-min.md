# Chapter 49 Podcast Script: The Stair Landing

*Eight to twelve minutes. Conversational, unhurried, and a little quieter than usual.*

---

We are near the end of the book now, and this chapter is about the only space in it that is not a room.

Think about a stair landing for a second. Nobody sits down there. Nobody works there. Nobody keeps anything there on purpose. It has no function of its own at all. It exists to be passed through, several times a day, by every single person in the house.

Which means it gets judged by a completely different question from everything else you have worked through. Not what does it hold. Can a person get through it.

And once you ask it that way, the honest answer to how much storage belongs on a staircase is: very nearly none.

That would make this the simplest chapter in the whole book. Except for one thing.

This is also the place where a small oversight stops being untidiness and becomes a real injury.

Now, we did the workshop three chapters ago, and that room was genuinely dangerous. So I want to be precise about the difference here, because it changes what you do about it.

A workshop hazard hurts you while you are paying attention to it. You are holding the tool. You are looking at the blade. You know, at that moment, that what you are doing is sharp. Your care is switched on.

A stair hazard does the exact opposite. It waits.

It waits in the dark, for somebody who is thinking about something else entirely. Somebody carrying a laundry basket they cannot see over. Somebody at the end of a long day going up to bed.

And here is the sentence I would most like you to take from this chapter: nobody approaches a staircase carefully, because a staircase is not a task. It is the thing you do on the way to the task.

That is precisely why a book left on a tread and a bulb nobody replaced matter so much more here than the equivalent oversight would matter anywhere else in your house.

Right. So what actually fills a landing?

It is not laziness. I want to be clear about that, because this is the chapter where blaming people would be easiest and least useful.

It is a completely reasonable sentence: I will take it up next time I go.

Every person in your house says it. Several times a day. And every single time, it is true in principle and false in practice. Nobody is lying. The trip genuinely was going to happen. It just did not happen with that thing in hand.

That one sentence is how a landing console fills, and it is how the bottom three steps quietly become a shelf.

So you do not try to defeat it, because you will not. You have been trying for years. You give it a container instead.

One basket. On the floor, at the end nearest the top step. And then the part that actually hurts: it empties every night, whether it is full or not. Whoever switches off the landing light carries it up or down and brings it back empty. The light switch is the trigger, so the basket never sleeps loaded.

And there is a rule that settles everything else. If an item rides that basket three nights running without landing anywhere, it is not waiting for a trip. It has no home on the destination floor. So stop carrying it back and forth, and either give it a home tonight or route it out of the house.

Two practical things, and then the one I want you to actually go and do.

The first is the handrail. It is one of the most touched and least washed surfaces in your entire house. Every hand that has ever used those stairs has left something on it, and almost nobody wipes it, because it does not look dirty the way a kitchen counter looks dirty. So: end to end, every week, and the underside your fingers actually wrap around, because that is the part carrying the oil.

The second is the crevice where each tread meets its riser. Vacuum right into it. That trapped grit is exactly what polishes a stair runner smooth and slick under a stocking foot, and almost nobody gets into it because a vacuum head does not naturally reach there.

And now the thing to go and do tonight, which takes about a minute.

Stand at the bottom of your stairs, in the dark, and look up at the top step. Then go up and stand at the top and look down at the bottom.

A dead or dim bulb over the top step is the single most dangerous thing a staircase can hold. And it is almost invisible. You cannot see it from the landing, because you are standing underneath it. You cannot see it from halfway up, because you are looking at your feet. You can only see it from the bottom of the flight, looking up, in the dark, which is a position nobody stands in deliberately.

So it goes unnoticed for weeks. And then somebody comes down at night with their arms full and takes the top step without being able to see where it ends.

Ten minutes and a bulb.

That is also how this chapter closes, and it is the only close in the book that is a check rather than a photograph. Every other room's standard is something you look at. This one's is something you have to stand in, from the two positions nobody occupies on purpose, because that is the view the fall would have.

Do one zone this week, and make it the flight itself.
