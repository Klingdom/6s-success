# 6S Success: Home Edition Newsletter

**Subject line (placeholder):** Before you charge the garage, read this

**Preview text (placeholder):** The trick is not more motivation. It is a smaller, smarter first target.

---

Hi friend,

There is a specific Saturday morning this issue exists to rescue.

You have learned the method. You feel ready. You are standing in the kitchen with a second cup of coffee and a dangerous amount of motivation, and a bright, certain thought arrives: *I should do the garage.*

I want to stop you right there, gently, before you open that door and lose the whole weekend along with most of your enthusiasm. The garage feels like the brave choice. It is usually the one that ends a fresh start before it gets going.

Here is the honest part about motivation. It does not last long, and it does not come back on command. The energy you feel right now is real, but it is also a budget, and the garage is the single most expensive room in the house. You pull everything out, you run out of daylight around the halfway point, and you are left with a space that is somehow worse than when you started, a sore back, and a quiet voice that says *see, you are not really an organized person.*

That voice is lying. You did not fail at organizing. You failed at choosing. You aimed a beginner's budget of energy at an expert's project.

**Your first project is not really about a room. It is about proof.**

A single tidy drawer will not change your life. But the win it gives you changes what you do next. People do not keep going because they are disciplined. They keep going because the last thing they tried worked, and they want that feeling again. A win you can see is the most reliable fuel there is. It does not run out the way willpower does. It compounds.

This is why a small, certain win beats a big, brave attempt every time at the start. So the question is not which space you feel worst about. Guilt is a terrible compass. It points at the hardest, heaviest, most emotional spaces, which are exactly the ones most likely to defeat a beginner.

Friction is the better compass. Stop asking which space you feel guiltiest about. Start asking which space costs you the most, most often.

**Two questions pick your target.**

First, friction: how much does this space cost me, and how often? The catch-all drawer you fight every evening is expensive. The crawl space you open twice a year is cheap, no matter how ugly it looks. Friction is not about how bad a space looks. It is about how often it taxes your actual life.

Second, effort: how big is the job, honestly? A drawer is fifteen minutes. A counter is half an hour. The garage is a series of weekends.

Put those two answers on a simple grid, the First Target Map, with friction on one axis and effort on the other. The sweet spot is the high-friction, low-effort corner. Big daily relief, small one-time cost. That is where you start. The garage sits in the other corner, high effort, friction often lower than its guilt. It is a graduation project, not a first lap.

**Try this week.** Walk your home slowly and name three to five spaces that genuinely annoy you. Not the messiest. The most annoying, the ones where you sigh or hunt or step over the same thing again. Score each on friction (one to five) and effort (one to five). Subtract effort from friction. The highest number is your target. Then write it down somewhere you will see it, put a rough day on it, and give it a time box. One space. One session. A finish line you can actually cross.

That is the whole skill this issue is about. Not working harder. Choosing better.

When you are ready, the full chapter walks you through the map, the garage, and the scorecard in detail. Read Chapter 4, "How to Choose Your First Target Area," then pick your spot.

Win where you will see it,

The 6S Success team

*P.S. You do not need more motivation to start. You need a smaller, smarter first target. Win where you will see it, and the next project gets easier on its own.*
