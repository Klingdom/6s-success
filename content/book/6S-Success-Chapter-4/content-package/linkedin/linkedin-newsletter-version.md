# The Garage Will Wait. Start Smaller.

Hi, and welcome back.

I want to talk you out of something, kindly, before you do it this weekend.

You learned a method. You feel ready. You are standing in the kitchen with a second cup of coffee and a dangerous amount of motivation, and a thought arrives, bright and certain and completely wrong. *I should do the garage.*

I know that thought. It feels like the brave choice. It is usually the one that ends an organizing habit before it starts.

## Motivation is a budget, not a battery

Here is the hard part about motivation. It does not last long, and it does not come back on command. The burst you feel right now is real, but it is a budget, and you are about to spend the whole thing on the single most expensive room in your house.

You will pull everything out. You will run out of daylight and steam at roughly the halfway point. And you will be left with a garage that is somehow worse than when you started, a sore back, and a quiet little voice that says *see, you are not really an organized person after all.*

That voice is lying. You did not fail at organizing. You failed at choosing. You aimed a beginner's budget of energy at an expert's project.

## Your first project is for proof, not the room

Let us be honest about what a first project is really for. It is not for the space. A single organized drawer will not change your life, and you know it.

The first project is for you. It exists to prove one quiet point to the part of your brain that has watched you start and quit a dozen times before: this time I had a method, and it actually worked.

That proof is the whole game. People do not keep going because they are disciplined. They keep going because the last thing they tried worked and they want that feeling again. A win you can see is the most reliable fuel there is. It does not run out the way willpower does. It compounds.

## Two questions, one map

So how do you find a space small enough to win and annoying enough to matter? Two questions, answerable about any space in under a minute.

First, friction. How much does this space cost me, and how often? The catch-all drawer you fight every evening is expensive. The crawl space you open twice a year is cheap, no matter how ugly it looks.

Second, effort. How big is the job, honestly? A drawer is fifteen minutes. A closet is an afternoon. The garage is a series of weekends.

Plot those answers and you get a simple grid I call the First Target Map. The sweet spot is the top-left corner: high friction, low effort. Big daily relief, small one-time cost. That is where you start.

And notice where the garage lands. Far over in high effort, and here is the surprise, its friction is often lower than its guilt. You do not stand in it every morning. It looms in your mind far larger than it bites in your life. The garage is a graduation project, not a first lap.

## My ask this week

Walk your home and name three to five spaces that genuinely annoy you. Not the messiest. The most annoying, the places where you sigh or hunt or step over the same thing again.

Score each twice, one to five. Friction, how often it bites. Effort, how big the job is. Subtract effort from friction. The highest number is your first target.

Then do the smallest, most powerful thing left. Write it down where you will see it. A target that lives only in your mind is a wish. A target written down with a rough day attached to it is a plan.

You do not need more motivation to start. You need a smaller, smarter first target. This is from Chapter 4 of *6S Success: Home Edition*, and you can read the whole thing free in the online book. Then go pick your spot.
