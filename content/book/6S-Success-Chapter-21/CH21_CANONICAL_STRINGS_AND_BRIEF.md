## Hero device (frozen): the hazard hunt
A calm, systematic search of the whole home, one room at a time, carrying the same short set of safety questions into every space and removing each hazard the moment it is found. Where Chapter 20's lens was one question asked opportunistically on a first pass, the hunt is that lens applied on purpose, everywhere, in order, so nothing is skipped, and paired with the discipline of fixing on the spot.
> **The rule (frozen):** Find it, and fix it now.
> **The method (frozen):** Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.

## The five safety questions (frozen set, home-scaled from "Steps to Complete Safety")
In every room, ask:
> 1. Could someone fall here?
> 2. Could something here burn, or catch fire?
> 3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?
> 4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush?
> 5. Could water and electricity meet?

And when the answer is yes: fix it now.

## Definition box (frozen)
> A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.

## The thoroughness reframe (frozen, pull-quote)
> A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.

## The found-not-fixed reframe (frozen, pull-quote, carries the rule's logic)
> A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.

## The camouflage reframe (frozen, pull-quote)
> The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.

## One Idea to Keep (frozen)
> Go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone.

## Callouts (frozen wording)
- **Quick Win:** Pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out: the cord tucked away, the pills moved up high, the rug taped down, the heavy thing brought off the top shelf. One room, thoroughly safe, today.
- **Family Challenge:** Turn the hunt into a game. Give each person a room and the five questions and send everyone off to find hazards at once, and the younger the sharper for the low ones. Then come back together and fix the worst finds as a team. A home searched by five pairs of eyes misses far less than one.
- **6S Tip:** Hunt every room twice, once standing and once low. Crouch to a small child's height and look again: the outlet at ankle level, the tablecloth a toddler can pull, the cleaning spray under the sink, the wobbling table leg. A whole layer of hazards lives in the band of space adults never occupy.
- **Common Mistake:** Walking the home with a notepad, listing hazards to deal with later, and calling that safety. The list is not the fix. Every danger you write down and leave in place is still live. Carry a roll of tape and a box instead of a notepad, and fix as you find.
