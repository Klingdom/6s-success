Chapter Twenty-One

Find and Remove Hazards

A first pass catches the dangers that are obvious the moment you finally look, and walks straight past the rest. This chapter makes Safety thorough: a calm, room-by-room hunt that carries the same short set of questions into every space, finds the hazards hiding in plain sight, and removes each one the moment it is found. Find it, and fix it now.

Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk through your home, and it showed you a handful of real dangers, and you handled them. The smoke alarm has a fresh battery. The cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch. The rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides. If you did that, you have already done something that matters, and you felt the particular relief of it, the sense of a worry set down.

Here is the honest next thing, and it is not said to take that relief away but to finish the job it started. The dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones. They were the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question, because they are the ones we already half-know about: the alarm we have been meaning to check, the bottles we always meant to move. And the obvious hazards, it turns out, are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it will survive any number of quick glances precisely because a glance is not built to catch it.

So this chapter is the search the first pass was not. It is a calm, thorough, room-by-room hunt for the hazards that hide, and, just as much, it is the discipline of removing each one the moment you find it. A first pass is a glance across a finished home. A hunt is a walk through it with a method and a roll of tape, room by room, missing nothing on purpose. That is how you get from a home whose worst dangers are handled to a home that is, all the way through, genuinely safe.

In this chapter

  • See why the obvious hazards are the few, and the hidden ones the many.

  • Recognize the camouflaged hazards a first pass always walks past.

Why thoroughFrom a Glance to a Search

The whole reason for this chapter is a simple, slightly uncomfortable fact about how we see our own homes.

A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.

A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small and particular set: the ones dramatic enough, or familiar enough, to catch the eye of someone doing a quick scan. That is genuinely worth doing, and it is why the last chapter came first. But the eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times, and it has long since stopped seeing most of what is in it. The cord that has run under the rug for three years is not going to announce itself now. The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything.

This is not a failure of attention or of care. It is just how familiarity works: the things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones. Which means the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and start running a method instead. You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before. The point of a hunt is that it finds what a glance cannot, because it does not depend on the thing being obvious. It depends only on your walking the whole home and asking.

The methodThe Hazard Hunt

So here is the tool, and it is less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house.

The hazard hunt

A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.

Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so that no space is skipped because you happened not to think of it. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you happen to remember to look for on a given day. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so that finding a hazard and removing it are one motion, not two. The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like.

Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.

What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage, because that is what hides the dangerous ones.

The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.

None of those objects looks like a hazard, because none of them is only a hazard. The lamp is a lamp; it is a good lamp; it just also happens to have a cord strung across the place people walk. The remote is a remote; it also holds a small round battery that, swallowed, does real harm, behind a cover a determined toddler can open. You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job, and the only way to catch them at it is to ask.

The toolThe Five Questions

Here is the short list you carry into every room. It comes, home-scaled, from the questions the method has always asked on a safety walk, and it is deliberately small enough to hold in your head.

In every room, ask:

  1. Could someone fall here?
  2. Could something here burn, or catch fire?
  3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?
  4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush?
  5. Could water and electricity meet?

Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens, and each finds a different family of hidden hazard.

Could someone fall looks down and looks at the edges: the rug that slides, the tread with a stray shoe on it, the wet patch by the tub, the top of the stairs, the small child near the low window. Falls are the most common serious home injury and the most walked-past, because floors and stairs are exactly what a familiar eye stops seeing.

Could something burn, or catch fire looks for heat too near things that burn and for electricity asked to do too much: the heater a foot from the curtain, the candle under the shelf, the outlet with a nest of plugs feeding a nest of cords, the lint packed in the dryer trap, the pan left where a sleeve can catch it.

Could something poison, choke, or strangle looks low, and looks for the small and the looped: the cleaning sprays and medicines within a child's reach, the button batteries and coins and small parts on the floor, the looped blind or curtain cord at a child's height, the plastic bag left where small hands find it.

Could something fall, cut, or crush looks up and looks at what is heavy and free: the tall bookcase or dresser not anchored to the wall, which can tip when a child climbs it, the heavy box on the top shelf, the knives within reach of the counter's edge, the drawer that slides all the way out.

Could water and electricity meet looks in the wet rooms: the hairdryer or radio near the filled basin, the frayed cord by the sink, the outlet without protection near water. Where those two meet, a small accident becomes a serious one.

You do not need to memorize categories of hazard or become an inspector. You need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room, and when the answer to any of them is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for.

The ruleFind It, and Fix It Now

There is a strong temptation, once you start finding things, to do the finding now and the fixing later: to walk the whole home with a notepad, write down every hazard, and come back to deal with them when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum.

A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.

The notepad feels like progress, and it is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend. Between finding a danger and removing it there is a gap, and everything bad that a hazard can do, it can do in that gap. So you close the gap to nothing: you fix each hazard the moment you find it, before you move on. This is the source's oldest safety instruction, "address all possible safety issues immediately", and it is the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list. Carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad, and let finding and fixing be one motion.

Most of what you find will yield to that, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove: a cord tucked out of the walkway, a bottle moved to a high shelf, a latch fitted, a rug taped, a pot handle turned in, a heavy thing brought down. The fix is usually smaller than the worry was. Now and then you will meet a hazard whose full fix is genuinely bigger than "now", a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding. The rule still holds, with a small honest amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. Move the climbing-height toys off the unanchored bookcase today, and buy the bracket this week. Reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done on the spot. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.

Quick Win

Pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out: the cord tucked away, the pills moved up high, the rug taped down, the heavy thing brought off the top shelf. One room, thoroughly safe, today.

6S Tip

Hunt every room twice, once standing and once low. Crouch to a small child's height and look again: the outlet at ankle level, the tablecloth a toddler can pull, the cleaning spray under the sink, the wobbling table leg. A whole layer of hazards lives in the band of space adults never occupy.

Worked throughA Room-by-Room Hunt

Let me walk it with you, the way you would actually do it, room by room, so you can see how ordinary the finds are and how quickly each one closes.

Start in the kitchen, because it holds more of all five kinds of hazard than any other room. Could someone fall: the rug by the sink is fine, but there is a slick spot where the dishwasher leaks a little, worth a look. Could something burn: a pot handle is turned out over the front edge of the stove where a passing hip or a reaching child could catch it, so you turn it in, a two-second fix you will now do by reflex forever. Could something poison or choke: the sprays under the sink are behind the latch you fitted last chapter, good, but a butane lighter sits in an open drawer at child height, and it goes up high. Could something fall or cut: the knives live in a block, but pushed to the very edge of the counter, so you slide them back. Could water and electricity meet: nothing by the sink, good. Four small fixes, one room, a few minutes.

The bathroom is quick and high-value. The hairdryer is plugged in beside the filled-at-bath-time basin, so it gets unplugged and moved to a drawer away from the water. The medicines are up, from last chapter. The bath mat has no grip, so it gets a grip pad or a wash-and-replace with one that does. A nightlight goes in, because most bathroom falls happen in the dark.

The living room is where camouflage lives. The tall bookcase has stood free of the wall for years and never fallen, and it is exactly the kind of tall, heavy, unanchored thing a climbing toddler can bring down on themselves, so it gets a simple anti-tip strap to the wall today. On the sofa arm is the old remote whose battery cover has gone loose, holding a button battery a small child could reach and swallow, so a strip of tape holds the cover shut until you replace it. And there, at last, is the lamp cord Chapter 20 promised would take a real hunt to catch: run under the edge of the rug, where it is both a thing to trip on and a wire being slowly crushed and heated where you cannot see it. It comes out from under the rug and gets routed along the wall.

The stairs and hall want two of the questions. Could someone fall: the treads are clear, but the hall runner has curled at one end, so it gets tacked flat, and a nightlight goes in at the top of the stairs. The child's room wants the poison-choke-strangle question most: the blind cord hangs in a loop at exactly a toddler's height, one of the quietest serious hazards in any home, so it gets wound up onto a cleat well out of reach, and the small parts and coins on the low shelf go up.

And the launch pad, your first small victory by the door, gets the hunt too, and pays off the last of Chapter 20's teasers. The little space heater you run on cold mornings sits a foot from the curtain, a flame beside a fabric, so it moves to a clear spot and gets a rule about the curtain. The charging cords are tucked so no foot catches them. Nothing here needed a deep clean or a rearrange; it was already sorted and arranged and clean. It just, in a handful of small ways, could have hurt someone, and now, room by room, it cannot.

Family Challenge

Turn the hunt into a game. Give each person a room and the five questions and send everyone off to find hazards at once, and the younger the sharper for the low ones. Then come back together and fix the worst finds as a team. A home searched by five pairs of eyes misses far less than one.

The stepThe Needle Takes Another Safety Step

Go and look at the friction meter, because it has moved again, and this time it moved for the same reason as last time, only more so.

The first Safety step, last chapter, came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass. This step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room, all the hidden ones a first pass could never reach. And again, notice what did not change: nothing got cleaner, nothing got tidier, no object found a better home. The daily friction of living here is exactly what it was this morning. What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving. A roomful of quiet dangers is simply gone, so the needle steps a little further toward calm.

Find it, and fix it now.

The step is a touch larger than the first one, because a thorough hunt clears more than a first glance did, and it is still, in absolute terms, a measured one, and both of those are honest. Larger, because you did more: you did not handle the obvious few, you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many. Measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks, and safety moves it in the smaller, quieter increments of things locked down rather than things swept out. Chapter 20's first Safety step is the dashed ghost just behind the live needle now; Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone sits further back as a passed marker; and the goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead. It is still ahead because Safety is not finished when the hazards are gone. It is finished when the home is made to keep itself safe, which is one more chapter, and Standardize and Sustain follow that. The needle moved for the best of reasons again, and it still has real distance to go, and both are true at once.

In closingSafety, Made Thorough

You carried five plain questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and you removed what they found before you left each room. That is the fourth S made thorough: not a first pass on the obvious dangers, but a complete sweep that reached the hidden ones, the button battery and the blind cord and the unanchored bookcase and the heater by the curtain, the hazards that would have survived any number of glances because they were wearing the disguise of ordinary things.

What is left is not more hazards to find, at least not today; it is the question of tomorrow. A hunt makes a home safe as of the day you run it, and homes do not hold still. New things come in, batteries die, a bracket loosens, a child grows tall enough to reach a shelf that was safe last month. Making safety survive that, turning a one-time sweep into a home that stays safe, and preparing calmly for the few emergencies you cannot prevent, is a different kind of work, and it is the next chapter, the one that completes the fourth S. For now, you have done the thing that had to come first: you found the dangers that were already here, and you removed them. Hold on to the five questions, because they work in any room, on any day, and a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe.

Common Mistake

Walking the home with a notepad, listing hazards to deal with later, and calling that safety. The list is not the fix. Every danger you write down and leave in place is still live. Carry a roll of tape and a box instead of a notepad, and fix as you find.

One Idea to Keep

Go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone.
