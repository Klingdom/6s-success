# Newsletter Edition: Find and Remove Hazards

**Subject line (placeholder):** Find it, and fix it now.

**Preview text (placeholder):** A first pass finds the obvious dangers. A hunt finds the ones that hide.

---

Hi friend,

Last issue you opened Safety, the fourth S. You took the safety lens for a first walk and handled a handful of real dangers: the smoke alarm got a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles went up high or behind a latch, the rug at the top of the stairs stopped sliding, and you felt the particular relief of a worry set down. Hold on to that relief, because this issue is going to finish the job it started. Here is the honest next thing, said gently: the dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question, because they are the ones you already half-knew about. And the obvious hazards are the minority.

Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances precisely because a glance is not built to catch it. So this issue is the search the first pass was not. Start with the reframe the whole chapter rests on.

**A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.**

This is just how familiarity works. The eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and has long since stopped seeing most of what is in it. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now; the bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything. It is not a failure of attention or care. The dangerous things become invisible right along with the harmless ones, so the only reliable way to find them is to stop relying on what catches your eye and start running a method. That method has a name.

**A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.**

Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you happen to remember to look for on a given day. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like.

**Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.**

What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage, because that is what hides the dangerous ones.

**The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.**

You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job, and the only way to catch them at it is to ask. So here is the short list you carry into every room, deliberately small enough to hold in your head.

**In every room, ask the same five questions. Could someone fall here? Could something here burn, or catch fire? Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? Could something here fall, cut, or crush? Could water and electricity meet?**

Between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home, and each is a different lens on the same space. Fall looks down and at the edges. Burn looks for heat near things that burn and electricity asked to do too much. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped. Fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector; you need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room, and when the answer is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for. Which brings us to the rule, and it is the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list.

**Find it, and fix it now.**

There is a strong temptation to resist here: to do the finding now and the fixing later, walking the whole home with a notepad and coming back when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum. The notepad feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend.

**A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.**

So you carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad, and you close the gap between finding and fixing to nothing. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. The honest exception: now and then a full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding, and the rule still holds with a small amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. Reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done on the spot. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.

Let it become something you can picture. You walk the home room by room. In the kitchen you turn the pot handle in from over the front edge, move the lighter from the open drawer up high, and slide the knives back from the counter's edge. In the bathroom you move the hairdryer away from the filled basin and put a grip pad under the mat. In the living room, where camouflage lives, the tall unanchored bookcase gets an anti-tip strap today, the old remote's loose battery cover gets a strip of tape, and the lamp cord comes out from under the rug and is routed along the wall. On the stairs the curled runner gets tacked flat. In the child's room the looped blind cord is wound up onto a cleat out of reach. At the launch pad the little space heater a foot from the curtain is moved to a clear spot. Nothing needed a deep clean or a rearrange; it was already sorted and arranged and clean. It just, in a handful of small ways, could have hurt someone, and now, room by room, it cannot.

**To try this week:** Pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out: the cord tucked away, the pills moved up high, the rug taped down, the heavy thing brought off the top shelf. One room, thoroughly safe, today. If you have children, hunt with them: crouch to their height and look again, because a whole layer of hazards lives in the band of space adults never occupy, and the younger the sharper for the low ones.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it has moved again, for the same reason as last time, only more so. The first Safety step came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass; this step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room, all the hidden ones a first pass could never reach. Again, notice what did not change: nothing got cleaner, nothing got tidier, no object found a better home. What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving. So the needle takes a second Safety step, a little further off the Shine-complete mark toward calm.

And now the honest reckoning. The step is a touch larger than the first, and still, in absolute terms, a measured one, and both are honest. Larger, because you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many rather than the obvious few. Measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks, and safety moves it in the smaller, quieter increments of things locked down rather than swept out. Chapter 20's first Safety step is the dashed ghost just behind the live needle now; Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone sits further back as a passed marker; the goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead, because Safety is finished only when the home is made to keep itself safe.

**Go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone.**
