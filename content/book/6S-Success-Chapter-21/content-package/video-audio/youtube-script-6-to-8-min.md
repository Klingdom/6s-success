Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk through your home, and it showed you a handful of real dangers, and you handled them. The smoke alarm has a fresh battery. The cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch. The rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides. You felt the particular relief of a worry set down.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The dangers you found were the obvious ones. And the obvious hazards are the minority.]

[HOST, on camera]
Here is the honest next thing, and I am not saying it to take that relief away. I am saying it to finish the job it started. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances because a glance is not built to catch it. So today, the fourth S gets thorough. A first pass is a glance across a finished home. A hunt is a walk through it with a method and a roll of tape, room by room, missing nothing on purpose.

## SECTION 1: THE OBVIOUS FEW, THE HIDDEN MANY

[HOST]
Let me start with the fact this whole chapter rests on. It is a simple, slightly uncomfortable one about how we see our own homes.

[VISUAL: The whole-home cutaway again, holding on the few green "handled" dots against the many red hazard marks, one or two in every room. The familiar eye sliding over a cord under the rug and a bookcase that reads as furniture.]

[HOST, voice over]
A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group. A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the eye doing that quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and stopped seeing most of it. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now. The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything.

[HOST, on camera]
This is not a failure of attention or care. It is just how familiarity works: the dangerous things go invisible right along with the harmless ones. So the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead. You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room.

---

## SECTION 2: THE HAZARD HUNT

[HOST]
So here is the tool, and it is less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house.

[VISUAL: A calm figure walking the home room by room with a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box. Three captions rise as they move: systematic, fixed, ends in action. Beside it, the definition box.]

[HOST, voice over]
A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: the same short list of questions carried into every room. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.]

[HOST, on camera]
The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like. And what the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage. The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain. You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things quietly doing a dangerous second job.

---

## SECTION 3: THE FIVE QUESTIONS

[HOST]
So here is the short list you carry into every room, small enough to hold in your head.

[VISUAL: A clean card titled "The Five Questions · ask in every room". Five rows, each with the frozen question in a bold slate heading and, beside it, two or three of the hidden hazards it surfaces: sliding rug and wet floor; heater by a curtain and overloaded outlet; chemicals and button battery and looped blind cord; unanchored bookcase and knives at the edge; hairdryer by the basin. At the foot, in terracotta, the rule: Find it, and fix it now.]

[HOST, voice over]
In every room, ask: could someone fall here. Could something here burn, or catch fire. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle. Could something here fall, cut, or crush. Could water and electricity meet. Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens. Fall looks down and at the edges. Burn looks for heat near things that burn. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low, for the small and the looped. Fall, cut, or crush looks up, at what is heavy and free. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms.

[HOST, on camera]
You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector. You need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room, and when the answer to any of them is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for.

---

## SECTION 4: FIND IT, AND FIX IT NOW

[HOST]
Now, once you start finding things, there is a strong temptation to do the finding now and the fixing later.

[VISUAL: The notepad trap: a home walked with a notepad, hazards listed to deal with later, still live on the shelves, quietly a hazard museum. Then the notepad set down and a roll of tape and an empty box picked up, a cord tucked and a latch fitted on the spot, a red mark flipping to green.]

[HOST, voice over]
To walk the whole home with a notepad, write down every hazard, and come back later. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum. A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone. The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Carry a roll of tape and a box, not a notepad. Fix as you find.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the good news underneath the rule: the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. A cord tucked, a bottle moved up, a latch fitted, a rug taped. Now and then a full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have. The rule still holds, with one honest amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.

## SECTION 5: A ROOM-BY-ROOM HUNT

[HOST]
Let me walk it with you, the way you would actually do it, so you can see how ordinary the finds are.

[VISUAL: A simple warm floor-plan or ordered list of rooms, walked in sequence: Kitchen, Bathroom, Living Room, Stairs and Hall, Child's Room, Launch Pad. In each, one or two ordinary hazards found and removed, a red hazard mark turning into a green "fixed" dot, a growing "fixed" count as the route goes on.]

[HOST, voice over]
Start in the kitchen, because it holds more of all five kinds of hazard than any other room: the pot handle turned in, the butane lighter moved up high, the knives slid back from the counter's edge. The bathroom is quick and high-value: the hairdryer moved away from the filled basin, a grip mat, a nightlight. The living room is where camouflage lives: the tall unanchored bookcase gets an anti-tip strap, the old remote's loose battery cover gets taped shut, and the lamp cord the last chapter promised would take a real hunt comes out from under the rug.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: None of these were messes. They were ordinary objects doing a quiet second job.]

[HOST, on camera]
The stairs get a curled runner tacked flat and a nightlight. The child's room gets the looped blind cord cleated up out of reach and the small parts moved up. And the launch pad by the door pays off the last teaser: the little space heater moves from a foot from the curtain to a clear spot, and the cords get tucked. Nothing needed a deep clean or a rearrange. It was already sorted and arranged and clean. It just, in a handful of small ways, could have hurt someone, and now, room by room, it cannot.

So that is Safety, made thorough.

[B-ROLL: A slow move from the fully hunted home, a roomful of quiet dangers now closed off for good, to the friction meter taking its second Safety step just past the marked SHINE COMPLETE tick, Chapter 20's first step now a dashed ghost just behind, the GOAL crosshair still ahead on the green calm side.]

[HOST, voice over]
You carried five plain questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and removed what they found before you left each room. That is the fourth S made thorough: not a first pass on the obvious dangers, but a complete sweep that reached the hidden ones, the button battery and the blind cord and the unanchored bookcase and the heater by the curtain. What is left is not more hazards to find, at least not today. It is the question of tomorrow, because homes do not hold still.

So here is your job, and it is small on purpose. Pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out: the cord tucked away, the pills moved up high, the rug taped down, the heavy thing brought off the top shelf. One room, thoroughly safe, today.
