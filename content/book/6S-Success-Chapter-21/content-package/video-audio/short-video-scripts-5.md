## SHORT 1: The obvious few, the hidden many
**Topic: the thoroughness reframe, a first pass catches the obvious, the hidden ones are the majority. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** You handled the obvious dangers in your home. They were the minority.

**Script:**
Last time you took the safety lens for a first walk and handled a few real dangers: the smoke alarm, the cleaning bottles, the rug at the top of the stairs. That mattered. Here is the honest next thing. A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group. Because a first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the eye doing that quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and stopped seeing most of it. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now. The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture. So you stop trusting the glance and run a method instead: a calm, room-by-room hunt for the hazards that hide.

- 28s: "A hunt finds the ones a glance never will."
- 40s: "Most home hazards are hidden."

The green dots are few and the red marks are many. Warm daylight.

## SHORT 2: The hazard hunt
**Topic: the hero device, systematic, fixed, ends in action. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The reason you keep walking past the most dangerous thing in your home: a glance is not built to catch it.

**Script:**
Here is the tool, and it is less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house. A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Three things make it a hunt and not another glance. It is systematic: room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: the same short list of questions carried into every room, so the search does not depend on what you remember to look for. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. Keep it this simple. Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave. The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The hazard hunt."
- 12s: "Systematic. Fixed. Ends in action."
- 28s: "Go room by room. Fix what you find before you leave."
- 40s: "Boring is what thorough looks like."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A calm, unhurried figure walking the home room by room with a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box. Three captions rise as they move: systematic, fixed, ends in action. A red hazard mark turning into a green fixed dot as they leave each room. Warm, calm.

## SHORT 3: The five questions
**Topic: the frozen portable tool, five lenses on every room. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Five questions catch most of what hurts people at home. Carry them into every room.

**Script:**
Here is the short list you carry into every room, small enough to hold in your head. In every room, ask five things. Could someone fall here. Could something here burn, or catch fire. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle. Could something here fall, cut, or crush. Could water and electricity meet. Between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home, and each one is a different lens. Fall looks down and at the edges: the sliding rug, the wet patch by the tub. Burn looks for heat near things that burn: the heater by the curtain, the overloaded outlet. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low: the chemicals in reach, the button battery, the looped blind cord. Fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free: the unanchored bookcase, the knives at the counter edge. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms. You do not need to become an inspector. You need five plain questions, asked in every room. And when the answer is yes: find it, and fix it now.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The five questions."
- 10s: "Ask all five, in every room."
- 32s: "Each one is a different lens."
- 44s: "Find it, and fix it now."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A clean card titled "The Five Questions · ask in every room". ## SHORT 4: Find it, and fix it now
**Topic: the rule and the notepad trap, found-not-fixed. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The notepad feels like progress. It is the one move that ruins a hazard hunt.

**Script:**
Once you start finding hazards, there is a strong temptation to do the finding now and the fixing later: walk the whole home with a notepad, write everything down, come back when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum. A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone. The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend. So carry a roll of tape and a box, not a notepad, and let finding and fixing be one motion. And the good news: the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. A cord tucked, a bottle moved up, a latch fitted, a rug taped. When a full fix is genuinely bigger than now, the rule still holds with one amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "Walking the home with a notepad?"
- 14s: "A found-but-not-fixed hazard is not handled. It is scheduled."
- 30s: "The only safe place for a danger is gone."
- 42s: "Carry tape, not a notepad. Fix as you find."

**[VISUAL] direction:** The notepad trap drawn plainly: a home walked with a notepad, hazards listed, still live on the shelves, a hazard museum. Then the notepad set down and a roll of tape and an empty box picked up, and a hazard fixed on the spot, red mark flipping to green. A quick beat of the honest amendment: climbing-height toys taken off an unanchored bookcase today, bracket scheduled for the week. Calm, warm.

## SHORT 5: The needle takes a second Safety step
**Topic: the honest friction-meter second step, and the room-by-room payoff. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The needle moved again, and again nothing got cleaner.

**Script:**
Go and look at the friction meter, because it has moved again, for the same reason as last time, only more so. The first Safety step came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass. This step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room, all the hidden ones a first pass could never reach. And again, notice what did not change: nothing got cleaner, nothing got tidier, no object found a better home. The daily friction of living here is exactly what it was this morning. What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving. The step is a touch larger than the first, because you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many, and it is still measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks. The goal is still ahead, because Safety is finished only when the home is made to keep itself safe. That is the next chapter.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The needle moved again."
- 12s: "Again, nothing got cleaner."
- 28s: "A whole home's worth of quiet dangers is gone."
- 40s: "The second Safety step. The goal is still ahead."
