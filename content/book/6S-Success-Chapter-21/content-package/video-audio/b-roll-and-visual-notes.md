# B-Roll and Visual Notes: Find and Remove Hazards

**Chapter 21, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** find-and-remove-hazards
**Use:** Shot list, on-screen text moments, and lower-third suggestions for the YouTube, short-form, and any companion video cuts.

**Visual vocabulary to keep consistent (from the chapter):**
- The hero device is the hazard hunt: not a gadget but a habit of moving through the house. Show it as a calm, unhurried figure walking room by room with a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, asking the same short set of questions in every space and fixing what they find before they leave. Three things make it a hunt and not another glance: it is systematic (room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped), it is fixed (the same short list carried into every room), and it ends in action (finding a hazard and removing it are one motion). The register is calm and almost boring on purpose, because boring is what thorough looks like. What you are building is not fear; it is a home searched all the way through.
- The rule carries the whole chapter, frozen, render verbatim: "Find it, and fix it now." Its companion is the method, also frozen, render verbatim: "Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave." The definition box is also frozen: "A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden."
- The five safety questions are frozen and are the portable tool, render verbatim: 1. Could someone fall here? 2. Could something here burn, or catch fire? 3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? 4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush? 5. Could water and electricity meet? Show them as a clean card, five rows, each question a bold slate heading with two or three of the hidden hazards it surfaces beside it, and the rule in terracotta at the foot.
- The starting point is the home from the end of Chapter 20: cleared, arranged, freshly cleaned, self-keeping, and given one first safety pass on the obvious dangers. It is genuinely lovely and, by every measure so far, safe at a glance. This chapter does not re-clean it, does not re-tidy it, and does not rebuild anything, and it does NOT re-teach the safety lens or the triage; those are Chapter 20 and are settled. Show the finished home carrying a few green "handled" dots from the first pass, and then let the hunt reveal the many hidden hazards a glance never reached. The hunt is a deeper look at the same home.
- The reframe the chapter rests on, frozen: "A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group." Draw it as a whole-home cutaway with a few green dots (the obvious few, handled last chapter) and many small red hazard marks (the hidden many), one or two in every room, on perfectly ordinary objects. The green dots are few and the red marks are many. Warm line-and-flat-color style.
- Camouflage is the enemy the hunt is built to defeat, frozen: "The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain." Show each object reading first as an ordinary, useful thing, then a small red mark naming the quiet second job. None of them is messy, misplaced, or dirty. You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared; you are hunting for tidy, useful things doing a dangerous second job.
- Familiarity is why the hidden hazards hide: the cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now, and the bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything. Show the same familiar eye sliding over both the harmless and the dangerous. This is not a failure of attention or care; it is just how familiarity works, and the fix is to run a method rather than trust the glance.
- The found-not-fixed reframe carries the rule's logic, frozen: "A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone." Show the notepad trap: a home walked with a notepad, hazards listed to deal with later, quietly turning a hazard hunt into a hazard museum. Then swap the notepad for a roll of tape and a box, and let finding and fixing be one motion. The honest amendment when a full fix is bigger than now: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now.
- The worked example is the room-by-room hunt, walked the way you would actually do it: kitchen (pot handle turned in, butane lighter up, knives slid back from the counter's edge), bathroom (hairdryer away from the filled basin, a grip mat, a nightlight), living room (tall bookcase strapped to the wall, the old remote's loose battery cover taped shut, the lamp cord out from under the rug and routed along the wall), stairs and hall (curled runner tacked flat, a nightlight at the top of the stairs), child's room (looped blind cord wound up onto a cleat out of reach, small parts and coins moved up), launch pad (the little space heater moved from a foot from the curtain to a clear spot, charging cords tucked). Each is an ordinary object doing a quiet second job, each fix small and quick, a red hazard mark turning into a green fixed dot. The hunt pays off Chapter 20's three teasers: the button battery in the old remote, the space heater by the curtain, the lamp cord under the rug.
- The factory-floor provenance is referenced lightly and home-scaled: the five questions come, home-scaled, from the source's "Steps to Complete Safety", and the rule find it and fix it now is the source's oldest safety instruction, "address all possible safety issues immediately", shrunk to fit a home. Reference lightly, do not overclaim.
- The friction meter TAKES A SECOND SAFETY STEP this chapter, a STEP, not a milestone, a touch larger than the first but still measured. The live needle steps a little further past Chapter 19's marked SHINE COMPLETE milestone tick toward the calm side, slightly larger than the previous chapter's move. Chapter 20's first-Safety-step position is now a dashed ghost just behind the live needle; Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone sits further back as a small passed marker; the earlier Shine steps, the Straighten-complete, and the Sort-complete milestones are fainter still. A terracotta marker labels the reached position "SAFETY · STEP 2". Larger because the whole home was hunted and the hidden many removed, not just the obvious few; measured because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks, and safety moves it in the smaller increments of things locked down rather than swept out. The GOAL crosshair, tagged "purpose", stays ahead on the green calm side, clearly not yet reached, because Safety completes only in the next chapter and Standardize and Sustain still follow. As in Chapter 20 the needle moves not because daily friction dropped but because the chance of harm did.
- Warm daylight, lived-in homes, real households. Not a styled catalog look. Hazard marks calm and quiet, never lurid; this is a steady hand with a roll of tape, not an alarm going off.
- Keep the whole thing calm, warm, and loving, and let it carry the note of relief that safety done well brings, because a hazard removed is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again. No force or violence framing. Nothing is attacked, blitzed, blasted, purged, or waged war on; you hunt for hazards the way you hunt for your keys, and you remove them the way you remove a typo. The tone is anti-shame: a home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space, not because anyone was negligent, and the fix is five plain questions carried room by room, not guilt.

The only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead.

### Shot 5: The five questions (the portable tool)
- A clean card titled "The Five Questions · ask in every room". Five rows, each with the frozen question in a bold slate heading and, beside it, two or three of the hidden hazards it surfaces. Fall: sliding rug, cluttered stair, wet floor by the tub. Burn or fire: heater by a curtain, overloaded outlet, candle under a shelf, dryer lint. Poison, choke, or strangle: chemicals and medicine in reach, button batteries and small parts, looped blind cord. Fall, cut, or crush: unanchored tall bookcase, heavy box up high, knives at the counter edge. Water and electricity: hairdryer by the basin, frayed cord near the sink. At the foot, in terracotta, the rule: Find it, and fix it now.
- The point: five questions, small enough to hold in your head, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens on the same space: fall looks down and at the edges; burn looks for heat near things that burn and electricity asked to do too much; poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped; fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free; water and electricity looks in the wet rooms. You do not need to become an inspector; you need five plain questions asked in every room.
- Coverage: one hold on the whole card; one that walks down the five rows, each heading lighting as it is read; one on the terracotta rule at the foot.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Five questions. Ask all five, in every room." then "Find it, and fix it now."
- **Lower third:** The five questions · ask in every room

### Shot 6: Find it, and fix it now (the rule, and the notepad trap)
- The notepad trap drawn plainly: a home walked with a notepad, hazards listed to deal with later, and the hunt quietly turning back into a hazard museum, every listed danger still live on the shelf. Then the notepad set down and a roll of tape and an empty box picked up instead, and finding and fixing becoming one motion: a cord tucked, a bottle moved up, a latch fitted, a rug taped, all on the spot. And the honest amendment, drawn once: an unanchored bookcase whose bracket you do not have yet, the climbing-height toys taken off it today, the bracket scheduled for the week, the danger reduced to near nothing now.
- The point: the notepad feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. Between finding a danger and removing it there is a gap, and everything bad a hazard can do, it can do in that gap, so you close the gap to nothing. The fix is usually smaller than the worry was. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.
- Coverage: one on the notepad and the hazard museum; one on the swap to tape and box, a hazard fixed on the spot; one on the honest amendment, made safe now, finished later.
- **On-screen text moment:** "A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled." then "The only safe place for a danger is gone."
