# Chapter 21 Key Takeaways: Find and Remove Hazards

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **This chapter makes Safety thorough.** It is the second chapter of Part Six. Chapter 20 took the safety lens for a first walk and handled the obvious dangers; this chapter is the search that first pass was not, a calm, room-by-room hunt for the hazards that hide and the discipline of removing each one on the spot.

2. **The obvious hazards are the few; the hidden ones are the many.** A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.

3. **Familiarity is why the hidden hazards hide.** The things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now, and the unanchored bookcase reads, to a glance, as furniture.

4. **A glance is not built to catch a hidden hazard.** A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small, particular set. The only reliable way to find the rest is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead.

5. **The hero device: the hazard hunt.** A calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.

6. **Three things make it a hunt, not another glance.** It is systematic (room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped); it is fixed (the same short list of questions carried into every room, so the search does not depend on what you remember to look for); and it ends in action (you fix what you find before you leave, so finding and removing are one motion). The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point: boring is what thorough looks like.

7. **The method, frozen.** Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.

8. **What the hunt defeats is camouflage.** The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain. You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared, but for ordinary, tidy, useful things doing a dangerous second job.

9. **The five safety questions, frozen.** In every room, ask: could someone fall here; could something here burn, or catch fire; could something here poison, choke, or strangle; could something here fall, cut, or crush; could water and electricity meet. Between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home.

10. **Each question is a different lens.** Fall looks down and at the edges; burn looks for heat near things that burn and electricity asked to do too much; poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped; fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free; water and electricity looks in the wet rooms. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector, only to ask all five in every room.

11. **The rule: find it, and fix it now.** This is the source's oldest safety instruction, address all possible safety issues immediately, home-scaled. It is the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list.

12. **The found-not-fixed reframe, frozen.** A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.

13. **The notepad is the common trap.** Walking the home listing hazards to deal with later feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. Carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad, and let finding and fixing be one motion.

14. **The fix is usually smaller than the worry was.** Most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove: a cord tucked out of the walkway, a bottle moved to a high shelf, a latch fitted, a rug taped, a pot handle turned in, a heavy thing brought down.

15. **The honest exception.** Now and then a hazard's full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding. The rule still holds, with a small amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. Reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done on the spot.

16. **A room-by-room hunt, worked.** Kitchen (pot handle turned in, lighter up, knives back from the edge), bathroom (hairdryer away from the basin, a grip mat, a nightlight), living room (bookcase strapped, remote battery taped shut, lamp cord out from under the rug), stairs and hall (runner tacked flat, a nightlight in), child's room (blind cord cleated up, small parts moved high), launch pad (space heater moved from the curtain, cords tucked). None were messes; they were ordinary objects doing a quiet second job.

17. **The hunt pays off Chapter 20's teasers.** The button battery in the old remote, the space heater a foot from the curtain, and the lamp cord run under the rug, the three hazards Chapter 20 said would take a calmer, more systematic search, are found and removed here.

18. **The friction meter takes a second Safety step.** The first step came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass; this step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room. Again nothing got cleaner or tidier, but the home's exposure to harm dropped, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, the friction of the worst day.

19. **The step is a little larger than the first, and still measured, and both are honest.** Larger, because you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many rather than the obvious few. Measured, because the earlier S's already cleared most of what this meter tracks, and safety moves it in the smaller increments of things locked down rather than swept out. It is a step, not a milestone.

20. **Safety, made thorough; next is making it last.** You carried five plain questions through the whole home and removed what they found. What remains is the question of tomorrow, because homes do not hold still. One Idea to Keep: go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions, then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone. Chapter 22, Improve Overall Safety, completes the fourth S.
