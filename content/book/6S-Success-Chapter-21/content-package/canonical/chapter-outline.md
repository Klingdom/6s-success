## 1. Chapter Opening: The search the first pass was not
Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk and handled a handful of real dangers: the smoke alarm has a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch, the rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides, and you felt the particular relief of a worry set down. The honest next thing, said not to take that relief away but to finish the job it started: the dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question because they are the ones we already half-know about. And the obvious hazards are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all; it is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances precisely because a glance is not built to catch it. So this chapter is the search the first pass was not: a calm, thorough, room-by-room hunt for the hazards that hide, and, just as much, the discipline of removing each one the moment you find it.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- See why the obvious hazards are the few, and the hidden ones the many
- Run a hazard hunt: a systematic, room-by-room search that leaves nothing to memory
- Carry the same five safety questions into every room, so the search is complete
- Recognize the camouflaged hazards a first pass always walks past
- Remove each hazard the moment you find it, because a danger is only handled once it is gone

## 3. From a Glance to a Search
The whole reason for this chapter is a simple, slightly uncomfortable fact about how we see our own homes. A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small and particular set. But the eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and has long since stopped seeing most of what is in it. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now; the bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture. This is not a failure of attention or care; it is just how familiarity works, and the dangerous things become invisible right along with the harmless ones. So the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and start running a method: you go looking, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the thoroughness reframe): A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.

## 4. The Hazard Hunt
The hero, less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house. Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you remember to look for on a given day. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point; boring is what thorough looks like. What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage, because that is what hides the dangerous ones: none of those objects looks like a hazard because none of them is only a hazard. You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared, but for ordinary, tidy, useful things doing a dangerous second job.
- Definition box (frozen): A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the method): Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the camouflage reframe): The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.

## 5. The Five Questions
The short list you carry into every room, home-scaled from the questions the method has always asked on a safety walk, deliberately small enough to hold in your head. In every room, ask: could someone fall here; could something here burn, or catch fire; could something here poison, choke, or strangle; could something here fall, cut, or crush; could water and electricity meet. Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens on the same space: fall looks down and at the edges; burn looks for heat near things that burn and electricity asked to do too much; poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped; fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free; water and electricity looks in the wet rooms. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector; you need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room, and when the answer is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for.
- Frozen five safety questions (the set): 1. Could someone fall here? 2. Could something here burn, or catch fire? 3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? 4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush? 5. Could water and electricity meet?

## 6. Find It, and Fix It Now
The rule, and the strong temptation to resist: to do the finding now and the fixing later, walking the whole home with a notepad and coming back when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum. The notepad feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one: the child does not know the button battery is on your list, the fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend. Between finding a danger and removing it there is a gap, and everything bad a hazard can do, it can do in that gap, so you close the gap to nothing. This is the source's oldest safety instruction, address all possible safety issues immediately, home-scaled. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. The honest exception: now and then a full fix is genuinely bigger than "now", and the rule still holds with a small amendment, make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the found-not-fixed reframe): A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.
- Callout (Quick Win): Pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out: the cord tucked away, the pills moved up high, the rug taped down, the heavy thing brought off the top shelf. One room, thoroughly safe, today.
- Callout (6S Tip): Hunt every room twice, once standing and once low. Crouch to a small child's height and look again: the outlet at ankle level, the tablecloth a toddler can pull, the cleaning spray under the sink, the wobbling table leg. A whole layer of hazards lives in the band of space adults never occupy.

## 7. A Room-by-Room Hunt
The worked whole-home sweep, walked the way you would actually do it, so the reader sees how ordinary the finds are and how quickly each one closes. Kitchen (it holds more of all five kinds of hazard than any other room): the slick spot where the dishwasher leaks, the pot handle turned out over the front edge turned in, the butane lighter from an open drawer moved up high, the knives slid back from the counter's edge. Bathroom, quick and high-value: the hairdryer unplugged and moved away from the filled basin, a grip pad for the bath mat, a nightlight in. Living room, where camouflage lives: the tall unanchored bookcase gets an anti-tip strap today, the old remote's loose battery cover gets taped shut, and the lamp cord Chapter 20 promised would take a real hunt comes out from under the rug and is routed along the wall. Stairs and hall: the curled hall runner tacked flat, a nightlight at the top of the stairs. The child's room: the looped blind cord wound up onto a cleat out of reach, the small parts and coins moved up. The launch pad, paying off the last of Chapter 20's teasers: the little space heater moved from a foot from the curtain to a clear spot with a rule about the curtain, the charging cords tucked. Nothing needed a deep clean or a rearrange; it was already sorted and arranged and clean. It just, in a handful of small ways, could have hurt someone, and now, room by room, it cannot.
- Infographic (the room-by-room hunt): a simple warm floor-plan or ordered vertical list of rooms walked in sequence, Kitchen, Bathroom, Living Room, Stairs and Hall, Child's Room, Launch Pad, each showing one or two representative hazards found and removed, a small red hazard mark turning into a green "fixed" dot. A green count of "fixed" grows as the route goes on. Caption: one home, walked room by room, each hazard fixed before moving on.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Turn the hunt into a game. Give each person a room and the five questions and send everyone off to find hazards at once, and the younger the sharper for the low ones. Then come back together and fix the worst finds as a team. A home searched by five pairs of eyes misses far less than one.

## 9. Safety, Made Thorough
Short retrospective and hand-off. You carried five plain questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and removed what they found before you left each room. That is the fourth S made thorough: not a first pass on the obvious dangers, but a complete sweep that reached the hidden ones, the button battery and the blind cord and the unanchored bookcase and the heater by the curtain. What is left is not more hazards to find, at least not today; it is the question of tomorrow, because homes do not hold still: new things come in, batteries die, a bracket loosens, a child grows tall enough to reach a shelf that was safe last month. Making safety survive that, turning a one-time sweep into a home that stays safe, and preparing calmly for the few emergencies you cannot prevent, is a different kind of work and the next chapter, the one that completes the fourth S. For now, you have done the thing that had to come first: you found the dangers that were already here and removed them. Hold on to the five questions, because they work in any room, on any day, and a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Walking the home with a notepad, listing hazards to deal with later, and calling that safety. The list is not the fix. Every danger you write down and leave in place is still live. Carry a roll of tape and a box instead of a notepad, and fix as you find.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): Go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone.
