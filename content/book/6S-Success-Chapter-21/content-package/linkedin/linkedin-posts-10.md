## 2. Familiarity is a kind of camouflage

Why do the dangerous things in a home hide so well? Not because they are hard to see. Because you have seen them ten thousand times.

The things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones.

The cord that has run under the rug for three years is not going to announce itself now. The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything.

This is not a failure of attention or of care. It is just how familiarity works.

Which means the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead.

## 3. The hazard hunt

If a glance cannot catch the hidden hazards, what can? A hunt.

A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on.

Three things make it a hunt, not another glance. It is systematic: room by room, so nothing is skipped. It is fixed: the same questions every time, so nothing depends on memory. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave.

The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like.

Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.

## 4. The whole method, in one line

You do not need a checklist app or a home inspector. You need a habit of moving through the house.

Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.

That is the whole method. Systematic, so no room is skipped. Fixed, so the search does not depend on what you happen to remember today. And it ends in action, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion, not two.

It is almost aggressively boring. That is exactly what thorough looks like. A first pass depends on the danger being obvious. A hunt depends only on your walking the whole home and asking.

## 5. The five questions

Here is the short list you carry into every room, small enough to hold in your head.

In every room, ask:

Could someone fall here?
Could something here burn, or catch fire?
Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?
Could something here fall, cut, or crush?
Could water and electricity meet?

Between them, these five catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each is a different lens. Fall looks down and at the edges. Burn looks for heat near things that burn. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low, and for the small and the looped. Fall, cut, or crush looks up, at what is heavy and free. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms.

You do not need to become an inspector. You need five plain questions, asked in every room.

## 6. The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one

We picture hazards as obvious: dramatic, dirty, easy to spot. Most are not.

The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.

None of those objects looks like a hazard, because none of them is only a hazard. The lamp is a good lamp. It just also has a cord strung across where people walk.

You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared, but for ordinary, useful things quietly doing a dangerous second job.

## 7. Find it, and fix it now

Once you start finding hazards, there is a strong pull to write them all down and deal with them later. Resist it.

A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone before its turn comes up.

The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend.

So you close the gap to nothing. You fix each hazard the moment you find it, before you move on.

Now and then a full fix is bigger than now. Then make it safe now, and schedule only the part that cannot be done on the spot.

## 8. Carry tape, not a notepad

Here is the most common way a safety effort quietly fails: you walk the home with a notepad, list every hazard, and call that safety.

The list is not the fix. A written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. Every danger you note and leave in place is still live.

The move that works is small: carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box instead of a notepad, and let finding and fixing be one motion.

Most of what you find will yield to that, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove. A cord tucked away, a bottle moved up, a latch fitted, a rug taped.

The fix is usually smaller than the worry was.

## 9. A hunt, walked room by room

Here is how a hunt actually runs, and how ordinary the finds are.

Kitchen: a pot handle turned out over the stove edge gets turned in; a lighter in an open drawer goes up high; the knives slide back from the edge. Bathroom: the hairdryer moves away from the filled basin; a nightlight goes in.

Living room, where camouflage lives: the tall unanchored bookcase gets a strap to the wall; the old remote's battery cover gets taped shut; the lamp cord comes out from under the rug. Stairs: the curled runner gets tacked flat. Child's room: the looped blind cord gets wound onto a cleat, out of reach.

None were messes. They were ordinary objects doing a quiet second job, and now, room by room, they cannot.
