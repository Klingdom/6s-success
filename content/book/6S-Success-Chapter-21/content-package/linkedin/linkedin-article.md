# You Handled the Obvious Dangers. The Ones That Hurt People Are Still Hiding in Plain Sight.

Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk through your home, and it showed you a handful of real dangers, and you handled them. The smoke alarm has a fresh battery. The cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch. The rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides. If you did that, you have already done something that matters, and you felt the particular relief of it, the sense of a worry set down.

Here is the honest next thing, and it is not said to take that relief away but to finish the job it started. The dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question, because they are the ones we already half-know about. And the obvious hazards, it turns out, are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it will survive any number of quick glances precisely because a glance is not built to catch it.

So this chapter is the search the first pass was not. It is a calm, thorough, room-by-room hunt for the hazards that hide, and, just as much, it is the discipline of removing each one the moment you find it.

## A first pass finds the obvious. A hunt finds the rest.

The whole reason for this chapter is a simple, slightly uncomfortable fact about how we see our own homes. A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small and particular set. But the eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times, and it has long since stopped seeing most of what is in it.

> A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.

This is not a failure of attention or of care. It is just how familiarity works: the things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones. The cord that has run under the rug for three years is not going to announce itself now.

> The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything.

Which means the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and start running a method instead. You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room.

## The hazard hunt

So here is the tool, and it is less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house.

> A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.

Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you happen to remember to look for. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion, not two.

> The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like.

> Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.

What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage.

> The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.

You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job, and the only way to catch them at it is to ask.

## The five questions

Here is the short list you carry into every room. It comes, home-scaled, from the questions the method has always asked on a safety walk, and it is deliberately small enough to hold in your head.

In every room, ask:

1. Could someone fall here?
2. Could something here burn, or catch fire?
3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?
4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush?
5. Could water and electricity meet?

Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens, and each finds a different family of hidden hazard. Fall looks down and at the edges. Burn looks for heat near things that burn and electricity asked to do too much. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped. Fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector, only to ask all five in every room.

## Find it, and fix it now

There is a strong temptation, once you start finding things, to do the finding now and the fixing later: to walk the whole home with a notepad, write down every hazard, and come back to deal with them when you have time.

> Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum.

> A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.

The notepad feels like progress, and it is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend. So you close the gap to nothing: you fix each hazard the moment you find it, before you move on. This is the source's oldest safety instruction, address all possible safety issues immediately, and it is the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list. Carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad, and let finding and fixing be one motion.

Most of what you find will yield to that, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove. The fix is usually smaller than the worry was. Now and then a hazard's full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding. The rule still holds, with a small honest amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. Reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done on the spot.

> What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.

## A room-by-room hunt

Let me walk it with you, the way you would actually do it, so you can see how ordinary the finds are and how quickly each one closes.

Start in the kitchen. A pot handle is turned out over the front edge of the stove where a passing hip or a reaching child could catch it, so you turn it in. A butane lighter sits in an open drawer at child height, and it goes up high. The knives are pushed to the very edge of the counter, so you slide them back. In the bathroom, the hairdryer is plugged in beside the filled basin, so it moves to a drawer away from the water, and a nightlight goes in, because most bathroom falls happen in the dark.

The living room is where camouflage lives. The tall bookcase has stood free of the wall for years and never fallen, and it is exactly the kind of thing a climbing toddler can bring down, so it gets an anti-tip strap to the wall today. The old remote holds a button battery a small child could reach, so a strip of tape holds the cover shut. And there, at last, is the lamp cord run under the edge of the rug, both a thing to trip on and a wire being slowly crushed where you cannot see it. It comes out and gets routed along the wall. On the stairs, the hall runner has curled at one end, so it gets tacked flat, and a nightlight goes in at the top. In the child's room, the blind cord hangs in a loop at a toddler's height, one of the quietest serious hazards in any home, so it gets wound onto a cleat well out of reach. And the launch pad by the door pays off the last teaser: the little space heater sits a foot from the curtain, so it moves to a clear spot.

None of these were messes. They were ordinary objects doing a quiet second job, and now, room by room, they cannot.

## The needle takes another Safety step

Go and look at the friction meter, because it has moved again, and this time it moved for the same reason as last time, only more so. The first Safety step, last chapter, came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass. This step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room, all the hidden ones a first pass could never reach. And again, notice what did not change: nothing got cleaner, nothing got tidier. The daily friction of living here is exactly what it was this morning.

> What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving.

The step is a touch larger than the first one, because a thorough hunt clears more than a first glance did, and it is still, in absolute terms, a measured one, and both of those are honest. Larger, because you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many. Measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks. Chapter 20's first Safety step is the dashed ghost just behind the live needle now, and the goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead, because Safety is finished only when the home is made to keep itself safe, which is one more chapter, and Standardize and Sustain follow that.

## Safety, made thorough

You carried five plain questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and you removed what they found before you left each room. That is the fourth S made thorough: not a first pass on the obvious dangers, but a complete sweep that reached the hidden ones, the button battery and the blind cord and the unanchored bookcase and the heater by the curtain, the hazards that would have survived any number of glances because they were wearing the disguise of ordinary things.

What is left is not more hazards to find, at least not today; it is the question of tomorrow, because homes do not hold still. Making safety survive that is a different kind of work, and it is the next chapter, the one that completes the fourth S.

> Hold on to the five questions, because they work in any room, on any day, and a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe.
