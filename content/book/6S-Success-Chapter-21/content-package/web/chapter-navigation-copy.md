## You are here blurb
You are at Chapter 21, the second chapter of Part Six, Safety, and it continues the fourth of the six S's, the "plus one" American practice added to 5S. Chapter 20 opened Safety: it changed the question from is this in order to could this hurt someone, taught the safety lens, triaged what it found, and handled the worst few dangers on one first high-value pass. This chapter is the search that first pass was not. The dangers you found on that first walk were the obvious ones, the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question, and the obvious hazards are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all; it is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances precisely because a glance is not built to catch it. The reason the hidden ones hide is familiarity: the things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones, so the cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself and the unanchored bookcase reads, to a glance, as furniture. This is not a failure of attention or care; it is just how familiarity works, and the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead. The hero is the hazard hunt: a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance: it is systematic, room by room in an order so no space is skipped; it is fixed, the same short list of questions carried into every room so the search does not depend on what you happen to remember; and it ends in action, you fix what you find before you leave, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point, because boring is what thorough looks like. What the hunt is built to defeat is camouflage: the dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one, it is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it, the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain. Into every room you carry the same five questions, home-scaled from the source's Steps to Complete Safety: could someone fall here; could something here burn, or catch fire; could something here poison, choke, or strangle; could something here fall, cut, or crush; could water and electricity meet. Between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home, and each is a different lens finding a different family of hidden hazard. The rule that makes the hunt end in action rather than in a list is find it, and fix it now, home-scaled from the source's address all possible safety issues immediately, and it is carried by the found-not-fixed reframe: a hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled, it is scheduled, and the only safe place for a danger is gone, so you carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad, and let finding and fixing be one motion. The chapter walks a worked whole-home sweep, kitchen, bathroom, living room, stairs and hall, child's room, and the launch pad, that pays off Chapter 20's teasers, the button battery in the old remote, the space heater a foot from the curtain, and the lamp cord run under the rug. Because a whole-home hunt removes a roomful of hidden hazards, far more than a first pass, the friction meter takes a second Safety step off the Shine-complete milestone toward the goal, a little larger than Chapter 20's first step because you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many, and still measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks and safety moves it in the smaller, quieter increments of things locked down rather than swept out. This chapter does not re-teach the safety lens or the protect-people-first triage (Chapter 20), does not build ongoing safety systems or emergency preparation (Chapter 22), does not write documented standards (Standardize), and does not solve long-term adherence (Sustain). It is a real, earned step and not a milestone: Chapter 20's first-Safety-step position is now the dashed ghost just behind the live needle, Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone sits further back as a passed marker, and the goal, out on the calm side, stays ahead, because Safety completes only when it is made to last and Standardize and Sustain still follow.
