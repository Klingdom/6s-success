# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 21, Find and Remove Hazards

Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk and handled a handful of real dangers. The smoke alarm has a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch, the rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides, and you felt the particular relief of a worry set down. Here is the honest next thing, said not to take that relief away but to finish the job it started: the dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question, because they are the ones we already half-know about. And the obvious hazards are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances precisely because a glance is not built to catch it. So this chapter is the search the first pass was not: a calm, thorough, room-by-room hunt for the hazards that hide, and, just as much, the discipline of removing each one the moment you find it.

The reason the hidden ones hide is not carelessness. It is familiarity. A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small and particular set. But the eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and has long since stopped seeing most of what is in it. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now; the bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything. This is not a failure of attention or care; it is just how familiarity works, and the dangerous things become invisible right along with the harmless ones. So the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and start running a method. You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before.

That method is the hazard hunt, and it is less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house. Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped because you happened not to think of it. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you remember to look for on a given day. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion, not two. The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like. What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage, because that is what hides the dangerous ones. The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one; it is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain. You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job.

Into every room you carry the same five questions, home-scaled from the questions the method has always asked on a safety walk and deliberately small enough to hold in your head. Could someone fall here. Could something here burn, or catch fire. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle. Could something here fall, cut, or crush. Could water and electricity meet. Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home, and each one is a different lens on the same space. Fall looks down and at the edges: the sliding rug, the tread with a stray shoe, the wet patch by the tub, the top of the stairs. Burn looks for heat too near things that burn and electricity asked to do too much: the heater by the curtain, the candle under the shelf, the overloaded outlet, the dryer lint. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped: the chemicals and medicines in reach, the button batteries and coins and small parts, the looped blind cord at a child's height. Fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free: the unanchored tall bookcase, the heavy box up high, the knives at the counter's edge. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms: the hairdryer near the filled basin, the frayed cord by the sink. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector; you need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room.

There is one temptation to resist, and it is a strong one: to do the finding now and the fixing later, walking the whole home with a notepad and coming back when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum. The notepad feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend. Between finding a danger and removing it there is a gap, and everything bad a hazard can do, it can do in that gap, so you close the gap to nothing. A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. Now and then a full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding, and the rule still holds with a small amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list.

None of this is about living afraid or turning your home into a place of dread. It is the opposite. A home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space; the hunt is a method, not a verdict on you. You hunt for hazards the way you hunt for your keys, and you remove them the way you remove a typo, plainly, once, and then they are gone.
