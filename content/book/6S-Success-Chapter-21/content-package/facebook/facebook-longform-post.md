# Facebook Longform Post: One Safety Walk Caught the Obvious Few. The Hunt Found the Rest.

A longer, story-driven post from Chapter 21 of 6S Success: Home Edition, the second chapter of Part Six and the one that makes Safety thorough with a calm, room-by-room hazard hunt. Conversational, like talking to a friend. Ends with a soft CTA. No em dashes. Body hard-capped 300 to 450 words.

---

Last month I did one honest safety walk of my house and set down a real worry. Fresh battery in the smoke alarm. Cleaning bottles up high. Grip tape under the rug at the top of the stairs. It felt finished, and the relief was genuine.

Then I read the line that told me it was only half the job.

"A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group."

That landed. The dangers I had caught were the ones I already half-knew about, the loud ones. The rest were still there, and they were the majority. They survive a quick walk because they are not messes. They are ordinary things doing a quiet second job.

"The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain."

So I stopped glancing and started hunting. One room at a time, in order, carrying the same five plain questions into each: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. And one rule that changed everything.

"Find it, and fix it now."

No notepad. Because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as one you never wrote down.

"A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone."

So I carried a roll of tape and an empty box instead. The kitchen pot handle turned in. The bathroom hairdryer moved from the basin. In the living room the bookcase finally got anchored, the old remote's battery taped shut, and that lamp cord came out from under the rug. The blind cord wound up onto a cleat. The little heater moved off the curtain.

Nothing got tidier. A roomful of quiet dangers is simply gone.
