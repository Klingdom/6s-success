## Post 1 · The obvious few, and the hidden many

You can do one honest safety walk of your home and handle the dangers that jump out: the dead smoke alarm, the bottles in reach, the sliding rug. That is real, and worth doing. But the dangers that jump out are the minority.

"A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group."

The obvious ones are the few you already half-know about. The rest sit in plain sight, quiet and ordinary, and a quick glance was never built to catch them. Finding them takes something slower and more deliberate: a hunt.

## Post 2 · Most hazards are wearing a disguise

The reason the hidden hazards hide is not carelessness. It is familiarity. The things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones go invisible right along with the harmless ones.

"The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything."

That is the whole trap. The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one.

"The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain."

You are not looking for mess. You are looking for useful, tidy things doing a quiet second job.

## Post 3 · The hazard hunt, and five plain questions

Here is the whole method, and it is almost boringly simple.

"Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave."

The questions are five, small enough to hold in your head, and you ask all five in every room:

1. Could someone fall here?
2. Could something here burn, or catch fire?
3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?
4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush?
5. Could water and electricity meet?

Each one is a different lens on the same room. Between them they catch the great majority of what actually hurts people at home. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector. You need five plain questions, and the willingness to ask them everywhere.

"The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like."

## Post 4 · Find it, and fix it now

There is one strong temptation on a hazard hunt: to do the finding now and the fixing later, walking the home with a notepad and coming back when you have time. Resist it. The notepad feels like progress and is not.

"A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone."

A written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as one you never wrote down.

"The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend."

So carry a roll of tape and an empty box instead of a notepad, and close the gap to nothing. Most fixes are small: a cord tucked away, a bottle up high, a rug taped, a heavy thing brought down. The rule is short.

"Find it, and fix it now."

## Post 5 · The needle takes a second Safety step

One honest note to close on. Last chapter the friction meter took its first Safety step, from removing a few real dangers on a first pass. This chapter it moves again, a little further, because this time you hunted the whole home room by room and removed the hidden many, not just the obvious few.

Notice what did not change. Nothing got cleaner. Nothing got tidier. No object found a better home. The daily friction of living here is exactly what it was this morning.

"What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving."

The step is a touch larger than the first, and still a measured one, because the earlier S's already cleared most of what this meter tracks and safety moves it in the quieter increments of things locked down rather than swept out. The goal is still ahead, because Safety completes only when it is made to last, and that is the next chapter.

"Hold on to the five questions, because they work in any room, on any day, and a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe."
