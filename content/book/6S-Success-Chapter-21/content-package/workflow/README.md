## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
A first pass catches the dangers that are obvious the moment you finally look, and walks straight past the rest, and the rest are the majority. Chapter 20 opened Safety with the safety lens (could this hurt someone) and did one first high-value pass on the worst and most obvious dangers; this chapter makes Safety thorough. The reframe it rests on is plain: a first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look, a hunt finds the ones that never will be, and most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group. Familiarity is why the hidden hazards hide, because the things we live with become invisible to us and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones: the cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now, and the unanchored bookcase reads, to a glance, as furniture. A glance is not built to catch a hidden hazard, so the only reliable way to find the rest is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead. The hero is the hazard hunt: a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Three things make it a hunt, not another glance: it is systematic (room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped), it is fixed (the same short list of questions carried into every room), and it ends in action (you fix what you find before you leave). The five safety questions, home-scaled from the source's Steps to Complete Safety, each surface a different family of hidden hazard, and what the hunt defeats is camouflage, the ordinary object doing a quiet second job you never gave it. The rule is find it, and fix it now, with the honest amendment that when a full fix is bigger than now you make it safe now and schedule only the rest, because a hazard found but not fixed is not handled, it is scheduled, and the only safe place for a danger is gone. A worked whole-home sweep pays off Chapter 20's teasers (the button battery in the old remote, the space heater a foot from the curtain, the cord run under the rug), and the friction meter takes a second Safety step, a little larger than the first because the whole home was hunted rather than glanced at, with the goal still ahead because Safety completes only when it is made to last. Hands off to Chapter 22 · Improve Overall Safety, which completes the fourth S: the safeguards that prevent new hazards, the preparation for the emergencies you cannot prevent, and the habit that keeps the home safe long after today's hunt.

## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule (hero): **Find it, and fix it now.**
- The method (frozen): **Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.**
- The five safety questions (frozen set): **Could someone fall here; could something here burn, or catch fire; could something here poison, choke, or strangle; could something here fall, cut, or crush; could water and electricity meet.**
- The definition box: **A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.**
- The thoroughness reframe: **A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.**
- The found-not-fixed reframe: **A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.**
- The camouflage reframe: **The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.**
- One Idea to Keep: **Go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone.**
