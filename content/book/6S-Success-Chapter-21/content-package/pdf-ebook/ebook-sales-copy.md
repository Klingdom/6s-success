# 6S Success: Home Edition (Sales Page Copy)

## You handled the obvious dangers. The hidden ones are the majority.

Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk through your home and handled a handful of real dangers. The smoke alarm has a fresh battery. The cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch. The rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides. You felt the particular relief of a worry set down, and you should, because it matters.

Here is the honest next thing, said not to take that relief away but to finish the job it started. The dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, the hazards that light up the instant you finally ask the safety question. And the obvious hazards are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances because a glance is not built to catch it. "The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything."

## Welcome to the thorough part of Safety.

Chapter 21 is the second chapter of Part Six, and Safety continues, the fourth of the six S's. Chapter 20 opened Safety with the lens and one first pass on the obvious dangers. This chapter is the search that first pass was not. It teaches no new way to tidy and clears no junk; the earlier S's already did that. What it hands you is a repeatable method and one rule, so that a home whose worst dangers are handled becomes a home that is, all the way through, genuinely safe.

> A first pass is a glance across a finished home. A hunt is a walk through it with a method and a roll of tape, room by room, missing nothing on purpose.

## A first pass finds the obvious. A hunt finds the rest.

The whole chapter rests on one simple, slightly uncomfortable fact about how we see our own homes. A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and stopped seeing most of what is in it.

> A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.

This is not a failure of attention or of care. It is just how familiarity works: the things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones. So the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and start running a method instead. "You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before."

## The hazard hunt: a method, not a glance.

The hero of the chapter is less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house.

> A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.

Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you remember to look for. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. "The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like."

> Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.

What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage. "The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain." You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things quietly doing a dangerous second job.

## The five questions.

Here is the short list you carry into every room, home-scaled from the questions the method has always asked on a safety walk, deliberately small enough to hold in your head.

> In every room, ask:
> 1. Could someone fall here?
> 2. Could something here burn, or catch fire?
> 3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?
> 4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush?
> 5. Could water and electricity meet?

Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens on the same space.

- **Could someone fall · looks down and at the edges** · the rug that slides, the tread with a stray shoe on it, the wet patch by the tub, the top of the stairs
- **Could something burn or catch fire · looks for heat near what burns** · the heater a foot from the curtain, the candle under a shelf, the outlet with a nest of plugs, the lint packed in the dryer trap
- **Could something poison, choke, or strangle · looks low and for the small and the looped** · the chemicals and medicines within reach, the button batteries and coins, the looped blind cord at a child's height
- **Could something fall, cut, or crush · looks up and at what is heavy and free** · the tall unanchored bookcase, the heavy box on the top shelf, the knives at the counter's edge
- **Could water and electricity meet · looks in the wet rooms** · the hairdryer near the filled basin, the frayed cord by the sink

You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector. You need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room, and when the answer is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for.

## Find it, and fix it now.

Once you start finding things, there is a strong temptation to do the finding now and the fixing later, to walk the whole home with a notepad and come back when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum.

> A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.

The notepad feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. "The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend." So you close the gap to nothing and fix each hazard the moment you find it. Carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. And for the rare hazard whose full fix is bigger than now, the rule holds with a small amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. "What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list."

## You do not have a minefield. You have a home.

One word about how the hunt is meant to feel, because the subject usually carries dread and this does not have to.

A home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space. The fix is a short set of questions carried room by room, not guilt and not fear. You hunt for hazards the way you hunt for your keys, and you remove them the way you remove a typo, calmly, one at a time. Nothing is attacked or waged war on; the hunt is quiet, ordinary attention. And what it buys you is the opposite of vigilance: a whole home searched once, its dangers gone, so you can stop carrying them around as worry.

## Carry tape, not a notepad.

One caution, so the hunt stays the thing that makes a home safer rather than the thing that produces a list and no fixes.

> Walking the home with a notepad, listing hazards to deal with later, and calling that safety. The list is not the fix. Every danger you write down and leave in place is still live. Carry a roll of tape and a box instead of a notepad, and fix as you find.

Finding and fixing are meant to be one motion. A hazard you write down and walk past is exactly as dangerous as it was before you noticed it. The only safe place for a danger is gone.

Go and look at the friction meter, because it has moved again, for the same reason as last time, only more so. The first Safety step came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass; this step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room, all the hidden ones a first pass could never reach. Again, notice what did not change: nothing got cleaner, nothing got tidier, and the daily friction of living here is exactly what it was this morning.

> Find it, and fix it now.

"What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving." The step is a touch larger than the first, because a thorough hunt clears more than a first glance did, and it is still, in absolute terms, a measured one, and both are honest.
