# Find and Remove Hazards

**Part Six, Safety**
Chapter 21 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

## Chapter descriptor

The second chapter of Part Six, and Safety continues. Chapter 20 opened Safety, taught the safety lens (could this hurt someone), and did one first pass on the worst and most obvious dangers. This chapter delivers the thorough part it handed off. You meet the reframe the chapter rests on, that a first pass finds only the dangers obvious the moment you look and walks past the camouflaged majority, and you learn why familiarity makes the hidden hazards hide right along with the harmless ones. The hero is the hazard hunt, a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, carrying the same short set of safety questions into every space and removing each hazard before you move on. You carry the five questions, could someone fall, could something burn or catch fire, could something poison choke or strangle, could something fall cut or crush, could water and electricity meet, home-scaled from the method's own safety walk, and you learn the rule, find it and fix it now, and the found-not-fixed reframe behind it, that a hazard found but not fixed is scheduled, not handled. A worked room-by-room sweep pays off Chapter 20's teasers, the button battery, the space heater by the curtain, the cord under the rug. Nothing new is taught about tidying; the hunt works on a home already sorted, arranged, and clean.

## How to read this chapter

This is the second chapter of Part Six, Safety, and it picks up exactly where Chapter 20 left off, with a home that is cleared, arranged, kept clean, and given one first safety pass on the obvious dangers. It uses the safety lens and the triage that chapter opened and does not re-teach them; it takes that lens and applies it everywhere, on purpose. It is not about cleaning again, and it opens no new tool beyond the hunt and the five questions. It is about one turn: going from a glance to a search, so the hidden hazards a first pass always walks past stop being able to hide.

Read it through once for the idea, then take the five questions for a slow, room-by-room walk through the home you already have. The idea rests on one uncomfortable fact about how we see our own homes: a first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the things we live with have long since stopped standing out, the dangerous ones invisible right along with the harmless ones. So the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead. That method is the hazard hunt: systematic, so you go room by room in an order and skip nothing; fixed, so you carry the same five questions into every room and the search does not depend on what you happen to remember; and ended in action, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. What the hunt defeats is camouflage, the ordinary object doing a quiet second job, the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a reachable battery, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain. And the register throughout is calm, competent, and loving. This is not fear. A home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space, and the fix is a short set of questions carried room by room, not guilt.

Let finding and fixing be one motion, because the rule is find it, and fix it now. Carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one, and a hazard found but not fixed is not handled, it is scheduled. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. Now and then a full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, and the rule still holds with a small amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. Nothing here re-teaches the safety lens or the protect-people-first triage; that was Chapter 20, and this chapter uses them without re-explaining them. Nothing here builds ongoing safety systems, the safeguards that keep new hazards from taking hold, or the calm preparation for the emergencies you cannot prevent; that is Chapter 22, Improve Overall Safety, which completes the fourth S. Nothing here writes documented standards, which is Standardize, or solves long-term adherence, which is Sustain. Because a whole-home hunt removes a roomful of hidden hazards, the friction meter takes a second Safety step off the marked Shine-complete milestone toward the goal, a little larger than the first because the whole home was hunted, and still measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks and safety moves it in the smaller increments of things locked down rather than swept out. It is a step, not a milestone, and the goal is still ahead, because Safety completes only when it is made to last and Standardize and Sustain still follow.

If you only do one thing: pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out, the cord tucked away, the pills moved up high, the rug taped down, the heavy thing brought off the top shelf. One room, thoroughly safe, today. That small proof is the whole chapter, and it is what makes Safety thorough.
