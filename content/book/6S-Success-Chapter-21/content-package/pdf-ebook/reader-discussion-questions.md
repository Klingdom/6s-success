# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Find and Remove Hazards

*Chapter 21, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter opens by admitting that the dangers you handled on the first pass were the obvious ones, and that "A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group." Which do you suspect your home holds more of right now, the obvious few you have already spotted, or the hidden many, and what makes you say so?

2. On why the hidden hazards hide, the chapter says "The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything." What has been sitting in plain sight in your home so long that, in the chapter's words, it has become furniture to you, invisible right along with the harmless things around it?

3. The turn the chapter asks for is from a glance to a search: "You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before." How would your own home look different if you walked it as a stranger rather than as the person who has looked at it ten thousand times?

4. The hero is the hazard hunt, and the chapter is proud of how dull it is: "The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like." Does framing thoroughness as boring rather than heroic make the hunt feel more doable to you, or less, and why?

5. Stripped to its bones, the method is three sentences: "Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave." Which of those three do you think you would be most tempted to skip, going out of order, changing the questions, or leaving before fixing, and what would that cost you?

6. On what the hunt is built to defeat: "The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain." Which ordinary, useful object in your home can you already name that is quietly doing a dangerous second job?

7. The chapter draws a clear line: "You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job, and the only way to catch them at it is to ask." Had you assumed a tidy, well-sorted home was mostly past the danger, and where does that assumption break down for you?

8. The five questions are one short list carried into every room: could someone fall, could something burn or catch fire, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Take one room in your mind and ask all five. Which question turns up something the other four walked past?

9. The rule is find it, and fix it now, and the reason is this: "A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone." Is there a hazard in your home that has been on your mental schedule for a while, and what would it take to move it from scheduled to gone today?

10. The chapter names the notepad as the trap: "The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend." When have you written something down as a way of feeling you had dealt with it, and would carrying tape and a box instead of a notepad change how you work through a room?

11. On the rare hazard that cannot be finished on the spot, the rule holds with a small amendment: "make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now." Reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done today. Where in your home could you make something safe now even if the full fix has to wait, and what would the near-nothing version look like?

12. The Quick Win asks you to prove the whole idea in one move: "Pick one room and give it a full hunt right now. Ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out." Which room would you hunt first, and what do you already suspect the five questions will turn up there?

13. The worked sweep pays off the hazards Chapter 20 said would take a real hunt: the button battery in the old remote, the space heater a foot from the curtain, the lamp cord run under the rug. Which of those three, or which like them, do you recognize in your own home, and were you surprised that such ordinary things made the list?

14. The friction meter takes a second Safety step, a little larger than the first, and the chapter is honest that it is still measured: "What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving." Standing with the whole home hunted and its hidden dangers removed, and the goal still ahead because Safety completes only when it is made to last, what does it mean to you that "a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe," as the book turns to Chapter 22 and making Safety last?
