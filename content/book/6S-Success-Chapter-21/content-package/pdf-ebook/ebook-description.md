# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk through your home and handled a handful of real dangers: the smoke alarm has a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch, the rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides, and you felt the particular relief of a worry set down. Chapter 21 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where you learn that the dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, that the obvious hazards are the minority, and what to do about all the rest.

"Find and Remove Hazards" is the second chapter of Part Six, and Safety continues, the fourth of the six S's. Chapter 20 opened Safety with the lens, could this hurt someone, and did one first pass on the worst and most obvious dangers. This chapter delivers the thorough part it handed off. It teaches no new way to tidy; the earlier S's already cleared the junk and the mess, and the hunt works on a home that is already sorted, arranged, and clean. What it adds is a repeatable method and one rule, so that a home whose worst dangers are handled becomes a home that is, all the way through, genuinely safe. As the chapter puts it, "A first pass is a glance across a finished home. A hunt is a walk through it with a method and a roll of tape, room by room, missing nothing on purpose."

The chapter rests on one reframe, a simple and slightly uncomfortable fact about how we see our own homes. "A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group." A first pass works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small, particular set. But the eye doing a quick scan is the same eye that has looked at this room ten thousand times and has long since stopped seeing most of what is in it. "The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything." This is not a failure of attention or of care; it is just how familiarity works, and the dangerous things become invisible right along with the harmless ones. So the only reliable way to find the hidden hazards is to stop relying on what catches your eye and run a method instead. "You do not wait to notice a hazard. You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before."

The hero of the chapter is the hazard hunt, less a gadget than a habit of moving through the house. "A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden." Three things make it a hunt rather than another glance. It is systematic: you go room by room, in an order, so no space is skipped. It is fixed: you carry the same short list of questions into every room, so the search does not depend on what you remember to look for on a given day. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave the room, so finding a hazard and removing it are one motion. "The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like." Stripped to its bones, the method is three sentences: "Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave." What the hunt is really designed to defeat is camouflage. "The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain." You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared; you are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job, and the only way to catch them at it is to ask.

So here is the short list you carry into every room, home-scaled from the questions the method has always asked on a safety walk, deliberately small enough to hold in your head. In every room, ask: Could someone fall here? Could something here burn, or catch fire? Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? Could something here fall, cut, or crush? Could water and electricity meet? Five questions, and between them they catch the great majority of what hurts people at home. Each one is a different lens on the same space. Fall looks down and at the edges, the rug that slides, the wet patch by the tub, the top of the stairs. Burn or fire looks for heat too near things that burn and for electricity asked to do too much, the heater by the curtain, the candle under a shelf, the outlet with a nest of plugs, the lint packed in the dryer trap. Poison, choke, or strangle looks low and for the small and the looped, the cleaning sprays and medicines in reach, the button batteries and coins, the looped blind cord at a child's height. Fall, cut, or crush looks up and at what is heavy and free, the tall bookcase not anchored to the wall, the heavy box on the top shelf, the knives at the counter's edge. Water and electricity looks in the wet rooms, the hairdryer near the filled basin, the frayed cord by the sink. You do not need to memorize categories or become an inspector; you need five plain questions and the willingness to ask all five in every room, and when the answer is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for.

Then comes the rule, and the strong temptation to resist. Once you start finding things, it is tempting to do the finding now and the fixing later, to walk the whole home with a notepad and come back when you have time. Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum. "A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone." The notepad feels like progress and is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as dangerous as an un-written-down one. "The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend." Between finding a danger and removing it there is a gap, and everything bad a hazard can do, it can do in that gap, so you close the gap to nothing. This is the source's oldest safety instruction, address all possible safety issues immediately, home-scaled, and it is the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list. Carry a roll of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box, not a notepad. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was. Now and then you meet a hazard whose full fix is genuinely bigger than now, a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding, and the rule still holds with a small honest amendment: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. Reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done on the spot. "What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list."

Then the chapter walks the hunt with you, room by room, so you can see how ordinary the finds are and how quickly each one closes. The kitchen holds more of all five kinds of hazard than any other room, so it starts there: the slick spot where the dishwasher leaks, the pot handle turned out over the front edge turned in, the butane lighter from an open drawer moved up high, the knives slid back from the counter's edge. The bathroom is quick and high-value: the hairdryer unplugged and moved away from the filled basin, a grip pad for the bath mat, a nightlight in. The living room is where camouflage lives: the tall unanchored bookcase gets an anti-tip strap to the wall today, the old remote's loose battery cover gets taped shut, and the lamp cord Chapter 20 promised would take a real hunt comes out from under the rug and is routed along the wall. The stairs and hall get the curled runner tacked flat and a nightlight at the top of the stairs. The child's room gets the looped blind cord wound up onto a cleat well out of reach and the small parts and coins moved up. And the launch pad, your first small victory by the door, pays off the last of Chapter 20's teasers: the little space heater a foot from the curtain moves to a clear spot with a rule about the curtain, and the charging cords are tucked. Nothing here needed a deep clean or a rearrange; it was already sorted, arranged, and clean. It just, in a handful of small ways, could have hurt someone, and now, room by room, it cannot.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it has moved again, for the same reason as last time, only more so. The first Safety step came from removing a few real dangers on a first pass; this step comes from removing a whole home's worth, room by room, all the hidden ones a first pass could never reach. And again, notice what did not change: nothing got cleaner, nothing got tidier, no object found a better home, and the daily friction of living here is exactly what it was this morning. "What dropped, again, is the home's exposure to harm, and that is the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving." The step is a touch larger than the first, because a thorough hunt clears more than a first glance did, and it is still, in absolute terms, a measured one, and both are honest. Larger, because you searched the whole home and removed the hidden many; measured, because the earlier S's already cleared the bulk of what this meter tracks, and safety moves it in the smaller, quieter increments of things locked down rather than swept out. Chapter 20's first Safety step is the dashed ghost just behind the live needle now, Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone sits further back as a passed marker, and the goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead. It is still ahead because Safety is not finished when the hazards are gone; it is finished when the home is made to keep itself safe, which is one more chapter, and Standardize and Sustain follow that.

You carried five plain questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and removed what they found before you left each room. That is the fourth S made thorough: not a first pass on the obvious dangers, but a complete sweep that reached the hidden ones, the button battery and the blind cord and the unanchored bookcase and the heater by the curtain. What is left is not more hazards to find, at least not today; it is the question of tomorrow, because homes do not hold still. New things come in, batteries die, a bracket loosens, a child grows tall enough to reach a shelf that was safe last month. For now, you have done the thing that had to come first: you found the dangers that were already here, and you removed them. "Hold on to the five questions, because they work in any room, on any day, and a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe."
