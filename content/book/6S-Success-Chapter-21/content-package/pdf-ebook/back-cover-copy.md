# Back Cover Copy: Find and Remove Hazards

## Back-cover text

Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk through your home and handled a handful of real dangers: the smoke alarm has a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles are up high or behind a latch, the rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides, and you felt the particular relief of a worry set down. Here is the honest next thing, said not to take that relief away but to finish the job it started: the dangers you found on that first pass were the obvious ones, and the obvious hazards are the minority. Most of what can quietly hurt someone in a home is not obvious at all. It is camouflaged, sitting in plain sight, wearing the disguise of a completely ordinary object, and it survives any number of quick glances because a glance is not built to catch it. "A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group."

Chapter 21 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the second chapter of Part Six, and Safety continues, the fourth of the six S's. Chapter 20 opened Safety with the lens and one first pass on the obvious dangers; this chapter is the search that first pass was not. "A first pass is a glance across a finished home. A hunt is a walk through it with a method and a roll of tape, room by room, missing nothing on purpose." The hero is the hazard hunt, a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard before you move on. Three words wide, the method is this: "Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave." You carry five plain questions into every room, could someone fall here, could something here burn or catch fire, could something here poison choke or strangle, could something here fall cut or crush, could water and electricity meet, and what the hunt is really built to defeat is camouflage. "The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain."

Then it gives the rule, the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list: "Find it, and fix it now." The temptation is to do the finding now and the fixing later, walking the home with a notepad, and it is exactly the move that turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum, because "A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone." A worked room-by-room sweep, kitchen to launch pad, pays off the hazards Chapter 20 said would take a real hunt: the button battery in the old remote, the space heater a foot from the curtain, the lamp cord run under the rug. Then the friction meter takes a second Safety step, a little larger than the first because the whole home was hunted, and again nothing got cleaner or tidier, and yet "removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is," the friction not of the everyday but of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving. The goal is still ahead, because Safety completes only when it is made to last, and Standardize and Sustain still follow. Today, pick one room and give it a full hunt, ask the five questions, and fix everything you can in ten minutes before you walk out. One room, thoroughly safe, today. Keep the tool close with **The Hazard Hunt checklist** and walk the whole home in order with **The Room-by-Room Safety Sweep**. Next, Chapter 22 · Improve Overall Safety.
