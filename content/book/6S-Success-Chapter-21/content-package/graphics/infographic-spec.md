# Infographic Spec: Find and Remove Hazards

**Chapter 21, "Find and Remove Hazards"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Six · Safety**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: the safety
lens from last chapter, made thorough, becomes a calm room-by-room hunt, and the whole hunt fits on one
card as five plain questions you carry into every room. The concept pairs the hero tool, **the hazard
hunt**, with its frozen rule, **Find it, and fix it now.**, and shows the picture at its heart: the
five frozen safety questions, each a different lens, and beside each the family of hidden hazard it
surfaces. Then it shows how you act on what you find: fix it the moment you find it, because a hazard
found but not fixed is only scheduled. So the viewer sees the whole move at once, the five questions,
what each reveals, and the discipline that closes each danger on the spot.

It stays consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_21_final.html`. Slate carries the
five questions, the definition, the headings, and the tags; a single small warm red is held back for
the hazard marks and is used sparingly on this calm image; a soft green marks the handled and fixed
dots. "Tidy" is not the point; a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold a hidden
hazard in every room, doing a quiet second job, so the target here is a home that is also thoroughly
safe. Anti-shame throughout: a home holds hidden hazards because ordinary, useful, tidy things quietly
do a dangerous second job and have become furniture to the eye, not because anyone was negligent, and
the fix is a method run on purpose, not vigilance or blame. Safety is love, not fear, so the register is
calm and reassuring, never an alarm.

  "The obvious dangers were the few. / The hidden ones take a hunt."

**2. The rule (the anchor, y approx 240 to 460)**
- The frozen rule set large in Fraunces ink as the top anchor line: "Find it, and fix it now."
- Beneath it, the frozen definition set smaller in a slate bordered strip: "A hazard hunt is a calm,
  systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety
  questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass
  catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden."
- Keep the rule the loudest line on the page: finding and fixing are one motion, not two.

**3. The five-questions card (the hero, y approx 540 to 1090)**
- The five frozen questions drawn as a calm, hand-made card, one row each, each question set in a bold
  slate heading, and beside each two or three small sketched icons or short labels of the hidden hazards
  it surfaces:
  - `Could someone fall here?` : a sliding rug, a cluttered stair, a wet floor by the tub.
  - `Could something here burn, or catch fire?` : a heater by a curtain, an overloaded outlet, a candle
    under a shelf, dryer lint.
  - `Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?` : chemicals and medicine in reach, a button
    battery and small parts, a looped blind cord.
  - `Could something here fall, cut, or crush?` : an unanchored tall bookcase, a heavy box up high,
    knives at the counter edge.
  - `Could water and electricity meet?` : a hairdryer by the basin, a frayed cord near the sink.
- Set the five questions exactly as they read in the chapter, each ending in its question mark; they
  are the frozen set and must not be trimmed or reworded.
- Beneath the card, a small row for the discipline as the way you act: the frozen found-not-fixed line
  set small in a slate strip, "A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled.",
  with a tiny glyph of a red hazard-mark turning into a green fixed-dot. The move that keeps a hunt from
  becoming a museum.

**4. Footer line (y approx 1270)**
- Newsreader italic, slate: "The same five questions, carried into every room. Ask all five everywhere,
  and the hidden dangers stop being able to hide."
- Tiny attribution, soft gray sans: `6S Success: Home Edition · Chapter 21`

## Layout notes

- The rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the whole chapter compressed to five words:
  find it, and fix it now. The eye should read the rule, then see the five-questions card make it
  usable, the same short list carried into every room until the hidden dangers stop being able to hide.
- The five-questions card is the hero, so make it clear and portable: five rows, each with its frozen
  question and the ordinary hidden hazards it surfaces, calm and hand-made rather than clinical. Set the
  five questions byte-for-byte as they read in the chapter, each ending in its question mark; they are
  the frozen set.
- Hold the color discipline: the single small warm red is reserved for the small hazard-marks and is
  used sparingly; slate carries the five questions, the definition, the headings, and the tags; a soft
  green marks the handled-dots and fixed-dots. This image is calm, so no fill-line red anywhere except
  those small marks.
- This image makes the tool, the hazard hunt and its rule and its five questions, and the discipline
  you act with, find it and fix it now. Do NOT re-teach Sort, Straighten, or Shine, and do NOT re-teach
  the safety lens or the triage from Chapter 20; the home is already clean and kept and given its first
  pass, and this chapter runs the thorough hunt of it. Do NOT build lasting or standardized safety
  (Chapter 22 · Improve Overall Safety, the next chapter; handoff only), and do NOT solve long-term
  adherence or audits (Standardize and Sustain, later S's); this is the thorough hunt for hidden hazards.
  And name no brand and draw no specific product; the remote, battery, cord, heater, bookcase, and blind
  cord are plain and generic, teaching the habit, not a shopping list.
- Anti-shame: a home holds hidden hazards because ordinary, useful, tidy things quietly do a dangerous
  second job and have become furniture to the eye, not because anyone was negligent, and the fix is a
  method run on purpose, not vigilance or blame. What you are building is not fear, it is a habit of
  asking. Keep the register calm and reassuring, safety is love, not fear; no alarm cues, no injury
  imagery, no scare framing, no force framing anywhere. A clean home is a fine thing to have made; this
  image adds the hunt that also makes it thoroughly safe.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the card and the icons
  drawn by hand rather than as a clinical chart, the glyphs sketched rather than typeset, the whole image
  the calm, warm register of a good idea rather than a warning poster.
