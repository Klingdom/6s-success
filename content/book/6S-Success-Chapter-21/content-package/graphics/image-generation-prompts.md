# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 21

**"Find and Remove Hazards"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Six · Safety*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to
hold across all five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`,
terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B`, soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single warm
red `#CB4B36` reserved for the small hazard marks only. Natural soft daylight, shallow but not showy
depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the finished home the reader has cleared, arranged, and cleaned across
Chapters 12 to 19, then given a first safety pass in Chapter 20: the settled launch pad by the door,
the whole home kept clean and read on a short shared rhythm, the worst and most obvious dangers already
handled. This chapter, the SECOND of Part Six, CONTINUES Safety, the fourth S, and does one turn: it
takes the safety lens Chapter 20 opened and makes it thorough, moving the book from a first glance at
the obvious dangers to a calm, room-by-room hunt that finds the hidden many and removes each on the
spot. It teaches the thoroughness reframe (a first pass finds the obvious, a hunt finds the rest, and
the rest are the majority), the hazard hunt (systematic, a fixed set of five questions carried into
every room, ending in action), the five frozen safety questions and the family of hidden hazard each
one surfaces, the rule find it and fix it now (the found-not-fixed reframe: a hazard found but not fixed
is scheduled, not handled), the camouflage reframe (the dangerous thing is the ordinary object doing a
quiet second job), a worked whole-home sweep that pays off Chapter 20's teasers, and the second Safety
step on the meter. Nothing of Sort, Straighten, or Shine is re-taught here, and neither is the safety
lens or the triage from Chapter 20; the home is already clean and kept and given its first pass, and
this chapter runs the thorough hunt of it. No lasting or standardized safety system is built here
(Chapter 22 · Improve Overall Safety, teaser and handoff only, which completes the fourth S), and no
long-term adherence or audits are solved (Standardize and Sustain, later S's). Keep the tone calm, warm,
and reassuring: a home holds hidden hazards because ordinary, useful, tidy things quietly do a dangerous
second job and have become furniture to the eye, not because anyone was negligent; the fix is a method
run on purpose, not vigilance or blame. Safety is love, not fear, so the register is relief and the ease
of a home made thorough, never lecture, never guilt, never a scare tone. Crucially, describe only the
calm, warm illustrated house style: no photoreal harm, no injury, no accident, no blood, no gore, no
distress; the hazards are patient and unhappening, marked with small quiet symbols, and any child in
frame is calm and safe. No force or violence framing anywhere, no war on hazards, no blitz, no purge. No
alarmist injury statistics. No hype words. Avoid brand names and specific product endorsements; the
remotes, batteries, cords, heaters, bookcases, and blind cords are plain and generic, teaching the
habit, not a shopping list.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical
object in the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real sheet, or the reading on a real
alarm indicator, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental hand-lettered
label: use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 21 · SAFETY`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot
between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: room-by-room, first-pass, hazard-hunt,
hazard-mark, fixed-dot, found-not-fixed, fix-it-now, button-battery, blind-cord, anti-tip, whole-home,
child-height, line-and-flat-color, second-job, Shine-complete, Straighten-complete, Sort-complete,
launch-pad.

## 1. The whole home, hunted (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of a finished, cared-for home drawn as a gentle cutaway with
several rooms visible at once, the kitchen and the living room and the stairs and a bathroom and a
child's room and the entry, all clean and arranged and lovely, in soft daylight. A few things carry
small soft green handled-dots, the marks of last chapter's first pass: a smoke alarm with a lit
indicator, a rug taped down at the top of the stairs, a latched low cupboard under the sink. Scattered
through every room, quiet and easy to miss, are many more small soft warm-red hazard-marks, and they
sit on perfectly ordinary objects: a remote control on the sofa arm holding a small round battery, a
looped blind cord by the window, a tall bookcase standing free of the wall, a small heater near a
curtain, an outlet with too many plugs crowded into it, a lamp cord running under the edge of a rug, a
pot handle turned out over the front of the stove. The green dots are few and the red marks are many.
Nothing here is a mess, nothing is dirty or misplaced; you simply see, through the small marks, that the
obvious dangers were handled and the hidden ones, one or two in every room, were not. Warm paper cream
and honey wood tones, ink-brown shadows, soft green for the few handled-dots, one soft warm-red accent
held only for the many small quiet hazard-marks. Editorial cookbook-style illustration. A finished home,
glanced at once, now hunted.

- Mood: the obvious dangers were handled last chapter, and they are the few green dots; the many red
  marks are the hidden hazards, camouflaged as ordinary objects, that a single pass never reached; there
  are far more of them than of the obvious kind, and finding them takes more than a glance, it takes a
  hunt; the hidden many, not the obvious few, are where most of what can quietly hurt a person lives.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, floating typeset text overlays, any messy or dirty
  home, any injury, accident, blood, gore, or a distressed or endangered child, a scared or scolded
  person, force or violence cues, an alarm or warning-poster tone, a warm-red accent sprayed anywhere but
  the small hazard-marks, more green dots than red marks, brand names or recognizable product labels;
  keep the home genuinely finished and lovely, the few green handled-dots clearly outnumbered by the many
  quiet red hazard-marks, the whole image a calm, thorough second look rather than a warning.

## 2. The five questions (the portable tool, one card)

A warm editorial illustration of a calm, hand-made tool card titled the five questions, drawn by hand
rather than as a clinical checklist, showing the five safety questions you carry into every room. Five
rows down the card, each with a frozen safety question set in a bold slate hand-lettered heading and,
beside it, two or three small sketched icons of the hidden hazards it surfaces. Row one, could someone
fall here: a sliding rug, a cluttered stair tread, a wet floor by the tub. Row two, could something here
burn or catch fire: a small heater by a curtain, an outlet with too many plugs, a candle under a shelf,
dryer lint. Row three, could something here poison choke or strangle: cleaning sprays and medicine
within a child's reach, a small round battery and small parts, a looped blind cord at child-height. Row
four, could something here fall cut or crush: a tall unanchored bookcase, a heavy box on a high shelf,
knives at the counter's edge. Row five, could water and electricity meet: a hairdryer by a filled basin,
a frayed cord near the sink. At the foot of the card, hand-lettered in terracotta, the rule: find it,
and fix it now. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, slate for the five hand-lettered
headings, soft warm-red only for the smallest hazard icons, one warm terracotta for the rule at the
foot. A picture of one plain, portable idea: the same five questions, asked in every room.

- Mood: five plain questions, small enough to hold in your head, and between them they catch the great
  majority of what hurts people at home; each one is a different lens on the same space and each finds a
  family of hidden hazard the others miss; ask all five in every room and the hidden dangers stop being
  able to hide; the tool is the whole hunt on one page.
- Avoid: a busy clinical checklist, cold clinical light, floating typeset text overlays, an alarmist or
  statistical tone, any injury imagery or graphic harm, warm-red sprayed beyond the small hazard icons,
  more than five rows or extra categories, brand names or recognizable products; keep it a calm,
  hand-made card, the five questions clear and the hazard icons ordinary and small, the register a
  usable tool rather than a warning poster.

## 3. A room-by-room hunt (the walk, found turning to fixed)

A warm editorial illustration of a calm, hand-drawn floor-plan of a home, or an ordered vertical route
of rooms, walked in sequence, kitchen then bathroom then living room then stairs and hall then child's
room then launch pad, drawn by hand rather than as a technical plan. In each room, one or two very
ordinary hazards are shown found and then removed, each drawn as a small soft warm-red hazard-mark with
a gentle arrow to a small soft green fixed-dot: kitchen, a pot handle turned in and a lighter moved up
high; bathroom, a hairdryer moved away from the filled basin and a grip mat laid down; living room, a
tall bookcase strapped to the wall, a remote's battery cover taped shut, a lamp cord brought out from
under the rug; stairs and hall, a hall runner tacked flat and a nightlight put in; child's room, a
looped blind cord wound up onto a cleat out of reach and small parts moved high; launch pad, a small
heater moved clear of the curtain and charging cords tucked away. A small hand-written count of fixed
grows as the route goes on. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, slate for the room labels
and the route, soft warm-red for the pre-fix hazard-marks, soft green for the fixed-dots and the growing
count. A picture of one plain idea: walk every room, ask, and fix each hazard on the spot before moving
on.

- Mood: a complete hunt as it actually runs, a calm route through the whole home, a small number of very
  ordinary hazards found in each room, each one fixed on the spot before moving to the next; none of
  these are messes, they are ordinary objects doing a quiet second job; the hunt is just the discipline
  of visiting every room and asking, and the fix, nearly always, is smaller than the worry was; concrete,
  doable, unfrightening.
- Avoid: a cold technical floor-plan, gridlines and dimensions everywhere, floating typeset text, any
  injury or accident imagery, any mess or dirt, a hazard-mark left standing with no fix, warm-red on
  anything but the pre-fix hazard-marks, an alarmist tone, brand names or recognizable products; keep the
  route calm and ordered and the finds ordinary and small, every red hazard-mark clearly turning into a
  green fixed-dot, the register a calm, complete walk rather than a crisis.

## 4. Found is not fixed (the notepad and the box)

A calm, warm illustration of the chapter's hardest discipline, drawn as two gentle facing panels of the
same found hazard, read left to right as the two ways you can respond, in soft daylight. The first
panel, the notepad: a hand writes a hazard onto a list, and the ordinary object itself, carrying its
small soft warm-red hazard-mark, sits exactly where it was, unchanged and still live, the register that
of progress that is not, a written-down danger only scheduled. The second panel, the box: the same hand
fixes the hazard on the spot, the warm-red hazard-mark turning into a small soft green fixed-dot, a roll
of grip tape, a few cabinet latches, and an empty box beside it instead of a notepad, the danger simply
gone. Between or beneath the two, a quiet hand-written line carries the honest amendment: for the rare
hazard whose full fix is bigger than now, make it safe now and schedule only the rest, move the toys off
the unanchored bookcase today and buy the bracket this week. To the right, a quieter, dimmer, unlit panel
outline stands for the next chapter, where Safety is made to last, with two fainter outlines behind it
for the chapters still to come, all kept low-contrast and clearly ahead. Warm paper cream background,
ink-brown line work, soft warm-red for the still-live hazard on the notepad side, soft green for the
fixed hazard on the box side, the right group dim and unlit. The image reads as one plain idea: a hazard
found but not fixed is only scheduled, and the only safe place for a danger is gone.

- Mood: the notepad feels like progress and it is not, because a written-down hazard is exactly as
  dangerous as an un-written-down one; between finding a danger and removing it there is a gap, and
  everything bad a hazard can do it can do in that gap, so you close the gap to nothing and fix each
  hazard the moment you find it; carry a roll of tape and a box, not a notepad, and let finding and
  fixing be one motion; a danger is only handled once it is gone.
- Avoid: a guilt-heavy or scolding tone on the notepad panel, a fearful or minefield register, any
  injury or accident, drawing the box panel as no lighter than the notepad panel, warm-red left on the
  resolved box side, teaching any next-chapter content in the dim right panel, floating typeset text,
  hype; keep the notepad panel honest and even a little tempting and the box panel visibly eased, the
  register calm resolve rather than alarm, a danger simply handled and gone.

## 5. The needle takes its second Safety step (the friction meter)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge dialing
from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right. This chapter the
dark live needle takes its second Safety step, a small step further off Chapter 19's clearly marked
Shine-complete milestone toward the calm side, a touch larger than the previous chapter's step because
the hunt removed a whole home's worth of hidden hazards room by room rather than the obvious few, and
still, in absolute terms, a measured one. Just behind the live needle, where it rested last chapter,
sits a faint dashed ghost marking Chapter 20's first-Safety-step position, so the eye reads the small
ground this step covered; Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone sits further back as a small passed
marker, with the earlier Shine steps of Chapters 16, 17, and 18 fainter ghosts behind that. Chapter 15's
Straighten-complete milestone is a small slate marker further back, and a slate marker for the earlier
Sort-complete milestone sits further back still. A soft terracotta marker labels the reached position as
the second Safety step, a step off the Shine-complete milestone rather than a milestone of its own. A
slate crosshair labeled the goal, carrying its purpose flag, still sits further out on the green calm
side, clearly ahead of the live needle, with visible room left between them, because Safety completes
only in the next chapter and Standardize and Sustain remain. Warm paper cream background, ink line work,
consistent stroke weight, friendly but not childish.

- Mood: as last chapter the needle moves not because daily friction dropped but because the chance of
  harm did, a whole home's worth of hidden dangers simply removed; what dropped is the home's exposure to
  harm, the deepest kind of friction there is, not the friction you feel every day but the friction of
  the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving; the step is a touch larger than the first
  because the hunt cleared more, and it is still measured, and the goal is still ahead with clear room
  left, because Safety completes only when it is made to last and Standardize and Sustain still follow; a
  real step and a long road still ahead, both true at once.
- Avoid: the live needle reaching or passing the goal crosshair, no room left between the needle and the
  goal, the step drawn as arriving or as a milestone of its own rather than a small step off Shine
  complete, the step drawn as the finish of the whole book, a completion glow or celebratory confetti,
  the dashed ghosts drawn solid or bright, hype, neon color, any sense of the whole journey being
  finished. The needle takes a small honest step, a touch larger than the first and still measured, and
  clear room remains between it and the goal.
