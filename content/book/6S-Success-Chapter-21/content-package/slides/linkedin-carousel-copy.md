### Slide 1 (hook)

**Your home is cleared, arranged, spotless, and safe, or so a glance says. There are hazards hiding in one or two rooms of it right now.**

Not the messy kind. Last chapter you took the safety lens for a first walk and handled the obvious dangers: the smoke alarm has a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles are up high, the rug at the top of the stairs no longer slides. Those were the few.

The many are still here, camouflaged as perfectly ordinary things. "A first pass is a glance across a finished home. A hunt is a walk through it with a method and a roll of tape, room by room, missing nothing on purpose." Swipe for the hunt.

### Slide 2

**A first pass finds the obvious. A hunt finds the rest, and the rest are the majority.**

The frozen reframe the whole chapter rests on:

"A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group."

The obvious hazards light up the instant you finally ask the safety question, because they are the ones we already half-know about. They are the few. The hidden ones survive any number of quick glances, because a glance was never built to catch them.

### Slide 3

**Familiarity is why the hidden hazards hide.**

"The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing that is not attached to anything."

The things we live with become invisible to us, and the dangerous ones become invisible right along with the harmless ones. The cord that has run under the rug for three years will not announce itself now. This is not a failure of care; it is just how familiarity works.

So you stop relying on what catches your eye. "You go looking for it, deliberately, in every room, asking the same questions you would ask in a room you had never seen before."

### Slide 4

**The hero: the hazard hunt.**

The frozen definition:

"A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden."

Three things make it a hunt, not another glance. It is systematic: room by room, in an order, so nothing is skipped. It is fixed: the same short list carried into every room. And it ends in action: you fix what you find before you leave.

"The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like."

### Slide 5

**The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one.**

"You are not hunting for junk or mess, which the earlier S's already cleared. You are hunting for ordinary, tidy, useful things that are quietly doing a dangerous second job, and the only way to catch them at it is to ask."

The frozen camouflage reframe:

"The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain."

### Slide 6

**Five plain questions, carried into every room.**

Home-scaled from the questions a safety walk has always asked, small enough to hold in your head. In every room, ask:

1. "Could someone fall here?"
2. "Could something here burn, or catch fire?"
3. "Could something here poison, choke, or strangle?"
4. "Could something here fall, cut, or crush?"
5. "Could water and electricity meet?"

Each one is a different lens on the same space. You do not need to become an inspector, only to ask all five in every room, and when the answer is yes, to do the one thing the answer asks for.

### Slide 7

**Find it, and fix it now.**

The rule, frozen, and the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list:

"Find it, and fix it now."

The method, frozen: "Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave." This is the source's oldest safety instruction, address all possible safety issues immediately, home-scaled. Most of what you find will yield, because most home hazards are cheap and quick to remove, and the fix is usually smaller than the worry was.

### Slide 8

**A hazard found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled.**

The temptation is to find now and fix later, walking the whole home with a notepad. "Resist it, because that is the one move that quietly turns a hazard hunt back into a hazard museum."

The frozen found-not-fixed reframe:

"A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone."

"The child does not know the button battery is on your list. The fire does not check whether you meant to move the heater this weekend."

### Slide 10

**Make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now.**

Now and then a full fix is genuinely bigger than "now," a bookcase that needs a bracket you do not have, a step that needs rebuilding. The rule still holds, with a small amendment: reduce the danger to near nothing immediately, and schedule only the part that truly cannot be done on the spot.

"What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed nothing but your to-do list."

Family Challenge: "Turn the hunt into a game. Give each person a room and the five questions and send everyone off to find hazards at once, and the younger the sharper for the low ones. Then come back together and fix the worst finds as a team. A home searched by five pairs of eyes misses far less than one."

### Slide 12 (soft CTA)

**Safety, made thorough. Next, we make it last.**

You carried five plain questions through the whole home, room by room, and removed what they found before you left each room. "Hold on to the five questions, because they work in any room, on any day, and a home you can hunt is a home you can keep safe."

One Idea to Keep: "Go room by room, and in each one ask the same few questions: could someone fall, could something burn, could something poison or choke or strangle, could something fall or cut or crush, could water and electricity meet. Then fix what you find before you leave the room, because a danger is only handled once it is gone."
