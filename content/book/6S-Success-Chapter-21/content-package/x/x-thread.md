3/
Familiarity is why the hidden ones hide. The things we live with go invisible, and the dangerous ones go invisible right along with the harmless. The bookcase that has never once fallen reads, to a glance, as furniture, not as a tall heavy thing attached to nothing.

(269 chars)

4/
A glance works by noticing what stands out, and the hazards that stand out are a small, particular set. The only reliable way to find the rest is to stop trusting what catches your eye and run a method instead. You go looking, deliberately, in every room.

(258 chars)

5/
The hero is the hazard hunt: a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. A first pass catches the obvious; the hunt catches the hidden.

(277 chars)

---

6/
Three things make it a hunt, not another glance: it is systematic, room by room; the questions are fixed; and it ends in action, fixed before you leave. "The method is almost aggressively boring, and that is the point. Boring is what thorough looks like."

(258 chars)

---

7/
In every room, ask five questions. Could someone fall here? Could something here burn, or catch fire? Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? Could something here fall, cut, or crush? Could water and electricity meet? Between them they catch most of what hurts people.

(280 chars)

---

8/
The method, frozen:

"Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave."

(114 chars)

---

9/
What the hunt defeats is camouflage. "The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach."

(258 chars)

---

10/
The rule is the source's oldest safety instruction, home-scaled:

"Find it, and fix it now."

It is the whole reason the hunt ends in action rather than in a list.

(167 chars)

---

11/
"A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone."

(237 chars)

---

12/
The common trap is the notepad: walking the home listing hazards to deal with later, and calling that safety. The list is not the fix. Every danger you write down and leave in place is still live. Carry a roll of tape and a box instead, and fix as you find.

(261 chars)

---

13/
Now and then a full fix is bigger than now, a bracket you do not have, a step to rebuild. The rule still holds, amended: make it safe now, even if you cannot make it finished now. What you never do is walk away from a live hazard having changed only your to-do list.
