4/
In every room, ask five questions. Could someone fall here? Could something here burn, or catch fire? Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? Could something here fall, cut, or crush? Could water and electricity meet? Carry them and a home stays safe.

(263 chars)

---

5/
"A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone."

(236 chars)

6/
The whole method fits in three lines. "Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave." Boring is what thorough looks like. Read the free chapter.

(190 chars)

7/
"Find it, and fix it now."

That is the rule, and it is why the hunt ends in action rather than in a list. You go looking, deliberately, in every room, and you remove each danger the moment you find it.

(205 chars)

---

8/
The common trap is the notepad: listing hazards to deal with later and calling that safety. The list is not the fix. Every danger you write down and leave in place is still live. Carry a roll of tape and a box instead, and fix as you find.

(242 chars)

---

9/
None of this is fear. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so you can stop carrying them as worry. Every hazard removed is a worry you set down and never pick up again. A safe home is not a fearful one. It is where you have already done the worrying, with your hands.

(280 chars)
