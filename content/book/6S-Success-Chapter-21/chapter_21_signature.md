- **The rule (frozen):** Find it, and fix it now.
- **The method (frozen):** Go room by room. Ask the same few questions in each. Fix what you find before you leave.
- **The five safety questions (frozen set):** 1. Could someone fall here? 2. Could something here burn, or catch fire? 3. Could something here poison, choke, or strangle? 4. Could something here fall, cut, or crush? 5. Could water and electricity meet?
- **Definition (frozen, defbox):** A hazard hunt is a calm, systematic walk through the whole home, one room at a time, asking the same short set of safety questions in every space and removing each hazard you find before you move on. Where a first pass catches what is obvious, the hunt is built to catch what is hidden.
- **The thoroughness reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** A first pass finds the dangers that are obvious the moment you look. A hunt finds the ones that never will be. Most of what can quietly hurt a person at home lives in the second group.
- **The found-not-fixed reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** A hazard you have found but not fixed is not handled. It is scheduled. And a hazard on a schedule is still a hazard, still able to hurt someone on any ordinary day before its turn comes up. The only safe place for a danger is gone.
- **The camouflage reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** The dangerous thing is rarely the ugly one. It is the ordinary object doing a second, quiet job you never gave it: the lamp cord that is also a tripwire, the old remote that is also a battery a small child can reach, the space heater that is also a flame a foot from the curtain.
- **Outcome vocabulary (frozen):** the hazard hunt / the five questions / find it, and fix it now / room by room. Chapter 20 gave the lens and a first pass; Chapter 21 makes the search complete and removes what it finds.

## Motifs
- **A glance versus a search.** The first pass was a glance; the hunt is a search. The obvious dangers are the minority.
- **Camouflage.** The dangerous object is usually an ordinary one doing a second, unassigned job.
- **The same questions, every room.** A fixed list carried everywhere is what makes the search complete.
- **Find it, fix it now.** A found-but-unfixed hazard is only scheduled; the only safe place for a danger is gone.
- **Low and high.** The two bands adults miss: a child's height, and above eye level.
- **Another step, still measured.** More hazards gone, so the needle moves again; still small, because Safety removes the friction of the worst day.

## Emotional purpose
Calm, competent reassurance, the feeling of a careful walk through your own home with a clear method and a roll of tape in hand, not a fearful inventory. The register is steady: name hidden dangers plainly, refuse to dramatize them, and frame the whole hunt as the thing that lets a household stop worrying because the dangers are gone rather than merely known. No shame (unnoticed hazards are the normal case), no fear-mongering, no graphic injury, no alarmist statistics, no force/violence framing of the "hunt".

## Practical purpose
Teach the reader to run a systematic room-by-room hazard hunt: carry the five questions into every room, find the hidden hazards a first pass misses, and remove each one on the spot. Do NOT re-teach the lens, the triage, the pyramid, or the 5S+1 history (Chapter 20). Do NOT build ongoing safety systems, emergency preparedness, or the home versions of safety habit/training/review (Chapter 22). Do NOT write documented standards (Standardize) or long-term adherence systems (Sustain). Do NOT re-open Sort/Straighten/Shine. Avoid brand names.
