# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

You have the keeping mindset and the friendly check-in now. Chapter 26 gave the mindset, that a home is kept and not finished, held good over the years by small, shared habits. Chapter 27 gave the first concrete practice, a light, regular check-in that catches drift while it is still small. What neither gave you was what to do about the findings that will not go away: not the one-off slip, corrected in two minutes and forgotten, but the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, the same shoes in the same doorway, week after week. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build. Chapter 28 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the reframe that dissolves it, and the reframe is the whole of the chapter: "A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong."

"Family Problem Solving" is the third chapter of Part Eight, and it gives the household a shared way to solve the recurring problems the friendly audit keeps turning up. Its whole work is a reframe. It uses the audit's findings from Chapter 27 without re-teaching the audit, and it uses the keeping mindset from Chapter 26 without re-teaching it. It keeps the problem-solving occasional, something you run when a recurring problem surfaces, not the daily rhythm, which is Chapter 29, and not the recurring event that completes the book, which is Chapter 30. What it hands you is the ability, as a household, to take one of those recurring problems, understand why it really keeps happening, and change the little system so that it stops, for good, together, without anyone being blamed.

The chapter starts with the distinction the whole thing turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns. Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. As the chapter puts it, "A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again." Fixing is correcting the symptom, and it is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. "Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak." Mopping is right for a spill, but if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer, and the recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are exactly that same square of floor.

That is the hero, and it fits in a box, a definition that describes settling a problem at its cause rather than patching it forever: "Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever." Two things make solving work, more than any technique, and the chapter gives each its own reframe.

The first is the heart of the method, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. "When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place." This carries the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, and it matters even more here. Ask who keeps dumping their coat and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask why the coats keep landing there and you are suddenly looking at the real thing: the chair is by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. Nobody is failing; the system is asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk. You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would, and you will notice something reassuring along the way: "the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw." A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. "That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system."

The second thing that makes solving work is who came up with the fix, because whether a solution survives depends not on how clever it is but on who designed it. "The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps." It is doubly true for two reasons, knowledge and ownership: the person who lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, do not, so leave them out and you design a worse solution; and any fix needs the people around it to keep using it, because "people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above." So you bring the problem to the household as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix.

Then the chapter runs the whole method once on the coats, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description, and the method is just four small steps done together: "Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it." Instead of hanging the coats up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes: they name the problem plainly, ask why together until they reach the cause everyone recognizes, change the small system so the shortest path and the right path are the same path with a row of hooks by the door, and, because a child picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, so they keep it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, a little further along the path it revealed two chapters ago. Last chapter the audit took the needle its first Sustain step by helping the home catch drift while it was small; this chapter goes a level deeper, stopping the drift from returning at all, by solving the system behind it. As the caption has it, "The needle takes its second Sustain step, a little further along the path to the goal. A home that solves its recurring problems, instead of patching them forever, has quietly removed a source of friction that used to return every week, which is exactly the kind of gain that makes a home more able to keep itself. The gap is shorter again, and the last of it is what the final chapters close." It is honestly small, because you solved one recurring problem and not all of them at once; but it is real, because solving a recurring problem changes something permanent, and a source of friction that used to come back every single week is simply gone now.
