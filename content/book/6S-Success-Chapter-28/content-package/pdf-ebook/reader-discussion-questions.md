# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Family Problem Solving

*Chapter 28, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter turns on a single reframe: "A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong." Which recurring problem in your home have you been treating as a discipline failure, and how does it feel to see it instead as a small system that is simply set up wrong?

2. The chapter separates two things we constantly confuse: "A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again." What is one thing you have fixed more than twice, and what would solving it, rather than fixing it again, actually look like?

3. The chapter gives a plain image for the difference: "Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak." Where in your home are you mopping the same square of floor every single day, and what might the leak behind it be?

4. The definition box names the practice: "Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again." What would it take, in your household, to move from patching the symptom forever to changing the small system once?

5. The heart of the method is one question in place of another: "When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything." Where do you catch yourself asking who keeps doing this, and what changes when you ask why it keeps happening instead?

6. The chapter insists the fault is in the setup, not the person: "The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place." Think of a recurring annoyance you have quietly blamed on a person. Where might the hooks be in the wrong place?

7. You reach the cause by asking why a few times, the way a curious child would: why the coats are on the chair, why that is the easiest place, why the hooks being far away matters so much. Take one recurring problem and ask why three or four times out loud. Where did you land, and was it a person or a system?

8. The chapter offers a reassuring find: "The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw." Why is that genuinely good news, and how does it change the mood of the conversation to look for the missing hook or awkward distance before you look at the people?

9. The chapter says whether a fix survives depends on who made it: "A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps." Where have you seen a good solution quietly dropped because it was imposed rather than chosen, and what might have kept it alive?

10. The chapter gives two reasons to solve together, knowledge and ownership: the person who lives with the problem knows why it happens, and "people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above." Who in your home actually lives with a given problem, and what do they know about it that you, solving it from the outside, do not?

11. The four-step method is short: "name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it." Which of the four steps do you think your household would skip, and what would it cost you to skip it?

12. In the worked example, a child picks where the hooks go and hammers one in, so the fix is theirs and they keep it. Who in your home is often left out of solving the problems they live with, and what might change if they got to help design and build the fix?

13. The Quick Win is the smallest possible proof: "Take the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile that always returns to the same spot, and ask out loud, 'why does this keep happening here?', three or four times, until you reach something you can actually change." Which pile would you pick, and could you ask the first why before this discussion ends?

14. The Common Mistake names the exact way it goes wrong: "Solving the problem for the family instead of with them, by imposing a clever fix nobody asked for ... it will be resented and abandoned within a fortnight, because people keep what they helped build and drop what was dropped on them." Where have you imposed a clever fix that was quietly abandoned, and how might solving it together have gone differently?

15. The friction meter takes its second Sustain step: "A home that solves its recurring problems, instead of patching them forever, has quietly removed a source of friction that used to return every week." With five of the six S's done and keeping still growing, how does it feel that solving one recurring problem moves the needle only a little, but permanently?

16. The One Idea to Keep closes the chapter: "when something keeps going wrong, do not look for someone to blame. Look, together, for the small system to fix." What would it take for your household to meet every recurring problem this way, as a small system to fix together rather than a person to blame?
