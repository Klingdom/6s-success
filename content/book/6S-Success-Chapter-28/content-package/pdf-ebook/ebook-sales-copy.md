## You have the check-in. Now here is how to solve what it keeps turning up.

Chapter 26 gave you the keeping mindset, that a home is kept and not finished. Chapter 27 gave you the first concrete practice, a light, regular check-in that catches drift while it is still small. But that check-in keeps turning up a more stubborn kind of finding: not the one-off slip, corrected in two minutes and forgotten, but the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, the same shoes in the same doorway, week after week. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build.

Here is the reframe that dissolves that frustration, and it is the whole of this chapter.

> A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong.

## Welcome to solving, not fixing.

Chapter 28 is the third chapter of Part Eight, and it gives the household a shared way to solve the recurring problems the audit keeps turning up. It uses the audit's findings from Chapter 27 without re-teaching the audit, and the keeping mindset from Chapter 26 without re-teaching it. What it hands you is the ability, as a household, to take one of those recurring problems, understand why it really keeps happening, and change the little system so that it stops, for good.

> Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

## Fixing is not solving, and confusing them is what exhausts you.

Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing corrects the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. It is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there, which is still fully in place and will do it again tonight. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced.

> A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. Mopping is right for a spill, but if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are exactly that same square of floor.

## Ask why, not who.

The tool for finding the cause is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. Ask who keeps dumping their coat and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask why the coats keep landing there and you are suddenly looking at the real thing.

> When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.

This carries the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, and it matters even more here. The chair is by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person coming home does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. Nobody is failing. The system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk, and it loses that argument every time.

## The real cause is a design flaw, not a character flaw.

You get from the symptom to the cause by gently asking why a few times over, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom you were blaming a person for to a cause you can actually change.

> The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw.

A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

## Solve it together, or do not bother.

There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is. It is who came up with it. It is doubly true for two reasons, knowledge and ownership: the person who lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, do not, and any fix needs the people around it to keep using it.

> The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.

People keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.

## The whole method, run once on the coats.

Seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description, and the method is just four small steps, done together.

> Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.

The check-in keeps flagging the same thing: coats on the chair by the door, every week. So instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes. First, they name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Second, they ask why, together, a few times, and arrive not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes the moment it is said: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. Third, they change the small system to remove that cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And fourth, because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.

## Do not solve it for the family instead of with them.

One caution, so the solving stays a shared thing and never becomes a fix handed down.

> Solving the problem for the family instead of with them, by imposing a clever fix nobody asked for. However good it is, it will be resented and abandoned within a fortnight, because people keep what they helped build and drop what was dropped on them. Solve it together, or do not bother.

The whole point is that the fix is owned because it was worked out together, and nobody is ever the problem. Keep it about the system, ask why and never who, and solve it as a household, and the fix stays alive because the people around it chose it.
