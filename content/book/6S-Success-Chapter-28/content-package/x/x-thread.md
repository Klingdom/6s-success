# X Thread: Stop Fixing It, Start Solving It

Chapter 28 of *6S Success: Home Edition*. One thread, 14 posts. Each post is at or under 280 characters, counting its number line, and stands on its own. Plain "1/" numbering on its own line.

---

1/
Chapter 27 gave the friendly check-in that catches drift while it is small. But it keeps turning up a more stubborn finding: the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, week after week. You fix them, they come back. Chapter 28 is how to end that.

(268 chars)

---

2/
Here is the reframe, and it is the whole chapter. "A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong." The coats return not because anyone lacks discipline, but because the setup makes the chair easiest.

(277 chars)

---

3/
"And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once."

(189 chars)

---

4/
"A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again."

(250 chars)

---

5/
"Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak." Both have their place. But if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows more mopping is not the answer. That square is what your check-in keeps finding.

(273 chars)

---

6/
"Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together... you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again."

(256 chars)

---

7/
The whole method hinges on the right question. "When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything."

(274 chars)

---

8/
"The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place." Ask who, and you get a defensive family member and a coat back tomorrow. Ask why, and the hooks are down the hall. Nobody is failing.

(258 chars)

---

9/
You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why on the chair? It is easiest. Why easiest? The hooks are far. Why does that matter? Tired people take the shortest path. A few whys in, you reach a cause you can actually change.

(279 chars)

---

10/
And the news is good. "The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw." A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin too far, a thing with no home. "You cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system."

(250 chars)

---

11/
One thing decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever it is. "A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps."

(211 chars)

---

12/
"Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it."

(278 chars)

---

13/
The rule to keep: "When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again." Because "you can nearly always change a system."

(247 chars)

---

14/
The needle takes its second Sustain step, a short way further along the dashed last-gap path. Small, honestly, because you solved one problem and not all of them; but real, because a friction that returned every week is simply gone.
