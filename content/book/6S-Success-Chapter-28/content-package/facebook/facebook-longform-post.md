For about a year, the coats landed on the same chair. I hung them up, they came back, I hung them up again. And a small, tired frustration built, the kind you feel when you keep failing at something that should be simple.

Here is what I had wrong.

"A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong."

I had been treating it as a discipline problem. It was never that. And you cannot fix a wrong system by correcting it more often.

"A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again."

The way in is one word. Not who, but why.

"When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place."

So we asked why, a few times, the way a curious child would. Why the chair? Easiest spot. Why easiest? The hooks are down the hall. A few whys in, you are looking at the real thing, and the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw.

Then the part that makes it stick: we solved it together.

"A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps."

We put hooks by the door. The kid chose the spot and hammered one in, so it was theirs. The coats stopped landing on the chair. The rule underneath it all:

"When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again."
