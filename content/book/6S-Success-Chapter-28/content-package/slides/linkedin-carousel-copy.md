### Slide 1 (hook)

**The same coats, two futures. Hang them up forever, or add a hook once. One is a loop with no end. One simply stops.**

You have the keeping mindset now, and the friendly check-in that catches drift while it is small. Most of what the check-in turns up is easy: a small slip, a two-minute correction, done and forgotten. But some findings are more stubborn. The same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, the same shoes in the same doorway, this week, and last week, and the week before. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back.

Here is the reframe that dissolves the small, tired frustration of that, and it is the whole of this chapter. A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong, and a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often. So you stop patching the symptom and solve the cause, once, together. Swipe.

### Slide 3

**Fixing is not solving, and confusing the two is what exhausts you.**

Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. It is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there, which is still fully in place and will do it again tonight. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced.

The book line, verbatim:

"Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak."

Both have their place, and mopping is right for a spill. But if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor, and this chapter is about finally looking up to find the pipe.

### Slide 4

**A problem you have to fix more than twice is not one you are failing to fix. It is one you are failing to solve.**

The reframe the whole chapter holds, frozen:

"A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again."

Read that carefully, because it takes the exhaustion out of the moment a problem returns. The returning is not your failure to fix hard enough. It is the sign that the fixing was never going to be enough, and that the thing to change is why it keeps falling.

The definition, frozen:

"Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever."

### Slide 5

**The whole method hinges on one question. Ask why, not who.**

The tool for finding the cause is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. This carries the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, and it matters even more here. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat on the chair", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing about the situation. Ask "why do the coats keep landing on the chair", and you are suddenly looking at the real thing.

The why-not-who reframe, frozen:

"When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place."

### Slide 6

**Ask why a few times, the way a curious child would, and you walk from the symptom to a cause you can change.**

You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest spot. Why is it the easiest spot? Because the hooks are down the hall. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom to a cause you can actually change, and nobody has been blamed for anything.

Nobody is failing. The system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk. The other question, who, only ever leads to a person, and a person is not the thing that needs changing.

### Slide 7

### Slide 8

**A solution handed down is dropped. A solution worked out together is kept. So solve it together.**

There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever it is; it is who came up with it. It is doubly true here, for two reasons. The first is knowledge: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you design a worse solution. The second is ownership: any fix needs the people around it to keep using it.

The book line, verbatim:

"people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above."

The solve-together reframe, frozen:

"The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps."

### Slide 9

**The whole method, in four small steps, done together. Name it. Ask why. Change the system. Decide together.**

The four-step method, frozen:

"Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it."

Run once on the coats: instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes. They name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person. They ask why together until they reach the cause everyone recognizes, the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. They change the small system, a row of hooks goes up right beside the door, so the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And because a child picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they keep it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.

Quick Win: "Take the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile that always returns to the same spot, and ask out loud, "why does this keep happening here?", three or four times, until you reach something you can actually change. You just started solving it instead of fixing it."

### Slide 10

**When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it. Ask why, not who. Change the system.**

The rule, frozen, the hero of the chapter:

"When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again."

Hold this one line and the whole practice stays alive. The blamelessness is not a nicety; it is the thing that keeps the household honest and willing, because a family that is not on trial will happily help you find the real cause. Point the question at why, treat the cause as a small system to change, and solve it together, and the same problem that returned every week simply stops returning.

### Slide 12 (soft CTA)

**Keeping grows. Mindset, then the check-in, and now a way to end the drift that keeps returning, at the source, together.**

Look at what keeping has become. You started with a mindset, that a home is kept and not finished. You added a check-in that catches drift while it is small. And now you have a way to take the drift that keeps returning and end it at the source, together, without anyone being blamed. Those three, held together, are most of what it takes to keep a home good for years. Two things remain, both about rhythm: folding all of this keeping into the ordinary shape of a day, then the recurring event that renews the whole loop and completes the book.

Common Mistake: "Solving the problem for the family instead of with them, by imposing a clever fix nobody asked for. However good it is, it will be resented and abandoned within a fortnight, because people keep what they helped build and drop what was dropped on them. Solve it together, or do not bother."

One Idea to Keep, frozen: "When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it. Ask why it keeps happening, together, not who is to blame, until you reach the real cause, then change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. Fix the cause once, and you will never fix the symptom again."
