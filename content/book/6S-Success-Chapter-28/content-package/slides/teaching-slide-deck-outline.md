### Slide 4 · Fixing is not solving

- Start with the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns. Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up; it is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there, which is still fully in place and will do it again tonight.
- Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Verbatim: "Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak." Both have their place, and mopping is right for a spill, but if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor, and this chapter is about finally looking up to find the pipe.
- The fixing-versus-solving reframe, frozen: "A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again." The definition box, frozen: "Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever."

### Slide 5 · Ask why, not who

- The tool for finding the cause is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. This carries forward, straight into problem-solving, the no-blame spirit you learned to give the audit, and it matters even more here. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat on the chair", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing about the situation.
- Ask "why do the coats keep landing on the chair", and you are looking at the real thing: the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. Nobody is failing; the system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk. You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would, and along the way you notice something reassuring: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw, which is good news, because you cannot easily change a person but can nearly always change a system.
- The why-not-who reframe, frozen: "When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place."

### Slide 6 · The ladder of whys

- Make the method visible, because seeing it once is worth more than any description. You get from a symptom you might blame someone for to a cause you can actually change by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest spot. Why is it the easiest spot? Because the hooks are down the hall. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom to a cause you can change.
- Notice what happened along the way: nobody was blamed, and the thing you arrived at is a design flaw, not a character flaw. A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. The other question, who, only ever leads to a person, and a person is not the thing that needs changing. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.
- The 6S Tip, verbatim: "The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw: the bin is too far, the hook is too high, the shelf has no label, the thing has no home. When a problem keeps returning, look for the missing or awkward part of the system before you look at the people."

### Slide 7 · Solve it together

- There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is; it is who came up with it. You met this principle when you assigned the standards a few chapters back: what is chosen gets done, and what is imposed gets subverted. It is doubly true for problem-solving, for two reasons. The first is knowledge: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you will design a worse solution.
- The second is ownership: any fix, however good, needs the people around it to keep using it, and people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.
- The solve-together reframe, frozen: "The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps."

### Slide 8 · A worked problem, and the four-step method

- Put the whole thing together on the coats, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. The method is just four small steps, done together. The check-in keeps flagging the same thing: coats on the chair by the door, every week. So instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes and does four small things.
- First, they name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Second, they ask why, together, a few times, and arrive not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. Third, they change the small system to remove the cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And fourth, because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all. That is the entire method, and it works on almost anything.
- The four-step method, frozen: "Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it." Quick Win, verbatim: "Take the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile that always returns to the same spot, and ask out loud, "why does this keep happening here?", three or four times, until you reach something you can actually change. You just started solving it instead of fixing it."

### Slide 9 · The rule

- That is the whole chapter, and it fits in a single line. When the same problem keeps coming back, do not fix it a two-hundredth time; solve it once, at the cause, together. Point the question at why and never who, treat the cause as a small system to change, keep it a shared puzzle the household owns, and the same problem that returned every week simply stops returning.
- The blamelessness is not softness; it is the thing that keeps the household honest and willing, because a family that is not on trial will happily help you find the real cause. Nobody is ever the problem. A recurring problem is a design flaw, not a character flaw, and the surest way to keep the fix alive is to make the solving a shared thing the household works out together, not a clever answer imposed on the rest.
- The rule, frozen, the hero: "When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again."
