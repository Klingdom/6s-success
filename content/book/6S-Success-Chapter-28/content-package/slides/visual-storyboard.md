## Beat 3 · Fixing is not solving

Be precise about the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns: fixing corrects the symptom and is completely temporary, while solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced.

- Composition: two small facing vignettes on paper ground. On one side, "fixing", a hand mopping the same wet square of floor over and over, tired and temporary. On the other, "solving", a hand reaching up to the pipe itself with a small note, the leak found, the floor drying underneath, the mopping finally unnecessary.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, greyed soft #6A625A on the endless mopping vignette, one honey #DDA63A note and green #6E8B5B drying floor on the solving vignette. Drawn without menace or scolding; mopping is right for a spill, just not for the same square every day.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "fix the cause once, instead of the symptom forever."
- Point: fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing is correcting the symptom, the coats are on the chair so you hang them up, and it is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there, which is still fully in place and will do it again tonight. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak, and if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor.
- The frozen fixing-versus-solving reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again." The frozen definition box sits as a Fraunces box: "Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever." The book line runs small in Newsreader italic: "Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak."

## Beat 4 · Ask why, not who

The method's heart, given the pull-quote treatment: the tool for finding the cause hinges entirely on asking the right question, because who finds a person to blame and changes nothing, while why finds a system to fix and changes everything.

- Composition: two small doors on paper ground. The "who?" door opens onto a single anxious person and a dead end, a gentle grey cross, going nowhere. The "why?" door opens onto a small system, the chair, the door, the far-off hooks, a green tick, the real thing you can change.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, greyed soft #6A625A on the "who?" door and its gentle cross, green #6E8B5B on the "why?" door and its tick, slate #3C5A6B on the labels. Warm and blame-free; nobody is failing, the system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "who finds a person and changes nothing; why finds a system and changes everything."
- Point: the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction, and this carries forward, straight into problem-solving, the no-blame spirit you learned to give the audit. Ask who keeps dumping their coat on the chair and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing. Ask why the coats keep landing there and you are looking at the real thing, the chair right by the door, the hooks down the hall, and every tired person doing the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would.
- The frozen why-not-who reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place."

## Beat 5 · The ladder of whys

The method made visible as the ladder of whys: asking why a few times walks you down from a symptom you might blame someone for to a cause you can change, and the reassuring truth you meet at the bottom, the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw.

- Composition: a short vertical ladder or staircase of about four rungs on panel #FBF7EF, drilling from a surface symptom down to a root cause and out to a fix, using the coats example. Top rung: "Coats on the chair (again)". A small "why?" leads down to "The chair is the easiest spot", another "why?" to "The hooks are down the hall", another "why?" to the bottom rung and real cause, "Tired people take the shortest path". From the bottom rung, a warm arrow points out to a highlighted box: "Fix: add hooks right by the door". To one side, a small contrast panel shows the two questions: "why?" with a green tick (leads to a system) and "who?" with a gentle grey cross (leads to a person, and nowhere).
- Palette: paper and panel ground, slate #3C5A6B on the rungs, the headings, and the "why?" labels, green #6E8B5B on the "why?" tick, greyed soft #6A625A on the "who?" cross, one warm honey #DDA63A accent on the fix box. Warm, legible, and clearly not a flowchart.
- Slate #3C5A6B heading: "asking why a few times walks you down from the symptom to the cause."
- Point: you get from a symptom you might blame someone for to a cause you can change by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest spot. Why is it the easiest spot? Because the hooks are down the hall. Why does that matter? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from symptom to cause, nobody blamed, and the thing you arrived at is a design flaw, not a character flaw, a missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person but can nearly always change a system.
- Frozen figure caption, verbatim: "Asking why a few times walks you down from the symptom to the cause. The coats on the chair lead, why by why, to tired people taking the shortest path, which points straight at a fix you can actually make: hooks by the door. The other question, who, only ever leads to a person, and a person is not the thing that needs changing." The 6S Tip sits in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw: the bin is too far, the hook is too high, the shelf has no label, the thing has no home. When a problem keeps returning, look for the missing or awkward part of the system before you look at the people."

## Beat 6 · Solve it together

The one thing that decides whether a solution survives, given the pull-quote treatment: not how clever it is, but who came up with it, because the person who lives with a problem knows why it really happens and has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it.

- Composition: a warm scene on paper ground of a household of three or four gathered loosely around a doorway, working one recurring problem out together, a child among them clearly contributing, ideas passing back and forth, the whole thing a shared puzzle and not a verdict.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, green #6E8B5B on the at-ease, engaged household, one honey #DDA63A accent on the shared, owned fix, slate #3C5A6B on the labels. Warm; no scolding, because the best solution often comes from the very person you were about to blame.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "bring it as a shared puzzle, not a verdict, and let everyone, including the children, propose the fix."
- Point: what decides whether a solution survives is not how clever it is but who came up with it, and it is doubly true here for two reasons. The first is knowledge: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you design a worse solution. The second is ownership: any fix needs the people around it to keep using it, and people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and let everyone, including the children, propose the fix.
- The frozen solve-together reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps." The book line runs small in Newsreader italic: "people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above."
