## 1. The one you keep fixing

You built the good home, and your friendly check-in is catching drift while it is still small.

But it keeps turning up a more stubborn kind of finding: the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, week after week. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back.

Here is the reframe that dissolves the frustration. A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong.

And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often. It has to be solved, at the cause, once.

## 2. Fixing is not solving

We confuse these two constantly, to our exhaustion.

Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. Necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put them there.

Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced.

Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. And if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows more mopping is not the answer.

The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor.

A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Read the free chapter.

## 3. The clue hiding in the frustration

The same shoes block the same doorway. Again. And it feels like you, or the family, or the home, keeps failing at something simple.

You do not. A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong.

The shoes are not there because anyone lacks discipline. They are there because something about the setup quietly makes that spot the easiest place to leave them.

That single reframe is the whole of this chapter, and it turns a tired, personal frustration into a small, solvable design question.

## 4. Ask why, not who

When something keeps going wrong, the instinct is to ask who keeps doing it.

That question sends you exactly the wrong way. Ask who, and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and the mess back tomorrow. You found a culprit and changed nothing.

Ask why instead, and you are looking at the real thing: the chair is by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person takes the shortest path.

Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything.

The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place. Read the free chapter.

## 5. Keep asking why, like a curious child

You get from a symptom to its cause the way a curious child would: by gently asking why, a few times over.

Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter? Because tired people always take the shortest path.

A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom you were blaming a person for to a cause you can actually change.

No argument, no culprit. Just a small system that was asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk.

## 6. A design flaw, not a character flaw

Here is the reassuring thing you notice as you ask why.

The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw. A missing hook. An awkward distance. A bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home.

That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

So when a problem keeps returning, look for the missing or awkward part of the setup before you look at the people. Nobody is failing. The design is.

## 7. Solve it together

Whether a solution survives depends less on how clever it is and more on who came up with it.

Two reasons. Knowledge: the person who lives with a problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not. Ownership: any fix needs the people around it to keep using it.

So bring the problem to the household as a shared puzzle, not a verdict, and let everyone, including the children, propose the fix. They are often startlingly good at it.

The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and it will still be running in a month.

A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps. Read the free chapter.

## 8. Four small steps

Solving a recurring problem is not a formula. It is just four small things, done together.

Name the problem that keeps drifting back, plainly, as a problem and not a person.

Ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause everyone recognizes.

Change the small system so the cause is gone.

And let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.

That is the whole method, and it works on almost anything: the coats, the dishes, the shoes, the homework, the endless small frictions of a shared home.

When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again. Read the free chapter.
