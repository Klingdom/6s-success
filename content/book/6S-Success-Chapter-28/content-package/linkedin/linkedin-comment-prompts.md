# Chapter 28 LinkedIn Comment Prompts: Stop Fixing the Same Thing, Start Solving It

Five prompts to start genuine discussion. Drop one in the comments of a post or article to invite replies. Warm, collaborative, blameless voice: a recurring problem is a design flaw and not a character flaw, nobody is ever the problem, and the household solves it together. No corporate framing, no force, blame, or control language, no scolding, no brand names.

---

1. Be honest with me: what is the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile or the mess that returns to the same spot no matter how many times you deal with it? A problem you have to fix more than twice is not one you are failing to fix; it is one you are failing to solve. If you stopped and asked, out loud, "why does this keep happening here?" three or four times, where do you think you would end up, and does it feel like a person to blame or a small system to change?

2. Here is the shift I want to test against your life: who finds a person to blame and changes nothing, while why finds a system to fix and changes everything. When something keeps going wrong in your home, is your first thought about who keeps doing it or about why it keeps happening? And have you noticed what changes in the room, and in the people, depending on which question you reach for first?

3. The reassuring claim of this chapter is that the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw: a missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a thing with no home. Can you think of a recurring annoyance in your home that turned out to be a setup problem rather than a discipline problem the moment you looked at it honestly? What was the small system underneath it, and what would it take to change that instead of the person?

4. The counterintuitive part is that the survival of a fix depends less on how clever it is and more on who came up with it, because people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. Think about the household rules or fixes you have tried. Were they handed down or worked out together? For those who have brought a recurring problem to the whole family as a shared puzzle, children included, did the solution turn out better, and, more to the point, was it still running a month later?

5. Here is the warmer half of the question. So many homes have one person quietly patching the same thing forever, the same coats, the same dishes, the same shoes, carrying it alone and slowly wearing down. What if instead you named it plainly as a problem and not a person, asked why together until you reached the cause, and let everyone help decide the fix? For those who have solved something this way rather than fixing it forever, did the best idea really come from the very person you were about to blame, and did the loop actually end?
