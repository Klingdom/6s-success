# The Same Coats Land on the Same Chair Every Week, and You Keep Hanging Them Up, Which Is Exactly Why It Feels Like a Discipline You Keep Failing, When It Is Really the Opposite: a Small System to Solve Once, Together, So You Never Have to Fix It Again.

You know the ones. The same coats on the same chair. The same dishes stacking by the sink. The same shoes blocking the same doorway. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build, because it feels like the home, or the family, or you, keeps failing at something simple.

> A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong.

The coats return to the chair not because your household lacks discipline but because something about the setup quietly makes the chair the easiest place to put them. And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once. That is a different skill from everything the book has taught, and it is the last one Sustain needs, and the two things that make it work, more than any technique, are these: you ask why it happens and never who is to blame, and you solve it together. ## Fixing is not solving

Start with the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns. Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion.

> A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. It is necessary, it is quick, and it is completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there, which is still fully in place and will do it again tonight. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. Both have their place, and mopping is right for a spill, but if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor, and this chapter is about finally looking up to find the pipe.

> Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

## Ask why, not who

The tool for finding the cause is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. This carries forward, straight into problem-solving, the no-blame spirit you learned to give the audit, and it matters even more here.

> When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.

Ask who keeps dumping their coat on the chair, and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing about the situation. Ask why the coats keep landing on the chair, and you are suddenly looking at the real thing: the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person coming home does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. Nobody is failing. The system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk. You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would, and you notice something reassuring along the way: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw. A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

## Solve it together

There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is. It is who came up with it.

> The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.

You met this principle when you assigned the standards a few chapters back: what is chosen gets done, and what is imposed gets subverted. It is doubly true for problem-solving, for two reasons. The first is knowledge: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you will design a worse solution. The second is ownership: any fix, however good, needs the people around it to keep using it, and people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.

## A worked problem: the coats

Put the whole thing together on the coats, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. The method is just four small steps, done together.

> Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.

The check-in keeps flagging the same thing: coats on the chair by the door, every week. So instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes and does four small things. First, they name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Second, they ask why, together, a few times, and arrive not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes the moment it is said: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. Third, they change the small system to remove that cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And fourth, because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all. That is the entire method, and it works on almost anything: the dishes, the shoes, the homework, the endless small recurring frictions of a shared home.
