# Newsletter Edition: Family Problem Solving

**Subject line (placeholder):** When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again.

Hi friend,

Last issue gave you the first real tool for keeping the gains: a friendly check-in on the home, a quick and regular look that catches drift while it is still small and never once puts the household on trial. It is good at what it does. But it keeps turning up a more stubborn kind of finding, and this issue is about that: not the one-off slip, which is a two-minute fix, but the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the same sink, week after week. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration builds. This chapter is the reframe that dissolves it.

**A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong.**

That is the whole turn, and everything else follows from it. If the problem were a slip, correcting it more carefully would settle it. But it is not a slip, so it will not settle.

**And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once.**

There is a plain distinction hiding under all of this, and naming it is half the chapter: the difference between fixing a thing and solving it. **Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak.** Both have their place, and mopping is exactly right for a spill. But if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. Here is the reframe to keep.

**A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.**

So the practice has a name, and here is the definition to hold on to.

**Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.**

The whole thing hinges on one small choice, the question you ask when something keeps going wrong. Ask who keeps dumping their coat, and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask why the coats keep landing there, and you are suddenly looking at the real thing. This is the heart of the method, and it carries the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, where it matters even more.

**When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.**

You get from the symptom to the cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, and you have walked from a symptom to a cause you can actually change. And **the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw.** A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. **That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.**

There is one more thing that decides whether a fix survives, and it is not how clever the fix is; it is who came up with it. The person who lives with a problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, do not, and they are the one who has to keep any fix alive. So leave them out and you design a worse solution that nobody keeps. Bring them in, and you get a better one that lasts, because **people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above.**

**The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.**

Put together, the practice is four small steps, and it is worth keeping them close.

**Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.**

Walk it once on the coats and you have the whole thing. Instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes: they name the problem plainly, ask why together until they reach the cause everyone recognizes, and change the small system so the shortest path and the right path become the same path, a row of hooks right by the door. And because a child picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, so they keep it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.

**To try this week:** Take the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile that always returns to the same spot, and ask out loud, "why does this keep happening here?", three or four times, until you reach something you can actually change. That is the whole move, and you just started solving it instead of fixing it. Then, when you are ready, bring one recurring annoyance to the whole household, phrase it as "why does this keep happening?" and never "who keeps doing this?", and let everyone, including the children, propose a fix. The best solution often comes from the very person you were about to blame, and a fix the household worked out together is the one it actually keeps.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, just a little further along.

**The needle takes its second Sustain step, a little further along the path to the goal. A home that solves its recurring problems, instead of patching them forever, has quietly removed a source of friction that used to return every week, which is exactly the kind of gain that makes a home more able to keep itself. The gap is shorter again, and the last of it is what the final chapters close.**

It is honestly a small step, and an honest one. The audit took the first Sustain step by catching drift faster; this chapter goes a level deeper, stopping one drift from returning at all. A source of friction that used to come back every single week is simply gone now, and a home with one fewer returning problem is a home that keeps itself that little bit more on its own. You now have the mindset, the check-in that catches drift while it is small, and a way to end the drift that keeps returning, at the source, together, without anyone being blamed. Two things remain, both about rhythm, and they are the next chapters. For now, hold on to the one idea this whole practice rests on.

**When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it. Ask why it keeps happening, together, not who is to blame, until you reach the real cause, then change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. Fix the cause once, and you will never fix the symptom again.**

Which is the same thing said more simply: **when something keeps going wrong, do not look for someone to blame. Look, together, for the small system to fix.**
