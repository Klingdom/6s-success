# Chapter 28 Key Takeaways: Family Problem Solving

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **This chapter is the second piece of keeping-machinery, and it solves what the audit finds.** Chapter 26 gave the mindset and Chapter 27 gave the friendly check-in that catches drift while it is small. But the check-in keeps turning up a more stubborn kind of finding: not a one-off slip, but the same problem drifting back in the same place, week after week. This chapter is how a household solves those, together.

2. **A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct; it is a signal that a small system is wrong.** The coats return to the chair not because your household lacks discipline but because something about the setup quietly makes the chair the easiest place to put them. That single reframe is the whole of the chapter, and it dissolves the small, tired frustration of failing at something simple.

3. **A wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often.** Any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once.

4. **Fixing is not solving, and confusing them is what exhausts you.** A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix; it is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

5. **The fixing-versus-solving reframe, frozen.** A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

6. **Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak.** Both have their place, and mopping is right for a spill, but if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor.

7. **The definition, frozen.** Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

8. **The whole method hinges on asking the right question: why, not who.** The wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. Ask who keeps dumping their coat, and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask why the coats keep landing there, and you are suddenly looking at the real thing.

9. **The why-not-who reframe, frozen.** When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.

10. **This carries the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, and it matters even more here.** Who finds a culprit but changes nothing about the situation that produced the behaviour; why looks at the chair by the door, the hooks down the hall, and the tired person who takes the path of least resistance. Nobody is failing. The system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk.

11. **You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would.** Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom to a cause you can actually change.

12. **The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw.** A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

13. **Whether a solution survives depends not on how clever it is, but on who came up with it.** The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it.

14. **The solve-together reframe, frozen.** The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.

15. **Solving together is doubly true for two reasons: knowledge and ownership.** The person who lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, do not, so leave them out and you design a worse solution. And any fix needs the people around it to keep using it, because people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above.

16. **Bring the problem to the household as a shared puzzle, not a verdict, and let everyone, including the children, propose the fix.** The children are often startlingly good at this. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.

17. **The four-step method, frozen.** Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.

18. **The worked example: the coats, solved end to end.** Instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes: they name the problem plainly, ask why together until they reach the cause everyone recognizes, change the small system so the shortest path and the right path are the same path, and, because a child picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, so they keep it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.

19. **The needle takes its second Sustain step, and it is honestly small.** The audit took the first step by catching drift faster; this chapter goes a level deeper, stopping the drift from returning at all. A source of friction that used to come back every single week is simply gone now, and a home with one fewer returning problem is a home that keeps itself that little bit more on its own. Small, but real and permanent.

20. **The rule, frozen (hero).** When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again.

21. **Keeping grows.** You now have the mindset, the check-in that catches drift while it is small, and a way to end the drift that keeps returning at the source, together, without anyone being blamed. Two things remain, both about rhythm: folding keeping into the daily shape (Chapter 29), and the recurring event that renews the whole loop and completes the book (Chapter 30). For now, the One Idea to Keep: when something keeps going wrong, do not look for someone to blame; look, together, for the small system to fix.
