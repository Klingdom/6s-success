## Search intent
Keeping a good home good, after the reader has built it, taken on the keeping mindset from Chapter 26, and started the friendly check-in from Chapter 27. Readers arriving here have a home that is cleared, arranged, clean, safe, and standardized, and a regular check-in that catches drift, but the check-in keeps surfacing the same stubborn problems: the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, week after week, no matter how often they are fixed. The chapter answers with one hero device and one rule: stop fixing the symptom and solve the cause, once, together, and when the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it, ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again. It draws the fixing-versus-solving distinction (a problem you fix more than twice is one you are failing to solve; fixing puts the thing back, solving changes why it fell); it delivers the definition (family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together); it delivers the why-not-who method, home-scaled (ask why a few times, the way a curious child would, until you reach the real cause, which is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw); it delivers the solve-together discipline (the person who lives with the problem knows why and keeps the fix alive, so they should help design it; handed-down solutions are dropped, shared ones are kept); and it walks the four-step method end to end on the coats example. It reads the friction meter as a small SECOND Sustain step, not a milestone: a home that solves its recurring problems, instead of patching them forever, has removed a source of returning friction and become a little more able to keep itself. Five of six S's are done; the goal is reached only at Chapter 30.

## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Names the distinction most advice skips: fixing puts the symptom back forever, solving changes why it happens once, and a problem you have to fix more than twice is one you are failing to solve, not one you are failing to fix
- Reframes a recurring problem as a signal that a small system is wrong, not a slip anyone is failing to correct, so a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently
- Makes the useful question explicit: not who keeps doing it (which finds a person to blame and changes nothing) but why it keeps happening (which finds a system to fix and changes everything), asked a few times the way a curious child would, until you reach the real cause
- Gives the reassuring truth that the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw: a missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home, which is good news because you cannot easily change a person but can nearly always change a system
- Makes ownership the deciding factor in whether a fix survives: the person who lives with a problem knows why it really happens and has to keep any fix alive, so they should help design it, because people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above
- Walks a full worked example end to end, the coats that always land on the chair, through the four-step method (name it, ask why together, change the small system, decide together), so the reader sees it run once
- Reads the friction meter honestly as a small second Sustain step, not a milestone: the needle moves a little further because solving a recurring problem removes one returning source of friction for good, which makes the home a touch more self-tending
- Keeps the register warm, collaborative, and blameless, never a cold corporate framework or a tense, blaming family meeting: nobody is ever the problem, and the emotional heart is the relief of ending the endless-patching loop
