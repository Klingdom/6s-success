## 1. Chapter Opening: The stubborn findings the check-in keeps turning up
If you have started running the friendly check-in from the last chapter, most of what it turns up is easy: a small drift, a two-minute correction, done and forgotten. But you will also have noticed a different, more stubborn kind of finding, the one that shows up this week, and last week, and the week before: the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes stacking by the sink, the same shoes blocking the same doorway. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build, because it feels like the home, or the family, or you, keeps failing at something simple. Here is the reframe that dissolves that frustration, and it is the whole of this chapter: a problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct, it is a signal that a small system is wrong, and a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently; it has to be solved, at the cause, once. That is a different skill from everything the book has taught, and it is the last one Sustain needs. The two things that make it work, more than any technique, are these: you ask why it happens and never who is to blame, and you solve it together, as the people who actually live with it. Part Eight continues.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- Tell a one-off slip from a recurring problem, and treat the two completely differently
- Stop patching the symptom, and solve the cause instead
- Ask why a problem keeps happening, not who keeps causing it, until you reach the real cause
- See the real cause as a design flaw in a small system, never a character flaw in a person
- Solve the problem together, so the household owns the fix and actually keeps it

## 3. Fixing Is Not Solving
Start with the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns. Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up; it is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak, and if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor, and this chapter is about finally looking up to find the pipe.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the fixing-versus-solving reframe): A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the definition box): Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

## 4. Ask Why, Not Who
The tool for finding the cause is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. This carries forward, straight into problem-solving, the no-blame spirit you learned to give the audit, and it matters even more here. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat on the chair", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing about the situation. Ask "why do the coats keep landing on the chair", and you are looking at the real thing: the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. Nobody is failing; the system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk. You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would, and along the way you notice something reassuring: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw, which is good news, because you cannot easily change a person but can nearly always change a system.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the why-not-who reframe): When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.

## 5. Solve It Together
There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is; it is who came up with it. You met this principle when you assigned the standards a few chapters back: what is chosen gets done, and what is imposed gets subverted. It is doubly true for problem-solving, for two reasons. The first is knowledge: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you will design a worse solution. The second is ownership: any fix, however good, needs the people around it to keep using it, and people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the solve-together reframe): The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.

## 6. A Worked Problem
The whole thing put together on the coats, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. The method is just four small steps, done together. The check-in keeps flagging the same thing: coats on the chair by the door, every week. So instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes and does four small things. First, they name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Second, they ask why, together, a few times, and arrive not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. Third, they change the small system to remove the cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And fourth, because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all. That is the entire method, and it works on almost anything.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the four-step method): Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.

- Callout (Quick Win): Take the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile that always returns to the same spot, and ask out loud, "why does this keep happening here?", three or four times, until you reach something you can actually change. You just started solving it instead of fixing it.
- Callout (6S Tip): The real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw: the bin is too far, the hook is too high, the shelf has no label, the thing has no home. When a problem keeps returning, look for the missing or awkward part of the system before you look at the people.

## 8. Keeping Grows (the hand-off)
Look at what keeping has become. You started with a mindset, that a home is kept and not finished. You added a check-in that catches drift while it is small. And now you have a way to take the drift that keeps returning and end it at the source, together, without anyone being blamed. Those three, held together, are most of what it takes to keep a home good for years: you notice early, and you solve what recurs, so the home neither slides back all at once nor wears you down with the same small battles fought over and over. Two things remain, and both are about rhythm rather than technique. The first is folding all of this keeping into the ordinary shape of a day, so the small acts of upkeep happen at natural, set moments instead of relying on anyone remembering; that is the next chapter. The second is coming back around, every so often, for a full renewal, the recurring event that carries you through the whole loop again, better each time, and completes the book. For now, hold the one idea this chapter turns on: when something keeps going wrong, do not look for someone to blame; look, together, for the small system to fix.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Solving the problem for the family instead of with them, by imposing a clever fix nobody asked for. However good it is, it will be resented and abandoned within a fortnight, because people keep what they helped build and drop what was dropped on them. Solve it together, or do not bother.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it. Ask why it keeps happening, together, not who is to blame, until you reach the real cause, then change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. Fix the cause once, and you will never fix the symptom again.
