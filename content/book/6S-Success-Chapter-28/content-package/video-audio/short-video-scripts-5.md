## SHORT 1: Stop fixing it, start solving it
**Topic: the hero reframe, a recurring problem is a signal that a small system is wrong. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** If you keep fixing the same thing over and over, you are not failing to fix it. You are failing to solve it. There is a difference, and it changes everything.

**Script:**
You have the mindset now, and the friendly check-in that catches drift while it is small. But the check-in keeps turning up a more stubborn kind of finding: the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes by the sink, week after week. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back. Here is the reframe that dissolves the frustration, and it is the whole chapter. A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong. And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once. A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

- 14s: "It is not a slip. It is a signal a small system is wrong."
- 30s: "Fixing puts the thing back."
- 44s: "Solving changes why it fell."

Warm daylight, no blame.

## SHORT 2: Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak
**Topic: the fixing-versus-solving distinction and the definition. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** If you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer.

**Script:**
Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. It is necessary, it is quick, and it is completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are that same square of floor, and this is about finally looking up to find the pipe. So here is the whole idea. Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

- 0s: "Fixing is mopping the floor."
- 12s: "Solving is finding the leak."
- 26s: "Stop patching the symptom."
- 38s: "Fix the cause once, instead of the symptom forever."

## SHORT 3: Ask why, not who
**Topic: the method's heart, the ladder of whys, no blame. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it. The useful one is why it keeps happening. And they lead to completely different places.

**Script:**
Here is the tool for finding the cause, and it is embarrassingly simple. When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat on the chair", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask "why do the coats keep landing on the chair", and you are suddenly looking at the real thing: the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person takes the shortest path. Nobody is failing. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place. You get there by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. And you notice something reassuring: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw. That is good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "Do not ask who keeps doing it."
- 14s: "Ask why it keeps happening."
- 30s: "Who changes nothing. Why changes everything."
- 44s: "A design flaw, not a character flaw."

**[VISUAL] direction:** The ladder of whys drawn as a short staircase: coats on the chair, again, drilling why by why down to tired people take the shortest path, then a warm arrow to a highlighted fix box, hooks by the door. A side panel contrasts "why?" with a green tick and "who?" with a grey cross. Warm, human, slate and honey.

## SHORT 4: Solve it together, or do not bother
**Topic: the ownership move, people keep what they helped build. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Whether a solution survives has almost nothing to do with how clever it is. It is about who came up with it.

**Script:**
There is one more thing that decides whether a fix actually survives, and it is not how clever it is. It is who came up with it. The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps. Two reasons. Knowledge: the person who lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not. And ownership: people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The clever fix nobody asked for gets dropped."
- 14s: "People keep what they helped build."
- 30s: "Bring it as a shared puzzle, not a verdict."
- 44s: "Let everyone, including the kids, propose the fix."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A warm household gathered around one recurring problem as four steps: name it, ask why together with a child contributing, change the system with a hand fixing hooks by the door, decide together with everyone nodding and a child hammering a hook. A foot line: the people who live with it help decide, so they keep it. Unmistakably a family, not a boardroom.

**Topic: the friction meter moves a little further, honestly small. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** For the second time in Sustain, the needle moves. Just a little. And that small, honest, permanent gain is the whole point.

**Script:**
Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, a little further along the path it revealed two chapters ago. Last chapter, the audit took the needle its first Sustain step, by helping the home catch drift while it was small. This chapter goes a level deeper: instead of catching the same drift faster, you have stopped it from returning at all, by solving the system behind it. So the needle takes its second Sustain step, a little further toward the goal. It is small, honestly so, because you solved one recurring problem, not all of them at once. But it is real, because solving a recurring problem changes something permanent. A source of friction that used to come back every single week is simply gone now, and a home with one fewer returning problem is a home that keeps itself that little bit more on its own. So hold the rule that carries it: when the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again. The goal is reached only at the very end of the book.
