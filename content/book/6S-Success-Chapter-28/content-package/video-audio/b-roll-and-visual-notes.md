The definition box is frozen: "Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever." The four-step method is frozen: "Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it."
- The three reframes are the spine of the chapter, each frozen. FIXING VERSUS SOLVING, frozen, render verbatim: "A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again." WHY, NOT WHO, frozen, render verbatim: "When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place." SOLVE TOGETHER, frozen, render verbatim: "The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps."
- The starting point is the good, standardized home now carried on the keeping mindset from Chapter 26 and the friendly check-in from Chapter 27. The check-in is good at catching drift while it is small, and most of what it turns up is easy: a small drift, a two-minute correction, done and forgotten. But it keeps turning up a more stubborn kind of finding, the one that shows up this week, and last week, and the week before: the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes stacking by the sink, the same shoes blocking the same doorway. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build, because it feels like the home, or the family, or you, keeps failing at something simple. This chapter is the reframe that dissolves that frustration, and it is the last skill Sustain needs.
- The reframe that is the whole chapter, drawn plainly: a problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong. The coats return to the chair not because your household lacks discipline but because something about the setup quietly makes the chair the easiest place to put them. And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once. Show the distinction the whole chapter turns on: fixing is correcting the symptom, necessary, quick, and completely temporary; solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak, and if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer.
- The hero is solving the recurring problem together, and the two things that make it work more than any technique: you ask why it happens and never who is to blame, and you solve it together, as the people who actually live with it. The why-not-who move is the method's heart, carrying forward the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, and it matters even more here. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat on the chair", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing about the situation. Ask "why do the coats keep landing on the chair", and you are suddenly looking at the real thing: the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing. Nobody is failing; the system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk.
- The ladder of whys, drawn as the way you get from symptom to cause: gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom you were blaming a person for to a cause you can actually change, and you notice something reassuring along the way: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw. It is a missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.
- The solve-together move is what decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is; it is who came up with it. Two reasons, knowledge and ownership: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you design a worse solution; and any fix needs the people around it to keep using it, because people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.
- The worked example is the coats, run through the four small steps, done together, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. First, they name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Second, they ask why, together, a few times, and arrive not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. Third, they change the small system to remove the cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And fourth, because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.
