## INTRO

Hey, welcome back. Over the last two episodes you built the foundation of keeping: the mindset, that a home is kept and not finished, and then the friendly check-in, that regular, warm look at the house that catches drift while it is still small. And if you have started running that check-in, you have probably noticed something. Most of what it turns up is easy. A small drift, a two-minute correction, done and forgotten. But there is another kind of finding, a more stubborn one, the kind that shows up this week, and last week, and the week before. The same coats on the same chair. The same dishes stacking by the sink. The same shoes blocking the same doorway. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build, because it feels like the home, or the family, or you, keeps failing at something simple.

This episode is the reframe that dissolves that frustration, and it is the whole of the chapter. Here it is. A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong. The coats return to the chair not because your household lacks discipline but because something about the setup quietly makes the chair the easiest place to put them. And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once. That is a different skill from everything the book has taught so far, and it is the last one Sustain needs.

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: FIXING IS NOT SOLVING

Let me start with the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns. Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion.

Fixing is correcting the symptom. The coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. It is necessary, it is quick, and it is completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there, which is still fully in place and will do it again tonight. Solving is different. It looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. And here is the image I want you to carry: fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. Both have their place, and mopping is right for a spill. But if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The recurring problems your check-in keeps surfacing are exactly that same square of floor, and this chapter is about finally looking up to find the pipe.

So here is the frozen line I want you to hold onto: a problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

And that gives us the definition, so listen for the spirit of it: family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together. When the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: ASK WHY, NOT WHO

So how do you find the cause? The tool is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction. And this is the heart of the whole method, so stay with me.

Here is the frozen reframe: when something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.

This carries forward, straight into problem-solving, the no-blame spirit you learned to give the audit, and it matters even more here. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat on the chair", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back on the chair tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing about the situation. Ask "why do the coats keep landing on the chair", and suddenly you are looking at the real thing. The chair is right by the door. The hooks are down the hall in a closet. And every tired person coming home does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing and drops the coat on the nearest surface. Nobody is failing. The system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk, and it loses that argument every time.

And the way you get from the symptom to the cause is just to keep gently asking why, a few times over, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter so much? Because people are tired when they come in and will always take the shortest path. A few whys in, you have walked from a symptom you were blaming a person for to a cause you can actually change. And you notice something reassuring along the way: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw. A missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin that is too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: SOLVE IT TOGETHER

Now there is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is. It is who came up with it.

Here is the frozen reframe: the person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.

You met this principle a few chapters back, when you assigned the standards: what is chosen gets done, and what is imposed gets subverted. It is doubly true for problem-solving, for two reasons. The first is knowledge. The person who actually lives with the problem, the teenager whose bag always ends up in the hall, the parent who always finds the dishes by the sink, knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not. Leave them out and you will design a worse solution. The second is ownership. Any fix, however good, needs the people around it to keep using it, and people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: A WORKED PROBLEM

Let me put the whole thing together on the coats, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. The method is just four small steps, done together. And the frozen version is this: solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.

So the check-in keeps flagging the same thing: coats on the chair by the door, every week. And instead of hanging them up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes and does four small things. First, they name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Second, they ask why, together, a few times, and arrive not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes the moment it is said: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, and tired people take the shortest path. Third, they change the small system to remove that cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And fourth, because the whole family worked it out, and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The coats go on the hooks, the chair stays clear, and the problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all. That is the entire method, and it works on almost anything: the dishes, the shoes, the homework, the endless small recurring frictions of a shared home.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So if you take one thing from this episode, take the rule, because it holds the whole chapter. When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again. That is it.

And here is your assignment, and it is one of the smallest in the whole book, which is exactly the point. Take the one thing in your home you are tired of fixing, the pile that always returns to the same spot, and ask out loud, "why does this keep happening here?", three or four times, until you reach something you can actually change. That is it. You just started solving it instead of fixing it. And if you want the shared version, bring one recurring annoyance to the whole household, phrase it as "why does this keep happening?" and never "who keeps doing this?", and let everyone, including the children, propose a fix. The best solution often comes from the very person you were about to blame.
