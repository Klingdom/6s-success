## SECTION 1: FIXING IS NOT SOLVING

[HOST]
Start with the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns.

[VISUAL: Someone mopping the same square of floor every single day, a weary loop. Then they look up and find the pipe leaking above, the real source. Warm line-and-flat-color style.]

[HOST, voice over]
Fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities, and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion. Fixing is correcting the symptom: the coats are on the chair, so you hang them up. It is necessary, it is quick, and it is completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. If you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Fixing is mopping the floor. Solving is finding the leak.]

[HOST, on camera]
Here is the line that holds the whole reframe. A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again. And that gives us the definition: family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together. When the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.

## SECTION 2: ASK WHY, NOT WHO

[HOST]
So how do you find the cause? The tool is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction.

[VISUAL: The ladder of whys, a short staircase of slate rungs. Top rung: "Coats on the chair (again)". A "why?" down to "The chair is the easiest spot", another "why?" to "The hooks are down the hall", another "why?" to the bottom rung: "Tired people take the shortest path". A warm arrow out to a highlighted honey box: "Fix: add hooks by the door". A side panel contrasts "why?" with a green tick and "who?" with a grey cross.]

[HOST, voice over]
When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. Ask "who keeps dumping their coat", and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask "why do the coats keep landing there", and you are looking at the real thing: the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person takes the shortest path. Nobody is failing. The system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. The hooks are in the wrong place.]

[HOST, on camera]
You get from the symptom to the cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would. Why are the coats on the chair? Because it is the easiest place. Why is it the easiest place? Because the hooks are far away. Why does that matter? Because tired people take the shortest path. And you notice something reassuring along the way: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw. A missing hook, an awkward distance, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

## SECTION 3: SOLVE IT TOGETHER

[HOST]
There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is. It is who came up with it.

[VISUAL: A warm household gathered loosely around a doorway, working out one problem together. A speech bubble, "the coats keep landing on the chair". A child among them exchanging "why?" bubbles. A hand fixing a row of hooks by the door. Everyone nodding, a child hammering in one hook.]

[HOST, voice over]
The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps. There are two reasons. Knowledge: the person who lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not. And ownership: people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: People keep what they helped build.]

[HOST, on camera]
So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The solution you land on together will be better than the one you would have imposed, and, far more importantly, it will still be running in a month.

## SECTION 4: A WORKED PROBLEM

[HOST]
Let me put the whole thing together on the coats, because seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description. The method is just four small steps, done together.

[VISUAL: The four steps as four labelled stages. "Name it": the speech bubble, "the coats keep landing on the chair". "Ask why, together": the family exchanging "why?" bubbles. "Change the system": a hand fixing hooks by the door. "Decide together": a child hammering in a hook. A foot line: "the people who live with it help decide, so they keep it".]

[HOST, voice over]
Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it. So instead of hanging the coats up a two-hundredth time, the household sits down for five minutes. They name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person. They ask why, together, until they reach the cause everyone recognizes: tired people take the shortest path. They change the small system: a row of hooks by the door, so the shortest path and the right path are the same path.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Name it. Ask why. Change the system. Decide together.]

[HOST, on camera]
And fourth, because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs, and they use it. The problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all. That is the entire method, and it works on almost anything: the dishes, the shoes, the homework, the endless small recurring frictions of a shared home.
