## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
You have the keeping mindset now, from Chapter 26, and the friendly check-in from Chapter 27 that catches drift while it is still small; here is how to solve the more stubborn thing the check-in keeps turning up. Most of what the check-in finds is easy, a small drift and a two-minute correction, done and forgotten; but you will also have noticed a different, more stubborn kind of finding, the one that shows up this week, and last week, and the week before, the same coats on the same chair, the same dishes stacking by the sink, the same shoes blocking the same doorway, and you keep fixing them and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration begins to build, because it feels like the home, or the family, or you, keeps failing at something simple. Here is the reframe that dissolves that frustration, and it is the whole of this chapter: a problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct, it is a signal that a small system is wrong, and a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently, it has to be solved, at the cause, once. Start with the distinction the whole chapter turns on, because naming it changes how you feel the moment a problem returns: fixing and solving are two genuinely different activities and we confuse them constantly, to our exhaustion, because fixing corrects the symptom and is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, while solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced; fixing is mopping the floor, solving is finding the leak, and if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. The tool for finding the cause is embarrassingly simple, and it hinges entirely on asking the right question, because the wrong question sends you in exactly the wrong direction: ask who keeps dumping their coat on the chair and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow, because you have identified a culprit but changed nothing; ask why the coats keep landing on the chair and you are looking at the real thing, that the chair is right by the door, the hooks are down the hall, and every tired person does the sensible, path-of-least-resistance thing, so nobody is failing, the system is simply asking people to walk further than a tired human will walk. This carries the no-blame spirit of the audit straight into problem-solving, and it matters even more here: you get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would, and along the way you notice something reassuring, the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw, which is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person but can nearly always change a system. There is one more thing that decides whether a solution survives, and it is not how clever the solution is, it is who came up with it, for two reasons, knowledge and ownership: the person who actually lives with the problem knows things about why it happens that you, solving it from the outside, simply do not, so leave them out and you design a worse solution, and any fix, however good, needs the people around it to keep using it, because people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above, so you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix. The whole thing put together on the coats is just four small steps done together, and seeing it run once is worth more than any amount of description: the household sits down for five minutes and names the problem plainly as a problem and not a person, asks why together a few times and arrives not at a guilty party but at a cause everyone recognizes, changes the small system to remove the cause so the shortest path and the right path become the same path, and because the whole family worked it out and one of the kids picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, it is theirs and they keep it, and the problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all. The rule holds the whole chapter together: when the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it, ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again. The friction meter takes its second Sustain step, a little further along the path it revealed two chapters ago, and it is honestly small because you solved one recurring problem and not all of them at once, but it is real because solving a recurring problem changes something permanent, a source of friction that used to come back every single week is simply gone now, and a home with one fewer returning problem is a home that keeps itself that little bit more on its own. Hands off to Chapter 29 · Daily 6S Times: you now have the mindset, the check-in that catches drift while it is small, and a way to end the drift that keeps returning at the source, together, without anyone being blamed, and the next chapter folds the whole of keeping into the ordinary rhythm of a day, so the small acts of upkeep happen at set, natural moments rather than depending on anyone remembering to do them.

## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule (hero): **When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again.**
- The four-step method (frozen): **Solve a recurring problem together in four small steps: name the problem that keeps drifting back; ask why, a few times, until you reach the real cause; change the small system so the cause is gone; and let the people who live with it help decide the fix, so they keep it.**
- The definition box (frozen): **Family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a home throws up, together: when the same thing keeps drifting back, you stop patching the symptom, ask why it keeps happening rather than who is to blame, and change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. It fixes the cause once, instead of the symptom forever.**
- The fixing-versus-solving reframe (frozen): **A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix. It is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.**
- The why-not-who reframe (frozen): **When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place.**
- The solve-together reframe (frozen): **The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps.**
- The friction-meter caption (frozen, for graphics): **The needle takes its second Sustain step, a little further along the path to the goal. A home that solves its recurring problems, instead of patching them forever, has quietly removed a source of friction that used to return every week, which is exactly the kind of gain that makes a home more able to keep itself. The gap is shorter again, and the last of it is what the final chapters close.**
- One Idea to Keep: **When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it. Ask why it keeps happening, together, not who is to blame, until you reach the real cause, then change the small system so it cannot easily happen again. Fix the cause once, and you will never fix the symptom again.**
