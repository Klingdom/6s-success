Scope fence: this chapter is the THIRD chapter of Part Eight, and it CONTINUES Sustain, the sixth and FINAL
of the six S's, with the second concrete piece of keeping-machinery on top of Chapter 27's friendly audit.
Where the audit FINDS the recurring problems, this chapter SOLVES them, together. Its whole work is a reframe:
a problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct, it is a signal that a small system
is wrong, and a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be
fixed by mopping the floor more diligently, so you stop patching the symptom and solve the cause, once,
together. It draws the fixing-versus-solving distinction (a problem you fix more than twice is one you are
failing to solve; fixing puts the thing back, solving changes why it fell) with the fixing-versus-solving
reframe and the definition box (family problem solving is how a household settles the recurring problems a
home throws up, together); it delivers the method's heart, ask why and never who (who finds a person to blame
and changes nothing, why finds a system to fix and changes everything; ask why a few times, the way a curious
child would, until you reach the real cause, which is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw) with
the why-not-who reframe; it delivers the discipline of solving together (the person who lives with the problem
knows why it happens and has to keep the fix alive, so they should help design it; a solution handed down is
dropped, a solution worked out together is kept) with the solve-together reframe; and it walks the four-step
method end to end on the coats that always land on the chair (name it; ask why together; change the small
system; decide together) with the frozen four-step method. It USES the keeping mindset Chapter 26 built and
the friendly check-in Chapter 27 built without re-teaching either, and does NOT build the daily-times system
(Chapter 29) or the recurring 6S event (Chapter 30), which are named only as still to come. CRITICAL fence:
this is the shared method for SOLVING the bigger recurring problems the audit keeps turning up (ask why not
who, fix the system not the symptom, solve it together), NOT the one-time baseline audit, NOT the recurring
drift-catching check-in of Chapter 27, and NOT the daily rhythm or the recurring event. Because a home that
solves its recurring problems removes a source of friction that used to return every week, the friction needle
takes its SECOND Sustain step, a small MOVE further along the dashed last-gap path revealed in Chapter 26,
from Chapter 27's position toward the goal; this is NOT a milestone, and honestly small, because you solved
one recurring problem, not all of them at once, but it is real, because solving a recurring problem changes
something permanent. Five of the six S's were complete going in; the goal is reached only at the very end of
the book, at Chapter 30. Separator discipline: labels use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 28 · SUSTAIN`,
never a floating hyphen or a floating dot between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen:
problem-solving, why-not-who, solve-together, fixing-versus-solving, no-blame, two-minute, five-minute,
path-of-least-resistance, root-cause, character-flaw, design-flaw, keeping-machinery, last-gap, three-tooth,
anti-backslide, no-backslide, Sort-complete, Straighten-complete, Shine-complete, Safety-complete,
Standardize-complete. Anti-shame throughout: nobody is the problem, the coats are not on the chair because
someone is lazy, they are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place; the tired figure in the
fixing loop is weary, not incompetent, and the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character
flaw, which is good news, because you cannot easily change a person but you can nearly always change a system.
No force, control, or blame framing anywhere (no crack down, no hold people accountable in the punitive sense,
no naming a culprit, no verdict handed down), no hype (no unlock, leverage, elevate, supercharge), no
corporate or boardroom register, no brand names. The register is calm, warm, human, homely, and collaborative,
the relief of an exhausting loop simply ending: when something keeps going wrong you do not look for someone to
blame, you look, together, for the small system to fix.

## 1. The Same Coats, Two Futures (the opener)

**Purpose:** Show the chapter's whole argument in one image. The identical small problem, coats landing on a
chair by the door, drawn twice, so the only variable the eye can read is the household's relationship to it:
fixing the symptom forever, or solving the cause once. On the LEFT, fixing: a single tired figure caught in an
endless loop, hanging the coats up while they keep returning to the chair, a loop with no end. On the RIGHT,
solving: the same household together adds a row of hooks by the door, once, and then, in a final calm panel,
the coats simply hang on the hooks, the chair clear, the loop broken. Same problem, two completely different
relationships to it. Mirrors Figure 1 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** The same coats-on-a-chair problem, drawn twice. The LEFT scene, `FIXING`, shows a single
tired figure caught in a loop drawn as a treadmill or hamster wheel of motion arrows: they hang the coats on a
distant closet rail, turn around, and the coats are already back on the chair, so they hang them again, and
again, going nowhere, the same weary act repeating. The RIGHT scene, `SOLVING`, shows the same household
gathered together for one moment, fixing a small row of hooks to the wall right beside the door; and then, in
a final calm panel, the coats hang on the new hooks by themselves, the chair clear, the loop broken, everyone
relaxed.

**Rough layout:**
- A soft warm wash on paper cream `#F7F2E9` falling into panel `#FBF7EF`. A faint Newsreader italic line,
  muted `#6A625A`: `the same coats, two futures`.
- LEFT scene eyebrow, cool slate sans caps: `FIXING`. The tired figure in the hamster-wheel loop of motion
  arrows, the coats reappearing on the chair as fast as they are hung, a weary but patient expression, the
  whole scene a loop with no end, with a Newsreader italic line beneath in slate: `hanging them up over and
  over while they keep returning to the chair, a loop with no end`.
- RIGHT scene eyebrow, warm terracotta sans caps: `SOLVING`. The same household together fixing a row of
  hooks by the door, once, then a final calm panel with the coats on the hooks and the chair clear, the loop
  broken, with a Newsreader italic line beneath in green: `the household adds a row of hooks by the door,
  once, together, and the problem simply stops`.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter): "The same coats, two futures. On the left, fixing: hanging them up over
  and over while they keep returning to the chair, a loop with no end. On the right, solving: the household
  adds a row of hooks by the door, once, together, and the problem simply stops. Fixing puts the coats back.
  Solving changes why they fell."

**Note for the illustrator:** the point is that the identical problem can be met two completely different
ways, so draw the two scenes unmistakably the same coats, the same chair, the same door, only the household's
relationship to it different. Keep the fixing figure tired but never incompetent or foolish: they are a weary
human doing the sensible short-term thing over and over, not a failure; the loop is the villain, not the
person. Keep the solving scene warm and collaborative, the whole household gathered, the fix small and
ordinary, the final panel calm and settled. Hold the color discipline: cool slate for the fixing loop, warm
terracotta and green for the solving side, one honey accent held to the new hooks or the fix; spark-red barely
appears if at all. Warm line-and-flat-color style, the register of a before-and-after that turns entirely on
whether the household patches the symptom forever or changes the little system once.

## 2. The Ladder of Whys (the root-cause tool, an infographic)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's core tool as one picture: asking why a few times walks you down from a
surface symptom you might blame someone for to a root cause you can actually change, and points straight at a
fix. The value is in the short drilling ladder and the small side panel that contrasts the useful question
(why?) with the dead-end one (who?). Mirrors Figure 2 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A short vertical ladder or staircase of about four rungs, drilling from a surface symptom
down to a root cause and then out to a fix, using the running coats example. The top rung, in slate, reads
`Coats on the chair (again)`. A small `why?` leads down to the next rung, `The chair is the easiest spot`.
Another `why?` down to `The hooks are down the hall`. Another `why?` down to the bottom rung, the real cause,
`Tired people take the shortest path`. From the bottom rung, a warm arrow points out to a highlighted box,
`Fix: add hooks right by the door`. To one side, a small contrast panel shows the two questions: `why?` with a
green tick, a system, and `who?` with a gentle grey cross, a person, and nowhere.

**Rough layout:**
- A calm panel background `#FBF7EF`, warm and homely. Top, a small sans caps label, letter-spaced, soft: `THE
  LADDER OF WHYS`.
- A short slate `#3C5A6B` ladder of four rungs descending, each rung a Fraunces line, with a small `why?` set
  between rungs:
  - top rung: `Coats on the chair (again)`
  - `why?` down to: `The chair is the easiest spot`
  - `why?` down to: `The hooks are down the hall`
  - `why?` down to the bottom rung, the real cause: `Tired people take the shortest path`
- From the bottom rung, a warm terracotta arrow out to a highlighted honey `#DDA63A` box: `Fix: add hooks
  right by the door`.
- To one side, a small contrast panel: `why?` with a green `#6E8B5B` tick and a tiny italic note `a system,
  which you can change`, and `who?` with a gentle grey cross and a tiny italic note `a person, and nowhere`.
- A line across the foot in Fraunces slate: `ask why a few times, and the symptom walks you down to the cause`.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter figure): "Asking why a few times walks you down from the symptom to the
  cause. The coats on the chair lead, why by why, to tired people taking the shortest path, which points
  straight at a fix you can actually make: hooks by the door. The other question, who, only ever leads to a
  person, and a person is not the thing that needs changing."

**Note for the illustrator:** the message is that a few gentle whys walk you from a symptom to a cause you can
change, so draw the ladder short and the descent easy, the way a curious child would ask, never an
interrogation. Keep the rungs plain and readable, the fix box warm and highlighted at the foot, and the
side panel clear: the green tick on why leads to a system, the grey cross on who leads only to a person. Keep
the grey cross gentle, never harsh; who is not wicked, it is simply useless, because a person is not the thing
that needs changing. Hold the color discipline: slate for the rungs and the ladder, green for the why tick,
one honey accent held to the fix box, grey for the dead-end who; spark-red does not appear. Warm
line-and-flat-color style, the register of a plain, friendly tool that turns blame into a fix.

## 3. Solve It Together (the four-step method, an infographic)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's method as one picture: the four small steps run together, as a warm,
five-minute family activity rather than a formal procedure, so the "together" is the visible heart of it. The
value is in how much it looks like a family sorting something out, because that is exactly what it is. Mirrors
Figure 3 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A warm illustration of a household of three or four gathered loosely around a table or a
doorway, working out one recurring problem together, organized around the four steps of the method as four
labelled stages. Stage 1, `Name it`, a small speech bubble reading `the coats keep landing on the chair`,
stated as a problem, not a person. Stage 2, `Ask why, together`, the little family exchanging `why?` bubbles,
a child among them clearly contributing. Stage 3, `Change the system`, a hand fixing a row of hooks by the
door. Stage 4, `Decide together`, everyone nodding at the shared fix, a child hammering in one hook. A short
line across the foot reads `the people who live with it help decide, so they keep it`.

**Rough layout:**

  `SOLVE IT TOGETHER`.

  - `NAME IT`, a small speech bubble: `the coats keep landing on the chair`, italic note `a problem, not a
    person`.
  - `ASK WHY, TOGETHER`, the family exchanging `why?` bubbles, a child contributing, italic note `a few
    times, until you reach the cause`.
  - `CHANGE THE SYSTEM`, a hand fixing a row of hooks by the door, italic note `so the cause is gone`.

- A line across the foot in Fraunces slate: `the people who live with it help decide, so they keep it`.

**Note for the illustrator:** the whole point is that the "together" is the heart of the method, so keep the
family gathered and loose, everyone included, the children visibly contributing rather than watching. Keep the
four stages clean and equal in weight, one small vignette each, warm and collaborative, never a formal flow

to the shared fix, green available for a small tick of agreement; spark-red does not appear. This is not a
process diagram: it is a warm scene of a household solving one small thing, drawn so the reader feels the

sorting something out together.

## 4. Mopping the Floor, Finding the Leak (the fixing-versus-solving move, no direct HTML twin)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's core distinction as a picture: fixing corrects the symptom and is completely
temporary, because it does nothing about whatever produces the symptom; solving looks past the symptom to the
cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. This diagram has no direct HTML figure twin; it
renders the fixing-versus-solving reframe as a calm side-by-side, so the reader feels that mopping the same
square of floor every day is not the answer, and that the recurring problems the check-in keeps surfacing are
exactly that same square of floor, asking the household to finally look up and find the pipe.

**What it shows:** Two quiet panels, read left to right. The LEFT panel, mopping: a patient figure mopping the
same small square of floor, a little puddle already forming again as they mop, the task necessary and quick
and completely temporary, the leak overhead still dripping, untouched and out of frame of their attention. The
RIGHT panel, finding the leak: the same figure, calm, looking up at last and reaching the pipe above, the
drip stopped at its source, the floor able to stay dry on its own now. A gentle arrow crosses from mopping to
finding-the-leak. Beneath, a small strip carries the distinction, and to one side a dim, unlit panel names
Daily 6S Times of Chapter 29 as next, the rhythm that carries all this keeping through the ordinary shape of a
day.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: sans caps label, soft, `MOPPING THE FLOOR, FINDING THE LEAK`, with a Fraunces ink line beneath
  carrying the chapter's spine: "Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak."
- Two cream `#FBF7EF` panels with rule `#E2D8C4` borders, joined by a gentle slate arrow:
  - `MOPPING` (left, patient but endless): the figure mopping the same square, a fresh puddle forming as they
    work, a muted note `necessary, quick, and completely temporary`, the leak overhead dripping, untouched.
  - `FINDING THE LEAK` (right, calm and settled): the same figure looking up and reaching the pipe, the drip
    stopped at its source, a small green sparkle, a note `the cause changed once, so the symptom stops being
    produced`.
- Beneath, a small slate-bordered strip carrying the fixing-versus-solving spine in ink: "Fixing puts the
  thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again."
- To the right or below, a dimmer honey-outlined panel, unlit, tagged `DAILY 6S TIMES · NEXT` in muted honey
  sans caps, a muted italic note beneath, `the rhythm that folds all this keeping into the ordinary shape of a
  day, so the small acts of upkeep happen at set, natural moments rather than depending on anyone remembering`,
  held quiet and clearly one step ahead, not yet reached.
- Footer, Newsreader italic, slate: "Mopping is right for a spill; but if you find yourself mopping the same
  square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows more mopping is not the answer."

**Note for the illustrator:** this is the chapter's core distinction, so keep it calm and plain, never
scolding; the point is not that mopping is wrong but that it is the wrong tool for a leak, and a recurring
problem is a leak. Draw the mopping panel patient and endless, the puddle forming again as fast as it is
wiped, the leak overhead untouched; draw the finding-the-leak panel calm and settled, the figure simply
looking up and reaching the source. Hold the color discipline: slate for the arrow and the sub-strip, green
for the settled sparkle, one muted honey for the dim next-chapter panel, spark-red held back; no blame, no
scolding, no failure. Keep the next-chapter panel dim, unlit, and clearly one step ahead; you may name Daily
6S Times of Chapter 29 as next, but teach nothing of it. The register is calm relief, not judgment: mopping
the same square forever is exhausting, and finding the leak is simply what ends it. Warm line-and-flat-color
style.
