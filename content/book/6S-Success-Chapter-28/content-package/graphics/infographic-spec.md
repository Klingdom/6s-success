# Infographic Spec: Family Problem Solving

**Chapter 28, "Family Problem Solving"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Eight · Sustain**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: a problem that
keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct, it is a signal that a small system is wrong, so
you stop fixing the symptom forever and solve the cause once, together. The concept pairs the hero rule,
**When the same problem keeps coming back, stop fixing it and start solving it: ask why it happens, not who is
to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again.**, with the picture at its heart: **the same
coats, two futures**, the same coats-on-a-chair problem drawn twice, on the left a tired figure caught in an
endless loop of fixing while the coats keep returning, on the right the household together adding a row of
hooks by the door, once, and a final calm panel with the coats on the hooks, the chair clear, the loop broken.
So the viewer sees the whole move at once, that fixing puts the coats back and solving changes why they fell,
and that nobody needs to change, only the little system does.

It stays consistent with the four inline figures in `chapter_28_final.html`. The two sides are unmistakably
the same coats and chair; cool slate carries the endless fixing loop, warm terracotta and soft green carry the
solving side, and one honey accent is held to the new hooks so the eye reads the two futures as the same
problem; the single small warm red is held back for the smallest marks only, because this is a gift of
problem-solving, not a hazard. "Blame" is not the point, and neither is "who"; a household that asks why and
solves together is warmer and lighter, a family sorting something out rather than a tribunal. Anti-shame
throughout: nobody is the problem, the coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy, they are on the
chair because the hooks are in the wrong place, and the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a
character flaw, which is good news, because you cannot easily change a person but you can nearly always change
a system. The register is warm and collaborative, never corporate, never blaming; when something keeps going
wrong you do not look for someone to blame, you look, together, for the small system to fix.

## Layout notes

- The rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the whole chapter compressed: ask why it happens, not
  who is to blame, and change the system so it cannot easily happen again. The eye should read the rule, then
  see the same coats, two futures make it real, the left side an endless loop of fixing the symptom, the right
  side the household solving the cause once, together.
- The same coats, two futures is the hero, so make the two sides unmistakably the same problem: the same
  coats, the same chair, the same door, only the household's relationship to it different. Keep the fixing
  figure tired but never incompetent or foolish, a weary human doing the sensible short-term thing over and
  over, the loop the villain and not the person, and the solving side warm and collaborative, the whole
  household gathered, the final panel calm. Set the rule, the definition, and the four-step method
  byte-for-byte as they read in the chapter; they are the frozen strings.
- Hold the color discipline: cool slate carries the endless fixing loop, warm terracotta and green carry the
  solving side, one honey accent is held to the new hooks so the two futures read as one problem; the single
  small warm red is reserved for the smallest marks and is barely used, because this image is a gift of
  problem-solving and not a live hazard.
- This image makes the concept, the reframe that opens family problem-solving: a recurring problem is a small
  system to solve, not a person to blame. Do NOT re-teach the first five S's, do NOT re-teach the keeping
  mindset of Chapter 26 or the friendly check-in of Chapter 27 (used but not re-explained), and do NOT build
  the later Sustain mechanisms, the daily rhythm (Chapter 29) and the recurring event (Chapter 30), which are
  named only as still to come. And name no brand and draw no specific product; the coats, the chair, the door,
  and the hooks are plain and generic, teaching the idea, not a shopping list.
- Anti-shame: nobody is the problem, the coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy, they are on the
  chair because the hooks are in the wrong place, and the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a
  character flaw, which is good news, because you cannot easily change a person but you can nearly always
  change a system. What you are building is not a search for a culprit, it is a shared way to change a little
  system. Keep the register warm and collaborative, a family sorting something out and not a tribunal; no
  force, control, or blame language (no crack down, no hold people accountable in the punitive sense, no
  naming a culprit, no verdict handed down), no punitive judgment presented as virtue, no cage, no hype. Use
  who ONLY as the useless question the useful one replaces.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the two sides drawn by hand as
  the same small home scene rather than clinical thumbnails, the fixing figure weary but human and the solving
  household warm and gathered, the new hooks a small honey accent, the whole image the calm, warm register of
  an exhausting loop simply ending once the little system changes.
