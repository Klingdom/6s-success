# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 28

**"Family Problem Solving"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Eight · Sustain*

Four prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to hold across
all four: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`, terracotta `#BC4B2A` (the
warm accent, the solving side, the Sustain-step tag), honey `#DDA63A` (the single accent, the fix), slate blue
`#3C5A6B` (data, rungs, milestones, the catch, the greyed "who" question), soft green `#6E8B5B` (the "why"
tick, the last-gap path, the goal), a single warm red `#CB4B36` reserved for the smallest marks only, the
no-backslide bar, used barely if at all on these calm images. Natural soft daylight, shallow but not showy
depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the finished home the reader has cleared, arranged, cleaned, made safe, and
standardized. This chapter, the THIRD of Part Eight, CONTINUES Sustain, the sixth and final S, and adds the
second piece of keeping-machinery on top of Chapter 27's friendly audit: where the audit finds the recurring
problems, this chapter solves them, together. Its whole work is a reframe: a problem that keeps coming back is
not a slip anyone is failing to correct, it is a signal that a small system is wrong, and a wrong system
cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor
more diligently, so you stop patching the symptom and solve the cause, once, together. It draws the
fixing-versus-solving distinction (a problem you fix more than twice is one you are failing to solve; fixing
puts the thing back, solving changes why it fell) and delivers the method's heart, ask why and never who (who
finds a person to blame and changes nothing, why finds a system to fix and changes everything; ask why a few
times, the way a curious child would, until you reach the real cause, which is almost always a design flaw,
not a character flaw). It delivers the discipline of solving together (the person who lives with the problem
knows why it happens and has to keep the fix alive, so they should help design it; a solution handed down is
dropped, a solution worked out together is kept) and walks the four-step method end to end on the coats that
always land on the chair (name it; ask why together; change the small system; decide together). Nothing of the
first five S's is re-taught, the friendly check-in of Chapter 27 is used but not re-explained, and none of the
later Sustain mechanisms are built (the daily rhythm of Chapter 29 and the recurring event of Chapter 30 are
named only as still to come). Keep the tone calm, warm, and collaborative: nobody is the problem, the coats
are not on the chair because someone is lazy, they are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place,
and the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw, which is good news, because you
cannot easily change a person but you can nearly always change a system. When something keeps going wrong you
do not look for someone to blame, you look, together, for the small system to fix. Crucially, describe only
the calm, warm illustrated house style: no corporate or boardroom look, no force, control, or blame framing
(no crack down, no hold people accountable in the punitive sense, no naming a culprit, no verdict handed
down), no hype (no unlock, leverage, elevate, supercharge), no photoreal polish; the tired figure in the
fixing loop is weary and human, never incompetent, and the solving is small, ordinary, warm, and shared.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical object in
the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real scrap, a hand-drawn mark on a real ladder rung,
or the reading on a real gauge, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental
hand-lettered label: use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 28 · SUSTAIN`, never a floating hyphen or a floating
dot between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: problem-solving, why-not-who, solve-together,
fixing-versus-solving, no-blame, two-minute, five-minute, path-of-least-resistance, root-cause,
design-flaw, character-flaw, last-gap, three-tooth, anti-backslide, no-backslide, Sort-complete,
Straighten-complete, Shine-complete, Safety-complete, Standardize-complete.

## 1. The same coats, two futures (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of the same small home problem, coats landing on a chair by the door,
drawn twice side by side in soft daylight, to show that the identical problem can be met two completely
different ways. On the left side, labelled fixing, a single tired figure is caught in an endless loop, drawn
as a treadmill or hamster wheel of soft motion arrows: they hang the coats on a distant closet rail, turn
around, and the coats are already back on the chair, so they hang them again, and again, going nowhere, the
same weary act repeating with a patient, tired expression. On the right side, labelled solving, the same
household is gathered together for one moment, fixing a small row of hooks to the wall right beside the door;
and then, in a final calm panel, the coats simply hang on the new hooks by themselves, the chair clear, the
loop broken, everyone relaxed. The two sides are unmistakably the same coats, the same chair, the same door,
so the only difference the eye reads is the household's relationship to the problem, patching the symptom
forever or changing the little system once. Warm paper cream, ink-brown line work, cool slate for the fixing
loop, warm terracotta and soft green for the solving side, one honey accent on the new hooks, warm-red held
back. Editorial storybook illustration. The same coats, two futures.

- Mood: fixing puts the coats back and solving changes why they fell; on the left, hanging them up over and
  over while they keep returning to the chair, a loop with no end; on the right, the household adds a row of
  hooks by the door, once, together, and the problem simply stops; the register is warm and human, the tired
  figure weary but never incompetent, the solving scene collaborative and calm.
- Avoid: a foolish, lazy, or shamed figure in the fixing loop, anyone being blamed, a corporate or boardroom
  look, floating typeset text overlays, a menacing or scolding tone, warm-red anywhere except the smallest
  marks, brand names or recognizable products; keep the two sides plainly the same coats and chair, the left a
  weary loop with no end, the right the household adding hooks together once and the final panel calm with the
  chair clear and the loop broken.

## 2. The ladder of whys (the root-cause tool)

A warm editorial illustration of a hand-made infographic titled the ladder of whys, on a soft cream panel, in
soft daylight, showing a short vertical ladder or staircase of about four rungs that drills from a surface
symptom down to a root cause and then out to a fix, using the running coats example. The top rung, hand-drawn
in slate, reads coats on the chair (again). A small hand-lettered why leads down to the next rung, the chair
is the easiest spot. Another why leads down to the hooks are down the hall. Another why leads down to the
bottom rung, the real cause, tired people take the shortest path. From the bottom rung, a warm hand-drawn
arrow points out to a highlighted honey box reading fix, add hooks right by the door. To one side, a small
contrast panel shows the two questions: why with a small soft-green tick, a system you can change, and who
with a gentle grey cross, a person, and nowhere. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, slate for
the rungs and the ladder, soft green for the why tick, one honey accent held to the fix box, a gentle grey for
the dead-end who, no warm-red on this calm image. A picture of one plain idea: asking why a few times walks
you from a symptom you might blame someone for to a cause you can actually change.

- Mood: ask why a few times, the way a curious child would, and the symptom walks you down to the cause; the
  real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw, a missing hook or an awkward distance, which
  is good news because you can nearly always change a system; who only ever leads to a person, and a person is
  not the thing that needs changing; the register is calm, friendly, and reassuring.
- Avoid: an interrogation or a harsh grilling, a harsh or accusing grey cross, a scoreboard, gridlines
  everywhere, neon color, a scolding tone, floating typeset text, any warm-red, brand names; keep the ladder
  short and the descent gentle, the fix box warm and highlighted at the foot, the side panel clear with a soft
  green tick on why and a gentle grey cross on who, the whole thing hand-made and friendly but not childish.

## 3. Solve it together (the four-step method)

A warm, homely editorial illustration of a household of three or four gathered loosely around a table or a
doorway, working out one recurring problem together, on a soft cream panel, in soft daylight, organized around
the four steps of the method as four hand-drawn labelled stages. Stage one, name it, shows a small speech
bubble reading the coats keep landing on the chair, stated as a problem, not a person. Stage two, ask why
together, shows the little family exchanging small why bubbles, a child among them clearly contributing. Stage
three, change the system, shows a hand fixing a row of hooks by the door. Stage four, decide together, shows
everyone nodding at the shared fix, a child hammering in one hook. A hand-lettered line across the foot reads
the people who live with it help decide, so they keep it. It is unmistakably a warm family sorting something
out together, not a boardroom procedure. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, slate for the stage
labels, one honey accent held to the shared fix, soft green available for a small tick of agreement, no
warm-red anywhere. A picture of one plain idea: the whole method is a warm, shared, five-minute family
activity, and the together is the heart of it.

- Mood: the whole method run together in a few minutes, name the problem, ask why until you reach the cause,
  change the small system, and decide the fix together so everyone keeps it; it looks like a family sorting
  something out because that is exactly what it is; the children are often startlingly good at this; the
  register is warm, collaborative, and homely.
- Avoid: a cold corporate flow chart, a formal procedure, a boardroom, gridlines everywhere, neon color, a
  parent lecturing while others watch, floating typeset text, any warm-red, brand names; keep the family
  gathered and loose with everyone included and the children visibly contributing, the four stages clean and
  equal in weight with one small vignette each, the whole thing hand-made and friendly but not childish.
