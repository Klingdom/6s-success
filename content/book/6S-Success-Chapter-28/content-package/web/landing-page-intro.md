# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 28, Family Problem Solving

You have the keeping mindset now, and you have the check-in. Chapter 26 gave you the heart of the sixth and final S: held is not the same as kept, so you do not finish 6S, you keep it, on small shared habits rather than willpower. Chapter 27 gave you the first concrete way to do that keeping, a regular, friendly check-in that walks the home and catches drift while it is still small, and feels like care rather than an inspection. This chapter adds the second piece, and it solves what the check-in keeps finding. Because the check-in is very good at catching drift while it is small, but every so often it turns up a more stubborn kind of finding: not a one-off slip, which is a two-minute fix, but the same problem drifting back in the same place, week after week. The same coats on the same chair. The same dishes by the sink. The same shoes in the same doorway. You keep fixing them, and they keep coming back, and a small, tired frustration builds. This chapter is the reframe that dissolves it.

Here is the whole of it. A problem that keeps coming back is not a slip anyone is failing to correct. It is a signal that a small system is wrong. And a wrong system cannot be fixed by correcting it more often, any more than a leaking pipe can be fixed by mopping the floor more diligently. It has to be solved, at the cause, once. That single reframe takes the exhaustion out of the recurring problem, because it turns out you were never failing at something simple; you were patching a symptom while the cause sat untouched, fully in place, ready to do the same thing again tonight.

So the chapter draws the distinction that most advice skips: fixing is not solving. Fixing is correcting the symptom, the coats are on the chair so you hang them up, and it is necessary, quick, and completely temporary, because it does nothing about whatever put the coats there. Solving looks past the symptom to the cause and changes that, so the symptom stops being produced. Fixing is mopping the floor; solving is finding the leak. And if you find yourself mopping the same square of floor every single day, some part of you already knows that more mopping is not the answer. A problem you have to fix more than twice is not a problem you are failing to fix; it is a problem you are failing to solve. Fixing puts the thing back; solving changes why it fell in the first place, so that you never have to put it back again.

Two things make solving work, more than any technique. The first is the question you ask. Ask who keeps dumping their coat on the chair, and you get a defensive family member, a small argument, and a coat that is back tomorrow. Ask why the coats keep landing there, and you are suddenly looking at the real thing: the chair right by the door, the hooks down the hall, and every tired person taking the sensible, path-of-least-resistance route. When something keeps going wrong, the useless question is who keeps doing it, and the useful one is why it keeps happening. Who finds a person to blame and changes nothing; why finds a system to fix and changes everything. The coats are not on the chair because someone is lazy. They are on the chair because the hooks are in the wrong place. You get from symptom to cause by gently asking why a few times, the way a curious child would, and you notice something reassuring: the real cause is almost always a design flaw, not a character flaw, a missing hook, an awkward distance, a bin too far, a shelf with no label, a thing with no home. That is genuinely good news, because you cannot easily change a person, but you can nearly always change a system.

The second thing that makes solving work is who does it. What decides whether a solution survives is not how clever it is, but who came up with it. The person who lives with a problem is the one who knows why it really happens, and the one who has to keep any fix alive, so they are exactly who should help design it. A solution handed down is resented and quietly dropped; a solution the household worked out together is one it actually keeps, because people keep what they helped build and abandon what was dropped on them from above. So you bring the recurring problem to the household, out loud, as a shared puzzle rather than a verdict, and you let everyone, including the children, who are often startlingly good at this, propose the fix.

The method is just four small steps, done together, and the chapter walks them end to end on the coats. Name the problem plainly, as a problem and not a person: the coats keep ending up on the chair. Ask why, together, a few times, until you reach a cause everyone recognizes: the hooks are down the hall, the chair is right there, tired people take the shortest path. Change the small system to remove that cause: a row of hooks goes up on the wall right beside the door, so now the shortest path and the right path are the same path. And decide together, so the fix is theirs: because a child picked where the hooks went and got to hammer one in, they use it, and the problem that returned every week for a year quietly stops returning at all.
