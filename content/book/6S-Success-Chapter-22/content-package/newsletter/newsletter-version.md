# Newsletter Edition: Improve Overall Safety

**Subject line (placeholder):** See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it.

**Preview text (placeholder):** The whole of Safety in four moves, and you now have them all.

---

Hi friend,

Last issue you ran the hazard hunt: a calm, room-by-room walk that carried the same five questions into every space and closed the gap between finding a danger and removing it to nothing. It was the thorough version of the first pass, and it did real work. Hold on to that, because this issue completes the fourth S, and it does so by being honest about what a hunt, however thorough, can and cannot do. Here are the two facts it rests on, said gently. A home does not hold still: the hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks. New cords arrive, new heights appear, batteries quietly die. And some harm cannot be removed in advance at all: a fire can still start, a person can still fall. Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach.

So this chapter does not hand you a longer list of hazards to chase. It raises the whole home's floor. Start with the reframe the chapter rests on.

**You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet.**

Think of it as the difference between bailing and fixing the boat. Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming. Raising the baseline changes the boat itself, so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. It just matters less once the floor is higher. Raising that floor has two halves, and the whole of this issue is those two halves: prevent the next hazard from taking hold, and prepare for the harm no prevention can stop.

The first half is prevention. You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. What works is design.

**A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way.**

You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing. In practice it is three small habits, none of them a schedule to write down and enforce. That documented standard is a later S.

**Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in.**

Choose the safer default at the one moment it is easiest, when the thing arrives: the cord routed along the wall the day the lamp comes home, not three years later. Give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place. And run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, so drift is caught while it is still small. That is prevention: quiet, cheap, and mostly invisible.

The second half is preparation, and it is the emotional heart of the whole chapter. Because however well you prevent, a fire can start in a wall you cannot see, and a person can fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor. You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps.

**Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes.**

Preparation is short and finite, not a bunker. A prepared home.

**Put the protections in place for the harm you cannot prevent: working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows.**

The alarm is the center of it, because it is the one protection that has to keep working while you are asleep, and its whole value depends on being tested and kept alive. A fire extinguisher within reach of the kitchen. A way out of every room and an exit plan the household has actually walked, from the bedrooms to one meeting spot outside. And the few plain numbers and steps everyone should know, the children included, so the knowledge does not live in one head.

**To try this week:** Test every smoke and carbon-monoxide alarm in the home right now, one press each, and note any floor that has none. It takes a minute, and it is the highest-value protection in the house. Then, on a calm evening, walk the exit plan together once: from each bedroom, how do you get out, and where does everyone meet outside. Children who have walked it once remember it, and walking it turns a frightening idea into a known, ordinary thing.

Now the whole of Safety is in your hands, and it is four moves. See the hazard, in Chapter 20. Remove it, in Chapter 21. Prevent the next one and prepare for the harm you cannot stop, here in Chapter 22. Each is the answer to a question the one before it could not ask.

**See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it.**

That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down. And it is the opposite of vigilance, not fear held at a high pitch, but a steady, low, permanent habit of care that mostly runs quietly in the background of an ordinary, happy home.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because the needle reaches a marked milestone: Safety complete, the fourth mark on the dial, beside Sort complete, Straighten complete, and Shine complete. Four of the six S's are done. The home is cleared, arranged, kept clean, and now safe: today's hazards gone, new ones kept from taking hold, and the worst days prepared for. And notice the honest size of the step. It is small, and that is true, because the big daily friction was cleared long ago in the first three S's. What these three safety chapters removed was not the friction of ordinary days but the friction of the worst day, which is quiet on a dial that mostly measures ordinary ones, and one of the most valuable things in the whole book. The mark is a true arrival.

The goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead, because the needle reaches it only when everything is not just built but made to last, and two S's remain to do that. So take a moment to look back across Part Six. In Chapter 20 you learned to see the hazards, putting on the safety lens for the first time. In Chapter 21 you removed them, room by room, the hidden many along with the obvious few. And here in Chapter 22 you made the home keep itself safe: prevention against the next hazard, preparation for the harm you cannot stop. For the first nineteen chapters you worked on the state of the room; in these three you turned to the people in it, which was always the more important thing. What is left is not more building; it is holding.

**Finding hazards makes a home safer once. Preventing them and preparing for them keep it safe for good.**
