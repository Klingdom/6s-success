## 1. A home does not hold still

You can see the hazards in your home, and you can remove them, room by room. Between them those two skills make a home meaningfully safer.

But a home does not hold still. The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks. A new cord, a child grown two inches, a dead battery in an alarm you fitted years ago.

And some harm cannot be removed in advance at all. A fire can still start. A person can still fall.

Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach.

So Safety has one more move. Two, really: prevent, and prepare.

## 2. Stop chasing hazards. Raise the floor.

There are two ways to keep a home safe, and only one of them scales.

You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet.

Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming. Raising the baseline changes the boat itself, so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does.

You still bail now and then. But once the floor is higher, it matters less.

## 3. Prevention is design, not willpower

You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater.

A home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way.

That is prevention, and it is three small habits. Choose the safer default when something new arrives, at the one moment it is easiest. Give the dangerous things a home, so away and safe are the same place. And re-hunt lightly, now and then, so drift is caught while it is small.

Not a schedule to enforce. Just the safe way, made the easy way.

## 4. Make the safe way the easy way

Here is the whole of prevention in one line.

Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in.

That is it. Not vigilance. Not resolving to be more careful. You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing.

A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place.

The one moment it is easiest to choose safe is the moment a thing arrives. Choose it then.

## 5. Some harm you cannot prevent

However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach. A fire in a wall you cannot see. Carbon monoxide with no warning your body can catch. A fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor.

You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps.

What you can do is prepare. And preparation is a short, concrete list, not a state of dread: working alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows.

Not a bunker. A prepared home.

## 6. Preparation is love

This is the gentlest idea in the whole book, and the one I most want you to keep.

Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes.

That is what the smoke alarm is. What the walked exit plan is. What the flashlight on a known shelf is. Not fear. Readiness, done once, quietly, on an ordinary calm afternoon, so no one has to invent it in the dark.

## 7. Test every alarm right now

Here is the smallest move in the chapter, and the highest-value one.

Test every smoke and carbon-monoxide alarm in the home right now, one press each, and note any floor that has none.

Working alarms are the highest-value protection in the house, and the only one that has to keep working while you are asleep. Their whole value depends on being kept alive. An alarm you fit once and never test again is only decoration, silent the night it matters.

So give the button a press. Do it whenever the clocks change, and you will never have to remember.

One minute now, for the one protection that works while you are unconscious.

## 8. Walk the exit once

A fire escape plan is not a document. It is a walk.

Walk it together, calmly, once. From each bedroom, how do you get out, and where does everyone meet outside? A way out of every room, ideally two, and one meeting spot the whole household knows.

Children who have walked it once remember it, and walking it turns a frightening idea into a known, ordinary thing. That is the point: you do the scary imagining once, on a calm afternoon, so no one has to do it in the moment.

It costs a quarter hour. It buys a certainty you keep for years.

## 9. Safety is four moves

Step back and look at the fourth S whole, because it turns out to have been four moves all along.

See the hazard, by asking "could this hurt someone?". Remove it, thoroughly, room by room. Prevent the next one, by making the safe way the easy way. And prepare for the harm none of that can stop.

See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it. That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down.

A safe home is not one where nothing can ever go wrong. It is one that is ready when it does.
