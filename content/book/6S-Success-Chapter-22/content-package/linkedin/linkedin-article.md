# You Cleared the Hazards. A Home Does Not Hold Still, and Some Harm You Cannot Prevent, Only Prepare For.

You can now do two things you could not do when this part of the book began. You can see the hazards in your home, because you have the safety lens. And you can remove them thoroughly, room by room, because you have the hunt. Between them, those two skills have made your home meaningfully safer, and if you stopped here you would have done real good.

But there is a reason Safety has one more chapter, and it is a pair of honest facts. The first is that a home does not hold still. The hunt was a snapshot, and after it the day ended and things began quietly to flow back: a new cord, a child grown two inches, dead batteries. The second is harder and gentler. Some harm cannot be removed in advance at all. You can clear every hazard you can find, and a fire can still start, a person can still fall.

> The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks.

> Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach.

So this chapter completes the fourth S by adding the two moves a hazard hunt cannot make: keep new dangers from taking hold, and be ready for the harm you cannot prevent. See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it.

## From removing to improving

Everything so far in this part has been subtraction: find a danger, take it out. That is the right place to start and not the place to stop, because subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever. Think of the difference between bailing a boat and fixing the leak.

> Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming.

Improving overall safety changes the boat itself, so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the home's baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less.

> You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet.

Raising the baseline has two halves, and the rest of the chapter is those two halves. One looks forward and keeps new hazards from taking hold, which is prevention. The other gets the home ready to meet the harm no prevention can stop, which is preparation.

## Prevent: keep new hazards from taking hold

A hunted home drifts back toward hazard, not because anyone is careless but because ordinary life brings new things in, and some are dangerous, and they do not announce themselves. The whole of prevention is making the safe way the easy way, which is the opposite of willpower. You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. What works is design.

> A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way.

In practice that is three small habits. First, choose the safer default when something new comes into the house, at the one moment it is easiest, which is the moment it arrives. Second, give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place. Third, run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, a slow walk with the five questions, so drift is caught while it is small.

> Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in.

None of this is a schedule to write down and enforce. That documented standard is a later S.

## Prepare: ready for what you cannot prevent

This is the harder, gentler half, and it is not about hazards at all. However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach: a fire in a wall you cannot see, carbon monoxide with no warning your body can catch, a fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor.

> You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps.

What you can do is prepare, and preparation is a short and concrete list, not a state of dread. The center is the alarm, the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious. A working smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where anything burns fuel, on every floor and near the bedrooms, is the single highest-value thing in the chapter, and its value depends on being tested and kept alive.

> Put the protections in place for the harm you cannot prevent: working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows.

Beyond the alarm: a fire extinguisher within reach of the kitchen and a rough sense of how to use it; a way out of every room, ideally two, and an exit plan the household has actually walked, to one meeting spot outside; and the few plain numbers and steps everyone should know, including the children. Not a bunker. A prepared home.

> Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes.

## The whole of Safety

Step back and look at the fourth S whole, because you have just finished it, and it turns out to have been four moves all along, each the answer to a question the one before it could not ask. You learned to see the hazard, by changing the question from "is it tidy?" to "could this hurt someone?". You learned to remove it, thoroughly, room by room. You have now learned to prevent the next one, by making the safe way the easy way. And you have learned to prepare for the harm none of that can stop.

> That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down.

And notice what it is not: not the exhausting vigilance the last chapter warned against. Each move is work you do deliberately, once or now and then, precisely so you can stop watching. The professionals name the goal zero accidents and injuries; read it as a direction, not a destination you tick off. Being turned that way, with these four moves in hand, is what safety consciousness actually is: a steady, low, permanent habit of care running quietly in the background of an ordinary, happy home.

## The needle reaches Safety complete

Go and look at the friction meter, because it has reached a mark that has been a long time coming. The needle has moved to the fourth milestone on the dial, the one labeled Safety complete, and sits there now beside Sort complete, Straighten complete, and Shine complete. Four of the six S's are done. The home is cleared, arranged, kept clean on a rhythm, and now safe: its hazards gone, new ones kept from taking hold, and its worst days prepared for.

The step onto this mark was small, and that is honest, and it does not diminish the milestone. It was small because the big daily friction was cleared long ago in Sort and Straighten and Shine, and what these three safety chapters added was the removal not of everyday friction but of the friction of the worst day, quiet on a dial that mostly measures ordinary days, and one of the most valuable things in the whole book.

> Finding hazards makes a home safer once. Preventing them and preparing for them keep it safe for good.

The mark is a true arrival, the fourth S finished. The goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead, because the needle reaches it only at the very end, when everything is not just built but made to last. Two S's remain to do that.

## Safety, complete

That is the fourth S, done, and it is worth marking, because it was a turn in the book. For the first nineteen chapters you worked on the state of the room, and in these three you turned to the people in it, which was always the more important thing.

> What is left is not more building; it is holding.

Everything you have made lives, for now, largely in your head and your hands: it works because you know how it works and you keep it going. The last two S's change that, so the home holds its shape without leaning on any one person and keeps holding it over years.

> Safety is four moves, and you now have them all: see the hazard, remove it, prevent the next one, and prepare for the harm you cannot stop. A safe home is not one where nothing can ever go wrong. It is one that is ready when it does.

For now, the fourth S is complete, and your home is safe. Take the win.
