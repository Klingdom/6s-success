# 6S Success: Home Edition (Sales Page Copy)

## You found the hazards and removed them. A home does not hold still.

Last chapter you carried the five questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and removed each hazard the moment you found it: the button battery, the blind cord, the unanchored bookcase, the heater by the curtain. You made your home genuinely safer, and if you stopped there you would have done real good.

Here is the honest reason this part has one more chapter, said not to take that away but to finish it. It is a pair of facts about homes. The first is that a home does not hold still. A new appliance arrives with a new cord, a child grows two inches and reaches a shelf that was safe last month, batteries die. "The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks." The second is harder and gentler: some harm cannot be removed in advance at all. You can clear every hazard you can find and a fire can still start, a person can still fall. "Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach."

## Welcome to the part of Safety that completes it.

Chapter 22 is the third and final chapter of Part Six, and Safety completes here, the fourth of the six S's. Chapter 20 taught you to see hazards and Chapter 21 taught you to remove them. This chapter adds the two forward-looking moves a hazard hunt cannot make, so the home is not just cleared of today's dangers but genuinely, durably safe. It teaches no new way to tidy and re-teaches neither the lens nor the hunt. What it hands you is a higher baseline: the moves that keep new dangers from taking hold, and the protections that stand ready for the day something goes wrong anyway.

> Improving overall safety is the work beyond removing today's hazards: preventing new ones from taking hold, and preparing for the harm you cannot prevent. Removing hazards makes a home safe now. Prevention and preparation are what keep it safe.

## Stop chasing hazards one at a time. Raise the whole floor.

Everything so far in this part has been subtraction: find a danger, take it out. That is the right place to start and not the place to stop, because subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever. Think of the difference between bailing a boat and fixing the leak.

> You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet.

"Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming." Improving overall safety is the other kind of work, the kind that changes the boat itself so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the home's baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less. Raising the baseline has two halves, and the rest of the chapter is those two halves: prevention, and preparation.

## Prevent: make the safe way the easy way.

A hunted home drifts back toward hazard not because anyone is careless but because ordinary life brings new things in, and some of them are dangerous, and they do not announce themselves.

> A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way.

That last phrase is the whole of prevention, and it is the opposite of willpower. You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. What works is design. "You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing." In practice that is three small habits.

> Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in.

- **Choose the safer default** when something new comes into the house, at the one moment it is easiest, which is the moment it arrives
- **Give the dangerous things a home** · the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place
- **Re-hunt lightly, now and then** · a slow walk with the five questions, so drift is caught while it is small

None of this is a schedule to write down and enforce; that documented standard is a later S.

## Prepare: ready for what you cannot prevent.

The harder, gentler half is not about hazards at all. However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach: a fire in a wall you cannot see, carbon monoxide with no warning your body can catch, a fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor. "You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps." What you can do is prepare, and preparation is a short and concrete list, not a state of dread.

> Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes.

The center is the alarm, the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious.

> Put the protections in place for the harm you cannot prevent: working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows.

- **Working alarms, kept alive** · a smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where anything burns fuel, on every floor and near the bedrooms, tested and kept alive; the single highest-value thing in the chapter
- **A fire extinguisher within reach** · of the kitchen, and a rough sense of how to use it
- **A walked exit plan** · a way out of every room, ideally two, and a plan the household has actually walked, to one meeting spot outside
- **The few numbers and steps** · the plain ones everyone should know, including the children, so the knowledge does not live in one head

"Not a bunker. A prepared home."

## Not fear. Love, and the opposite of vigilance.

One word about how this is meant to feel, because the subject usually carries dread and this does not have to.

A home that has drifted, or that lacks a plan, is the normal case, and the fix is two calm moves, prevent and prepare, not guilt or fear. Preparation is not a state of high alarm; it is a short list you put in place once and keep alive, precisely so you can stop carrying the worry around. Each of the four moves is work you do deliberately, once or now and then, so you can stop watching. What it buys you is the opposite of vigilance: a home that is ready, so you do not have to be, on every ordinary happy day between now and the one you are preparing for.

## Keep the alarm alive.

One caution, so the one protection you are counting on is not silent the night it matters.

> Fitting a smoke alarm, feeling safe, and never testing it again, so the one protection you were counting on is silent the night it matters. A protection you do not keep alive is only decoration. Give the button a press whenever the clocks change.

A protection you do not keep alive is only decoration. The whole value of an alarm depends on being tested and kept alive, and it is the one thing on this list that has to keep working while you are asleep.
