# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Improve Overall Safety

*Chapter 22, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter opens with a pair of honest facts about why removing hazards is not the end of the story. The first is that "The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks." Since your own last real tidy or safety pass, what has quietly flowed back into your home, a new cord, a grown child's new reach, a battery that has died, that a snapshot could never have caught?

2. The second fact is gentler and harder: "Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach." How does it change the feeling of safety work to accept that some harm cannot be removed in advance at all, and can only be prepared for?

3. The chapter asks for a shift in aim: "You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all." Where in your home do you notice yourself chasing the same kind of hazard again and again, and what would raising the floor there look like instead of catching it one more time?

4. The governing image is a boat: "Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming." What is one place in your home where you have been bailing, handling the same danger over and over, that you could instead fix at the source so less water gets in?

5. Prevention, the chapter says, is the opposite of willpower: "You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing." Think of a safe habit you have tried to hold by resolving to be more careful. How could you redesign it so the safe way is simply the easy way, and no resolve is required?

6. The first prevention habit is to choose the safer default at the one moment it is easiest, the moment a thing arrives. What recently came into your home, and if you could rewind to the moment it crossed the threshold, what safer default would you have set for it then?

7. The second habit is to give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place. Which dangerous thing in your home does not yet have a home, and where would "away" and "safe" become the same place for it?

8. On the harm you cannot prevent, the chapter is blunt: "You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps." Have you ever tried to prevent something that could only be prepared for, and what did that cost you in worry that preparation might have quietly retired?

9. The emotional core of the chapter is this: "Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes." Whom in your household are you preparing for, and does framing preparation as love rather than fear change how willing you are to do it?

10. The chapter centers the alarm as "the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious." When did you last press the test button on every alarm in your home, and is there a floor right now that has none?

11. The Common Mistake is "Fitting a smoke alarm, feeling safe, and never testing it again, so the one protection you were counting on is silent the night it matters." Where else in your life have you put a protection in place and then quietly stopped keeping it alive, and what would "give the button a press whenever the clocks change" look like for it?

12. Preparation is deliberately finite, not a fortress: "Not a bunker. A prepared home." Which of the short list, the alarms kept alive, the extinguisher within reach, the walked exit plan, the few numbers everyone knows, does your home already have, and which one would you put in place first?

13. The Family Challenge asks you to walk the exit plan together, calmly, once, because "walking it turns a frightening idea into a known, ordinary thing." Has your household ever walked its way out, from each bedroom to one meeting spot, and what do you think stops most families from doing it?

14. The chapter gathers the whole of Safety into four moves: "See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it." Which of the four comes least naturally to you, and what would it take to make it as much a habit as the others?

15. The chapter warns that safety is not endless vigilance: "That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down." How is a steady, low, permanent habit of care different from being on guard all the time, and which one have you been carrying?

16. The needle reaches the fourth milestone, and the chapter is honest that the step was small: what these three safety chapters removed was "the friction of the worst day," quiet on a dial that mostly measures ordinary days. Standing with four of the six S's done and the rule in hand, "Finding hazards makes a home safer once. Preventing them and preparing for them keep it safe for good," what does it mean to you that "a safe home is not one where nothing can ever go wrong. It is one that is ready when it does," as the book turns to Chapter 23 and Standardize?
