# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

Last chapter you carried the five questions through the whole home, one room at a time, and removed each hazard the moment you found it: the button battery, the blind cord, the unanchored bookcase, the heater by the curtain. You made your home genuinely safer, and if you stopped there you would have done real good. Chapter 22 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where you learn why this part has one more chapter, and it is a pair of honest facts about homes: a home does not hold still, and some harm cannot be removed in advance at all, only prepared for.

"Improve Overall Safety" is the third and final chapter of Part Six, and Safety completes here, the fourth of the six S's, at the SAFETY COMPLETE milestone. Chapter 20 taught you to see hazards and Chapter 21 taught you to remove them thoroughly. This chapter adds the two forward-looking moves a hazard hunt cannot make, so the home is not just cleared of today's dangers but genuinely, durably safe. It teaches no new way to tidy and re-teaches neither the safety lens nor the hunt; it takes the home those left you and raises its baseline. As the chapter puts it, "The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks." A new cord arrives, a child grows two inches, batteries die. And you can clear every hazard you can find and a fire can still start, a person can still fall. "Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach."

The chapter rests on one reframe, the baseline. "You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet." The image behind it is a boat. Everything so far in this part has been subtraction, find a danger and take it out, and subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever. "Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming." Improving overall safety is the other kind of work, the kind that changes the boat itself so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the home's baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less. Raising the baseline has two halves, and the rest of the chapter is those two halves: prevention, which keeps new hazards from taking hold, and preparation, which readies the home for the harm no prevention can stop. As the definition box has it, "Improving overall safety is the work beyond removing today's hazards: preventing new ones from taking hold, and preparing for the harm you cannot prevent. Removing hazards makes a home safe now. Prevention and preparation are what keep it safe."

Prevention keeps new hazards from taking hold, and its whole content is making the safe way the easy way. "A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way." This is the opposite of willpower. You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater; what works is design. "You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing." In practice that is three small habits. Choose the safer default when something new comes into the house, at the one moment it is easiest, which is the moment it arrives. Give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place. And run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, a slow walk with the five questions, so drift is caught while it is small. None of this is a schedule to write down and enforce; that documented standard is a later S. Held together, the move is this: "Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in."

Then comes the harder, gentler half, and the emotional core of the chapter. However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach: a fire in a wall you cannot see, carbon monoxide with no warning your body can catch, a fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor. "You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps." What you can do is prepare, and preparation is a short and concrete list, not a state of dread. "Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes." The center is the alarm, the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious: a working smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where anything burns fuel, on every floor and near the bedrooms, is the single highest-value thing in the chapter, and its value depends entirely on being tested and kept alive. Beyond it, the list is finite: a fire extinguisher within reach of the kitchen and a rough sense of how to use it; a way out of every room, ideally two, and an exit plan the household has actually walked, to one meeting spot outside; and the few plain numbers and steps everyone should know, including the children, so the knowledge does not live in one head. "Not a bunker. A prepared home." Held together: "Put the protections in place for the harm you cannot prevent: working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows."

Then the chapter steps back and looks at the fourth S whole, because you have just finished it, and it turns out to have been four moves all along, each the answer to a question the one before it could not ask. You learned to see the hazard, by changing the question from "is it tidy?" to "could this hurt someone?". You learned to remove it, thoroughly, room by room. You have now learned to prevent the next one, by making the safe way the easy way, and to prepare for the harm none of that can stop. "See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it." And notice what it is not: not the exhausting vigilance the last chapter warned against. "That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down." Each move is work you do deliberately, once or now and then, precisely so you can stop watching. The professionals name the goal zero accidents and injuries; read it as a direction, not a destination you tick off. Being turned that way, with the four moves in hand, is what safety consciousness actually is: a steady, low, permanent habit of care running quietly in the background of an ordinary, happy home. The whole rule of it: "Finding hazards makes a home safer once. Preventing them and preparing for them keep it safe for good."

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it has reached a mark that has been a long time coming. "The needle reaches the Safety-complete mark, the fourth milestone on the dial, after Sort, Straighten, and Shine. Four of the six S's are done. The home is cleared, arranged, kept clean, and now safe: today's hazards gone, new ones kept from taking hold, and the worst days prepared for. The goal is still ahead, because two S's remain, Standardize and Sustain, the work of making all of it last." The step onto the mark was small, and that is honest, and it does not diminish the milestone. It was small because the big daily friction was cleared long ago in Sort and Straighten and Shine, and what these three safety chapters added was the removal not of everyday friction but of the friction of the worst day, quiet on a dial that mostly measures ordinary days, and one of the most valuable things in the whole book. The mark is a true arrival.

That is the fourth S, done, and it is worth marking, because it was a turn in the book. For the first nineteen chapters you worked on the state of the room; in these three you turned to the people in it, which was always the more important thing. "What is left is not more building; it is holding." Everything you have made lives, for now, largely in your head and your hands, and the last two S's change that, so the home holds its shape without leaning on any one person and keeps holding it over years. One Idea to Keep: "Safety is four moves, and you now have them all: see the hazard, remove it, prevent the next one, and prepare for the harm you cannot stop. A safe home is not one where nothing can ever go wrong. It is one that is ready when it does." For now, the fourth S is complete, and your home is safe. Take the win.
