## Post 1 · A hunt is only a snapshot

You can hunt your whole home, room by room, and fix every hazard you find. That is real, and worth doing. But a hunt is only a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks. A new cord, a child grown two inches, a battery gone quietly dead.

"You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet."

Removing hazards makes a home safe now. Two more moves keep it safe: preventing the next hazard from taking hold, and preparing for the harm you cannot prevent at all.

## Post 2 · Make the safe way the easy way

You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. Willpower runs out. Design does not.

"A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way."

In practice it is three small habits. Choose the safer default at the moment a new thing arrives, when it is easiest. Give the dangerous things a home, so away and safe are the same place. And re-hunt lightly, now and then, to catch drift while it is small.

## Post 3 · Preparation is love, not fear

Here is the gentlest idea in the whole book, and the one I keep coming back to. However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach. A fire can start in a wall you cannot see. A person can fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor.

"Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes."

The center of it is the alarm, the one protection that has to work while you are asleep. A working smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where anything burns fuel, on every floor. Its whole value depends on being tested and kept alive.

## Post 4 · Safety is four moves

Step back and look at Safety whole, because it turns out to have been four moves all along, each the answer to a question the one before it could not ask.

"See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it."

You learned to see the hazard, by asking "could this hurt someone?" instead of "is it tidy?". You learned to remove it, room by room. Now you have learned to prevent the next one, by making the safe way the easy way, and to prepare for the harm none of that can stop.

"That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down."

And it is the opposite of exhausting vigilance. Each move is work you do deliberately, once or now and then, precisely so you can stop watching.

"Finding hazards makes a home safer once. Preventing them and preparing for them keep it safe for good."
