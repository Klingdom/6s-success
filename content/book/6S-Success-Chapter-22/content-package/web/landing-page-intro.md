# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 22, Improve Overall Safety

By now you can do two things you could not do when this part began. You can see the hazards in your home, because Chapter 20 gave you the lens and changed the question from is it tidy to could this hurt someone. And you can remove them thoroughly, because Chapter 21 gave you the hunt, room by room, fixing each danger the moment you found it. Between them, those two skills have made your home meaningfully safer, and if you stopped here you would have done real good. But this part has one more chapter, and it rests on a pair of honest facts said not to take the relief away but to finish the job it started. The first is that a home does not hold still: the hunt was a snapshot, and after the shutter clicks the day ends and things begin quietly to flow back, a new cord, a child grown two inches, a battery gone flat. The second is harder and gentler: some harm cannot be removed in advance at all. You can clear every hazard you can find, and a fire can still start, a person can still fall. Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach.

So this chapter completes the fourth S by adding the two moves a hazard hunt cannot make: keep new dangers from taking hold, and be ready for the harm you cannot prevent. Everything so far in this part has been subtraction, find a danger and take it out, and that is the right place to start and not the place to stop, because subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever. Think of the difference between bailing a boat and fixing the leak. Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming. Improving overall safety changes the boat itself, so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the home's baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less. That is the whole idea in one line: you can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing, not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet.

Raising the baseline has two halves, and the rest of the chapter is those two halves. The first looks forward and keeps new hazards from taking hold, and its whole of it is making the safe way the easy way, which is the opposite of willpower. You will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. What works is design. In practice that is three small habits. Choose the safer default when something new comes into the house, at the one moment it is easiest, which is the moment it arrives: the heater that switches itself off if it tips, the cleaner that lives behind a latch. Give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place: the medicines in one latched box, the knives in the block, the button batteries up high. And run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, a slow walk with the five questions, so drift is caught while it is small. None of this is a schedule to write down and enforce; that documented standard is a later S. A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted.

The second half is the harder, gentler one, and it is not about hazards at all. However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach: a fire in a wall you cannot see, carbon monoxide with no warning your body can catch, a fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor. You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps. What you can do is prepare, so that on the day one of them comes the home meets it ready. Preparation is a short and concrete list, not a state of dread. Its center is the alarm, the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious: a working smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where anything burns fuel, on every floor and near the bedrooms, is the single highest-value thing in the chapter, and its whole value depends on being tested and kept alive, or it is a plastic disc that does nothing. Beyond the alarm: a fire extinguisher within reach of the kitchen and a rough sense of how to use it; a way out of every room, ideally two, and an exit plan the household has actually walked, from the bedrooms to one meeting spot outside, so that in the dark and the noise nobody has to invent a plan; and the few plain numbers and steps everyone should know, including the children, so the knowledge does not live only in one head. Not a bunker. A prepared home. Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes.

None of this is about living afraid or turning your home into a place of dread. It is the opposite. A home that has drifted, or that lacks a plan, is the normal case, and the fix is two calm moves, prevent and prepare, not guilt or fear. Step back and the fourth S turns out to have been four moves all along, each the answer to a question the one before it could not ask: see it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it. That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down, and it is the opposite of vigilance, because each move is work you do deliberately, once or now and then, precisely so you can stop watching. Read Chapter 22 free in the online book, then take the smallest possible first step: test every smoke and carbon-monoxide alarm in the home right now, one press each, and note any floor that has none. Working alarms are the highest-value protection in the house, and the only one that has to keep working while you are asleep. Four of the six S's are now done, and the goal stays ahead, because everything you have built must still be made to last.
