## Beat 2 · What removing cannot do

The premise made plain: two honest facts about homes mean removing hazards, on its own, is not enough. A home does not hold still, and some harm cannot be removed in advance at all.

- Composition: the prepared-home cutaway held on paper ground with the two facts made gentle and explicit. On one side, a soft time arrow showing ordinary life flowing back in, a new cord, a taller child's reach, a dead battery, one or two small spark red #CB4B36 marks to show drift, quiet and undramatic. On the other, a plain note that some harm sits outside reach entirely, a fire that can still start, a fall that can still happen.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, a sparing spark red #CB4B36 on the drift marks, green protection-dot #6E8B5B on the protections that stand ready. No verdict scolding; a home that drifts is the normal case.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "removing is necessary, and not enough."
- Point: between the lens and the hunt you have made your home meaningfully safer, and if you stopped here you would have done real good. But a home does not hold still: the hunt was a snapshot, and after it new cords, new heights, and dead batteries flow quietly back. And some harm cannot be removed in advance at all: you can clear every hazard you can find and a fire can still start, a person can still fall. So this chapter completes the fourth S by adding the two moves a hazard hunt cannot make.
- The frozen line sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "The hunt is a snapshot, and the home keeps moving after the shutter clicks." Beneath it may run, in Fraunces: "Those are not failures of your search. They are simply the part of safety that removing cannot reach." And, small: "See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it."

## Beat 3 · From removing to improving

Why removal alone leaves you working forever: bailing a boat versus fixing the leak, and the baseline reframe that names the better aim.

- Composition: two calm panels on panel #FBF7EF. On the LEFT, headed "Bailing," a hand lifting water from a boat that keeps taking it on, a small spark red #CB4B36 mark, necessary and endless. On the RIGHT, headed "Fixing the leak," the same boat with its hull sealed and its life jackets stowed, a green protection-dot #6E8B5B, so less water gets in and you are ready if it does.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the panel frames and the two headings, a sparing spark red #CB4B36 on the bailing side, green protection-dot #6E8B5B on the sealed-and-ready side. Calm and legible at a glance.
- Caption, from the book: "Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming."
- Point: everything so far in this part has been subtraction, find a danger and take it out, the right place to start and not the place to stop, because subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever. Improving overall safety changes the boat itself so less water gets in and you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less. Raising the baseline has two halves, prevention and preparation, and the rest of the chapter is those two halves.
- The frozen baseline reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet."

## Beat 5 · Prevent: keep new hazards from taking hold

The first forward move made visual: making the safe way the easy way, the opposite of willpower, in three small habits.

- Composition: three plain vignettes across panel #FBF7EF, each a green protection-dot #6E8B5B and a slate #3C5A6B label: a new heater chosen because it switches itself off if it tips ("safer default"); a cleaner going into a latched cupboard, with medicines in one latched box, knives in the block, and button batteries up high ("a home for the dangerous things"); a slow walk with the five questions ("a light re-hunt").
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, green protection-dot #6E8B5B on the three habits, slate #3C5A6B on the labels, terracotta #BC4B2A accent on the word Prevent. Calm; design, not resolve.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "make the safe way the easy way."
- Point: a hunted home drifts back toward hazard not because anyone is careless but because ordinary life brings new things in, and some are dangerous, and they do not announce themselves. The whole of prevention is making the safe way the easy way, so staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing. Three small habits: choose the safer default at the moment a thing arrives; give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place; run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, so drift is caught while it is small. Nothing here is a schedule to write down and enforce; that documented standard is a later S.
- The frozen prevention reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way." The frozen Prevent hero line "Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in." may run beneath in Fraunces. The 6S Tip sits in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Keep the tools of preparation in one known place, a single shelf or box: the spare alarm batteries, the fire extinguisher, a flashlight, the first-aid kit. - Slate #3C5A6B label: "love for the day you hope never comes."
