### Slide 4 · From removing to improving

- Everything so far in this part has been subtraction: find a danger, take it out. That is the right place to start and not the place to stop, because subtraction alone leaves you doing the same work forever. Think of the difference between bailing a boat and fixing the leak. Verbatim: "Removing hazards is bailing: necessary, effective, and endless, because the water keeps coming." Improving overall safety changes the boat itself so less water gets in and so you are ready if it does. You will still bail now and then; the hunt is not retired. But once the baseline is higher, the bailing gets rarer and matters less.
- Raising the baseline has two halves, and the rest of the chapter is those two halves. One looks forward at the hazards that have not arrived yet and keeps them from taking hold (prevention). The other looks at the harm that no prevention can stop and gets the home ready to meet it (preparation). Together they turn a home that has been made safe into a home that stays safe.
- The baseline reframe, frozen: "You can chase hazards one at a time forever, or you can raise the whole home's floor so it is harder to get hurt in at all. Overall safety is the second thing: not a longer list of dangers removed, but a home that is safer even against the ones you have not met yet."

### Slide 5 · Raise the baseline: prevent plus prepare

- The hero of the chapter is not a gadget but a change of aim: stop chasing hazards one at a time and raise the whole home's floor, so it is safer even against the dangers you have not met yet. Removing hazards (Chapters 20 and 21) lowers the danger that is here; improving overall safety raises the floor under everything. Raising it has exactly two halves, and naming them cleanly is the whole map of the chapter: prevention, which keeps new hazards from taking hold, and preparation, which gets the home ready for the harm no prevention can stop.
- The two are different in kind and both are needed. Prevention looks forward at hazards that have not arrived and steers them away before they settle. Preparation is not about hazards at all; it accepts that some harm sits outside your reach and makes the home ready to meet it. Made safe is what the hunt did; kept safe is what these two do together.

### Slide 6 · Prevent: keep new hazards from taking hold

- A hunted home drifts back toward hazard not because anyone is careless but because ordinary life brings new things in, and some are dangerous, and they do not announce themselves. The whole of prevention is making the safe way the easy way, which is the opposite of willpower: you will not keep a home safe by resolving to be more careful, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. Verbatim: "You make the safe way the easy way, so that staying safe is what naturally happens when everyone just does the convenient thing." What works is design.
- In practice that is three small habits. First, choose the safer default when something new comes into the house, at the one moment it is easiest, the moment it arrives (the heater that switches itself off if it tips, the cleaner that lives behind a latch). Second, give the dangerous things a home, the Straighten move aimed at safety, so away and safe are the same place (the medicines in one latched box, the knives in the block, the button batteries up high). Third, run the hunt again, lightly, now and then, a slow walk with the five questions, so drift is caught while it is small. Nothing here is a schedule to write down and enforce; that documented standard is a later S.
- The prevention reframe, frozen: "A hunt clears the hazards that are here. Prevention is what keeps the next one from quietly taking their place, because a home does not stay hunted. It drifts, and things flow back in, and the only defense that scales is making the safe way the easy way." Prevent hero line, frozen: "Make the safe way the easy way, so hazards stop coming back: choose the safer default, give the dangerous things a home, and re-hunt now and then to catch what has drifted in."

### Slide 7 · Prepare: ready for what you cannot prevent

- The harder, gentler half, the one that is not about hazards at all. However well you prevent, some harm sits outside your reach: a fire in a wall you cannot see, carbon monoxide with no warning your body can catch, a fall on a night when nothing was wrong with the floor. Verbatim: "You cannot remove these in advance, and pretending you can is not safety; it is just anxiety with extra steps." What you can do is prepare, so that on the day one of them comes the home meets it ready. Preparation is a short and concrete list, not a state of dread.
- The center is the alarm, the one protection that has to work while you are unconscious: a working smoke alarm, and a carbon-monoxide alarm where anything burns fuel, on every floor and near the bedrooms, is the single highest-value thing in the chapter, and its whole value depends on being tested and its battery kept alive, or it is a plastic disc that does nothing. Beyond it: a fire extinguisher within reach of the kitchen and a rough sense of how to use it; a way out of every room, ideally two, and an exit plan the household has actually walked, to one meeting spot outside; and the few plain numbers and steps everyone should know, including the children. Verbatim: "Not a bunker. A prepared home."
- The preparation reframe, frozen, the emotional core: "Some harm you cannot prevent, only prepare for. You cannot promise a fire will never start. You can promise that the alarm will wake you and that the way out is clear. Preparation is love for the day you hope never comes." Prepare hero line, frozen: "Put the protections in place for the harm you cannot prevent: working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms you keep alive, a fire extinguisher within reach, an exit everyone has walked, and the few numbers and steps everyone knows." Quick Win, verbatim: "Test every smoke and carbon-monoxide alarm in the home right now, one press each, and note any floor that has none. Working alarms are the highest-value protection in the house, and the only one that has to keep working while you are asleep."

### Slide 9 · The whole of Safety

- Step back and look at the fourth S whole, because you have just finished it, and it turns out to have been four moves all along, each the answer to a question the one before it could not ask. You learned to see the hazard, by changing the question from "is it tidy?" to "could this hurt someone?". You learned to remove it, thoroughly, room by room. You have now learned to prevent the next one, by making the safe way the easy way. And you have learned to prepare for the harm none of that can stop. Verbatim: "See it, remove it, prevent it, prepare for it." Verbatim: "That is not a checklist to run once; it is a way of holding your home that, once you have it, you do not really put down."
- And notice what it is not. It is not the exhausting vigilance the last chapter warned against. Each of the four moves is the opposite of vigilance: a piece of work you do deliberately, once or now and then, precisely so that you can stop watching. The professionals name the goal zero accidents and injuries, and it can sound cold, but read it as a direction rather than a destination you will ever tick off. You will not achieve a mathematically perfect home; you are simply always turned that way, and being turned that way, with these four moves in hand, is what safety consciousness actually is: not fear held at a high pitch, but a steady, low, permanent habit of care that mostly runs quietly in the background of an ordinary, happy home.
- Family Challenge, verbatim: "Walk the exit plan together, calmly, once. From each bedroom, how do you get out, and where does everyone meet outside? Children who have walked it once remember it, and walking it turns a frightening idea into a known, ordinary thing."
