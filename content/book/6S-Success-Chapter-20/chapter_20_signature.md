## Identity strings (frozen; copy verbatim in manuscript and HTML)
- **Title:** Safety: Protect People First
- **Subtitle:** A home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with, because tidy and safe are different questions. The fourth S turns from the state of the room to the people in it: look at the home through one new question, could this hurt someone, and fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, first.
- **Masthead:** "Part Six · Safety" / "Chapter Twenty"
- **The rule (frozen):** Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone.
- **The triage (frozen):** Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else.
- **Definition (frozen, defbox):** A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy.
- **The tidy-is-not-safe reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask.
- **Why safety is its own S (frozen, pull-quote):** The method began with five S's. When it spread beyond the factory floor, people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1. It was not that the others ignored safety. It was that something this important should never be left to chance, and making it a step of its own is a way of refusing to assume it.
- **The pyramid (frozen, pull-quote, Heinrich home-scaled):** Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is exactly why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was.
- **Safety is love, not fear (frozen, pull-quote, the emotional core):** None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared.
- **Outcome vocabulary (frozen):** the safety lens / could this hurt someone / protect people first / worst and soonest first. The home is already sorted, arranged, and clean; Chapter 20 asks the safety question and handles the worst dangers first.

## Hero device: The safety lens (develop safety consciousness) plus protect-people-first triage
A new question asked of the whole home ("could this hurt someone?") that finds a different set of things than the first three S's, paired with a triage (rank by how badly and how soon; fix the worst first) because you cannot fix everything at once. Motivated by Heinrich's pyramid: remove hazards at the base while they are cheap, because you cannot tell which is the dangerous one. Source: Seiketsu-adjacent "safety as the added S" and "develop safety consciousness"; the hazard definition and Heinrich's Principle from the 6S materials. Referenced lightly and accurately; do not overclaim.

## Motifs
- **Tidy is not safe.** The first three S's never ask the safety question; hazards pass every earlier test.
- **The plus one.** Safety was pulled out and named so it would never be left to chance.
- **The new question.** Change the question and you change what you can see (the two lenses).
- **Worst and soonest first.** Triage, not a spreadsheet; the pyramid is why the top matters now.
- **Safety is love, not fear.** Handle the dangers so you can stop worrying; safety is what lets you relax.
- **The deepest friction.** The needle moves not because daily friction dropped but because the chance of harm did.

## Emotional purpose
Calm, steady reassurance and quiet love, never alarm. This chapter touches real fear (children, falls, fire, poison), so the register is a steady hand: it names dangers plainly, refuses to dramatize them, and frames the whole S as the thing that REDUCES worry rather than stoking it. The payoff is a parent breathing out. No shame (adults habituate to their own homes; unnoticed hazards are normal), no fear-mongering, no graphic injury, no alarmist statistics.

## Practical purpose
Teach the reader to ask the safety question of the whole home, recognize hazards (including the tidy ones and the knee-height ones), rank them by harm, fix the worst and soonest first, and make one first pass handling the top dangers today. Do NOT re-teach cleaning (Shine). Do NOT build the systematic room-by-room hazard hunt or the full safety-questions checklist (Ch 21). Do NOT build ongoing safety systems, emergency prep, or the home versions of safety training/goals (Ch 22). Do NOT build documented standards (Standardize) or long-term adherence systems (Sustain). Do NOT re-open Sort/Straighten/Shine. Avoid brand names.
