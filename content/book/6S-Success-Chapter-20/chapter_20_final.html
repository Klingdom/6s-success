Chapter Twenty

Safety: Protect People First

A home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with, because tidy and safe are different questions. The fourth S turns from the state of the room to the people in it: look at the home through one new question, could this hurt someone, and fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, first.

Stand in the home you have built across this book and look at it with plain pride. It is cleared of what did not belong. Everything that stayed has a place, and the places make sense, and they explain themselves. It is clean, and it stays clean, on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight.

Not because you did anything wrong. Because nothing you have done so far was ever aimed at that. Sort asked whether a thing belonged. Straighten asked where it should live. Shine asked whether it was clean and what its condition was telling you. Three good questions, and not one of them is the question that notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, or the medicine sitting at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm that has been quietly dead since its battery gave out last spring. Those things were never messy, never misplaced, never dirty. They passed every test the first three S's could give, and they were dangerous the whole time.

That is why there is a fourth S, and why it is not an afterthought but a turn. For nineteen chapters this book has been about the state of the room. Safety is about the people in it, which is a different thing entirely and, when the two ever compete, the more important one. A cluttered counter is an annoyance. A cabinet a child can open is a catastrophe waiting for a quiet afternoon. This chapter teaches you to see the difference, to look at your finished home through one new question, and to act on what you find in the order that protects people first. It is the most important S in the book, and it asks the least tidying of any of them.

In this chapter

  • See why a clean, arranged, well-kept home can still be genuinely dangerous.
  • Understand why safety is its own S, and why it comes before tidiness and efficiency.
  • Look at your home through the safety lens: the question could this hurt someone.
  • Protect people first by ranking hazards by harm and fixing the worst and soonest ones first.
  • Make a first safety pass, and handle a few real dangers today so you can stop worrying about them.

The reframeA Clean Home Is Not a Safe Home

Let me say plainly the thing this whole part turns on, because it runs against a feeling most of us carry without examining it.

A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask.

We tend to feel that a tidy home is a safe one, that order and cleanliness add up to protection, and it is a comforting feeling and it is not true. Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap can be dangerous precisely because it is invisible. The most immaculate kitchen in the world is not made one bit safer by its gleam if the pan handles turn out over the edge of the stove where a child can reach them. The danger and the cleanliness are simply unrelated, and a home can score perfectly on one while failing quietly on the other.

This is worth sitting with, because it is the reason the fourth S has to exist as its own step rather than living inside the others. If cleaning made a home safe, we would not need to talk about safety separately; it would come free with Shine. It does not come free. Safety is a separate question that has to be asked on purpose, because the home will never raise it for you. A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not.

The plus oneWhy Safety Is Its Own S

There is a small piece of history here that is worth knowing, because it is the story of the very "plus one" that makes this book about six S's and not five.

The method began with five S's. When it spread beyond the factory floor, people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1. It was not that the others ignored safety. It was that something this important should never be left to chance, and making it a step of its own is a way of refusing to assume it.

The original method, born on the factory floors of Toyota, had five steps, and safety was meant to run through all of them. And it did, and it does; a sorted, arranged, clean workspace really is a safer one. But as the method spread and people lived with it, they noticed that "safety runs through everything" has a quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one, exactly the trap you met with the cleaning in Chapter 17. Something that matters as much as whether people get hurt cannot be left as a happy side effect of the other steps. So it was pulled out and named, given its own S, made a thing you do on purpose rather than a thing you hope falls out of the rest.

That is the whole reason this S comes with the word first in its title. It is not first in sequence; you did it fourth, after the home was cleared and arranged and clean, and that order is fine. It is first in priority. When a safety fix and a tidiness improvement and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time, without a debate, because the downside on the other two is inconvenience and the downside on safety is somebody you love getting hurt. Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list.

The new questionThe Safety Lens

So how do you see what you have been walking past for years? You change the question. For three parts of this book you have looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely.

Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone.

That single swapped question is the whole tool, and it is more powerful than it sounds, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm, which is, after all, perfectly tidy. Ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same smoke alarm lights up like a warning, because now you are looking for the right thing. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness, which sounds grand and means something simple: teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing.

And it helps to know exactly what you are noticing, so here is the plain definition, straight from the method and shrunk to fit a home.

Hazard

A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy.

Read that last line twice, because it is the thing people miss. We picture hazards as obvious, dramatic, dirty, a frayed wire throwing sparks, a spill on the floor. A few are. But most of the real dangers in a home are quiet and orderly and have been sitting in plain sight so long that they have become furniture to you. The lens is how you make them visible again, by asking, of everything your eye has stopped seeing, the one question that turns furniture back into a hazard.

TriageProtect People First

Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip quickly from useful into overwhelming. The answer to overwhelming is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order, because not all hazards are equal and they are not meant to be treated as if they were.

Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else.

Rank what you find by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. A dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a small child can open score high on both, they could cause terrible harm and the chance is real and present, so they go to the very top and they get fixed today. A bannister that is slightly loose but holds fine could matter someday and is not going to fail this week, so it waits its turn. This is not being casual about the bannister. It is refusing to let the bannister, and forty other small things, crowd out the two that could actually hurt someone this afternoon. Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology.

And here is why you act on the top of that list now rather than someday, and it comes straight from a pattern safety workers have watched for a century.

Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is exactly why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was.

That is the quiet logic under "protect people first." You are not fixing the smoke alarm because you expect a fire tonight; you almost certainly will not have one. You are fixing it because you cannot know which of a home's small dangers is the one that will matter, and the cost of removing them while they are harmless is so low, and the cost of guessing wrong so high, that there is nothing to weigh. Handle the worst ones now, while they are still just items on a list, and the day you would have found out which one was real simply never comes.

The heart of itSafety Is Love, Not Fear

If this chapter has started a small knot of anxiety in you, a sense that your home is a minefield and you have been negligent, stop and read this next part carefully, because it is the heart of how the fourth S is meant to feel.

None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared.

The goal of safety is not vigilance. Vigilance is exhausting and it does not scale; you cannot watch everything forever, and the attempt just turns a home into a place of low, constant dread. The goal is the opposite of vigilance: it is to deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and then you never again have to track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach, because the bleach is handled. You put the working battery in the alarm, and then the whole category of "what if there's a fire and we don't wake up" simply closes. Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again.

That is why safety, of all the S's, is the one most rooted in love rather than order. You are not tidying here. You are quietly, unglamorously making sure the people you live with come to no harm in the place that is supposed to be their safest, and then, having made sure, you get to relax inside it. A safe home is not a fearful one. It is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands, so you do not have to keep doing it with your mind.

AppliedA First Safety Pass

So make a start, today, and keep it small, because protecting people first means the first pass is not a thorough hunt; it is the worst few things, handled now. Take the lens for a slow walk through the home you know so well you have stopped seeing it, and let it show you the dangers hiding in plain sight.

You will find them quickly once you are asking. The smoke alarm you cannot remember ever testing: press the button, and if it is silent, a new battery goes in before you do anything else on this list, because nothing else you find will matter as much. The cupboard under the sink, tidy and ordinary, full of cleaning bottles at the exact height of a small child: everything in it that could poison someone goes up high or behind a latch today. The rug at the top of the stairs that has always slid a little: a strip of grip tape underneath, or the rug gone, because a fall down a staircase is precisely the worst-and-soonest kind of hazard. Even the launch pad, that first small victory by the door, gets the new question: is anything heavy stored where it could fall on someone, is a charging cord strung where a foot could catch it, is there anything at knee height a crawling child could reach. Fix what the lens finds at the top of the list, and leave the rest for the thorough pass that is coming.

Notice what that walk was and was not. It was not a deep clean and it was not a tidy; the home was already both. Nothing moved for the sake of order. A few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. That is Safety in its smallest complete form: one lens, one walk, the worst dangers handled first, and a handful of the ways your home could have hurt someone quietly closed off for good.

Quick Win

Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing today. You just protected people first.

6S Tip

Get down to a child's height and look. Most of the worst home hazards, low cupboards, dangling cords, small swallowable things, sit in a band of space adults never look at because they never live there. A lot of danger is at knee height.

The stepThe Needle Takes Its First Safety Step

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, and it moves for a reason it has never moved for before.

Every step until now, the needle traveled because the home got less cluttered, better arranged, or cleaner: because the daily friction of living there went down. This time nothing about the daily friction changed at all. The counters are no tidier than they were this morning; the home is no cleaner. And yet the needle steps toward calm, because a few real dangers that were in this home are now gone, and removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is. It is not the friction you feel every day. It is the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving, and taking that away moves the needle more honestly than any amount of tidying.

Protect people first.

The step is real and, you will notice, it is small, and both of those are true on purpose. Small, because the earlier S's already removed most of what this meter measures; the home was already close to calm, so what remains is not big friction to clear but important things to lock down, and safety, standards, and staying power move the needle in smaller, quieter increments than the first sweep of clearing and arranging did. Real, because a hazard removed is a genuine and permanent gain, worth every millimetre it earns. Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone is the passed marker just behind the live needle now; the goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead, because Safety has barely begun, with a thorough hazard hunt and a whole layer of lasting safety still to come, and Standardize and Sustain beyond them. The needle moved for the best of reasons today. It also has real distance left, and both are true at once.

Family Challenge

Ask everyone, including the children, to point out one thing in the home they think could hurt someone. Children notice hazards adults have stopped seeing, and being asked makes them safer. Fix the worst one together.

In closingSafety Has Only Begun

You changed the question, and that changes everything that follows. You looked at a finished, cared-for home through the one lens the first three S's do not carry, and it showed you a handful of ways the place could hurt the people in it. You put those findings in order by harm, you handled the worst ones today, and, doing it, you traded a little vague background worry for the solid quiet of a few real dangers dealt with. That is the fourth S in a single pass: not a tidier home, a safer one.

What you have not done is the thorough part, and that is deliberate, because a first pass catches the dangers that are obvious once you are finally looking, and it will walk right past the ones that are not. The medicine you thought of is handled; the button battery in the old remote that a toddler could swallow, the space heater too close to the curtain, the extension cord run under the rug where it can overheat, those take a calmer, more systematic search than a first walk gives. That search is the next chapter. And past it lies the work of making safety not a one-time sweep but a lasting part of how the household lives, which is the chapter after. For now, hold on to the lens, because it works in any room, on any day: not is it tidy, but could it hurt someone.

Common Mistake

Treating every hazard as equal and trying to fix them all at once, so you fix none. Safety is triage, not a spreadsheet. The dead smoke alarm cannot wait; the slightly loose bannister can. Rank by harm, and start at the top.

One Idea to Keep

Protect people first. Walk the home asking not whether it is tidy but whether it could hurt someone, fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest before anything else, and you trade a low hum of worry for the quiet of a home whose real dangers have been handled.
