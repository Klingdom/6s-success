# Facebook Longform Post: A Clean Home Is Not a Safe Home, and That Took Me Years to See

A longer, story-driven post from Chapter 20 of 6S Success: Home Edition, the first chapter of Part Six and the one that opens Safety, the fourth S. Conversational, like talking to a friend. Ends with a soft CTA. No em dashes. Body hard-capped 300 to 450 words.

---

Last month I stood in my living room feeling genuinely proud. It was cleared of what did not belong. Everything in it had a place. It was clean, and it stayed clean, on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure I had, it was finished. And it could still have sent someone to the hospital that night.

Not because I had done anything wrong. Because none of the questions I had been asking were ever aimed at that. I had asked whether things belonged, where they should live, whether they were clean. Not one of those notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, or the cleaning bottles at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm that had been quietly dead since its battery gave out last spring. Those things were never messy, never misplaced, never dirty. They passed every test I had, and they were dangerous the whole time.

Then I read the line that named it.

"A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask."

That was it exactly. Tidy and safe are simply different questions, and I had only ever asked one of them.

So I changed the question. Instead of walking the home asking whether it was in order, I walked it asking one thing.

"Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone."

The same rooms gave up answers the first question could never see. I did not try to fix everything. I ranked what I found by two plain things: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon. Protect people first. The smoke alarm got a fresh battery before anything else. The cleaning bottles went up high, behind a latch. A strip of grip tape went under the rug at the top of the stairs.

Three fixes, one slow walk. Nothing got tidier. A few real dangers simply, quietly stopped being dangers, and I got to set down a low hum of worry I had not even known I was carrying.
