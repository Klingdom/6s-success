# Chapter 20 Calls to Action: Safety: Protect People First

This is the first chapter of Part Six, and it opens Safety, the fourth S. Its strongest CTA is the smallest possible proof of the whole idea: walk one room, find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, and fix that one thing today. The CTAs match the chapter's tone of calm, loving attention, not alarm: a home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space, and the fix is a new question and a short triage, not guilt or fear. The move costs a few minutes, once, because the value is trading a low hum of worry for the quiet of a real danger handled.

## Primary CTA
Protect people first, today. "Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing today. ## In-chapter actions a reader can take (from the callouts)
- **Quick Win:** Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing today. You just protected people first.
- **Family Challenge:** Ask everyone, including the children, to point out one thing in the home they think could hurt someone. Children notice hazards adults have stopped seeing, and being asked makes them safer. Fix the worst one together.
- **6S Tip:** Get down to a child's height and look. Most of the worst home hazards, low cupboards, dangling cords, small swallowable things, sit in a band of space adults never look at because they never live there. A lot of danger is at knee height.

## CTA writing notes
- The action is a small, provable move that demonstrates the whole chapter: walk one room, find the single most dangerous thing, and fix it today. Lead with it, which is concrete and doable in a few minutes.
- Keep it non-prescriptive and anti-shame, in a register of calm, loving attention and relief. A home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space; the fix is a new question and a short triage, not guilt.
- The rule (ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone), the triage (protect people first, worst and soonest first), the tidy-is-not-safe reframe, and the safety-is-love-not-fear line are the most memorable, shareable lines. Lead social with them.
- Avoid hype, alarmist statistics, graphic injury, and any force or violence framing. Nothing is attacked, blitzed, or waged war on; a few real dangers are simply handled. Keep the calm register throughout, and avoid brand names (say "a cabinet latch", not a brand).
- Scope note: this opens Safety and does one first high-value pass on the worst and most obvious dangers; it does not re-teach cleaning (Chapters 16 to 19), build the systematic room-by-room hazard hunt or the full safety-questions checklist (Chapter 21), build ongoing safety systems or emergency prep (Chapter 22), write documented standards (Standardize), or solve long-term adherence (Sustain). Safety has only begun and the goal is still ahead.
