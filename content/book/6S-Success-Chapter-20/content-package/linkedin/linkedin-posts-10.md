## 1. A clean home is not a safe home

You can clear a home of what does not belong, give everything a place, and keep it clean. By every measure of tidiness, it is finished.

And it could still hold a danger none of that ever asked about.

Sort asked whether a thing belonged. Straighten asked where it lived. Shine asked whether it was clean. Not one of them asked whether it could hurt someone.

So a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. The rug at the top of the stairs. The medicine within reach. The smoke alarm quietly dead since spring. All tidy. All dangerous.

Tidy and safe are different questions. That is why there is a fourth S.

## 2. A hazard does not announce itself

We picture hazards as obvious: dramatic, dirty, easy to spot. A few are.

But most of the real dangers in a home are quiet and orderly, and have been sitting in plain sight so long that they have become furniture to you.

A mess announces itself. A hazard does not. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not.

That is what makes it dangerous, and it is why cleaning alone will never find it. If tidying made a home safe, safety would come free with a good clean. It does not.

Safety is a separate question that has to be asked on purpose, because the home will never raise it for you.

## 3. Why safety got its own S

Here is the small piece of history behind the "plus one" that makes this six S's and not five.

The method began on the factory floors of Toyota with five steps, and safety was meant to run through all of them. But people noticed that "safety runs through everything" has a quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one.

So safety was pulled out and named, made a thing you do on purpose rather than a thing you hope falls out of the rest. That is 5S+1.

Something this important should never be left to chance. Making it a step of its own is a way of refusing to assume it.

## 4. The safety lens

You see what you have walked past for years by changing one question.

For a long time you looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely: could this hurt someone?

That single swapped question is the whole tool, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides right over the dead smoke alarm. Ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same alarm lights up.

The professionals call it developing safety consciousness: teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic.

What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing.

Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone. Read the free chapter.

## 5. What counts as a hazard

Here is the plain definition, straight from the method and shrunk to fit a home.

A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf.

Notice what is not in that list. Nothing about mess. Nothing about dirt. A hazard does not have to look like anything is wrong.

It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy.

Once you have the definition, you cannot unsee them, and that is exactly the point. The lens makes visible again the dangers that have quietly become furniture to you.

## 6. Protect people first

Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected. That can tip from useful into overwhelming.

The answer is not to look away. It is to put the findings in order, because not all hazards are equal.

Rank what you find by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to.

A dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a small child can open score high on both, so they get fixed today. A slightly loose bannister that holds fine can wait its turn.

Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology.

Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else. Read the free chapter.

## 7. Why act now, not someday

There is a calm reason to fix the top of the list today rather than someday.

Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury.

You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one.

That is exactly why you do not wait. You remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was.

You are not fixing the smoke alarm because you expect a fire tonight. You fix it because removing a harmless hazard costs so little, and guessing wrong costs so much, that there is nothing to weigh.

## 8. Safety is love, not fear

If any of this has started a small knot of anxiety, read this carefully, because it is the heart of the fourth S.

The goal of safety is not vigilance. Vigilance is exhausting and it does not scale, and it just turns a home into a place of low, constant dread.

The goal is the opposite: deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and never again track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach.

Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented. It is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again.

Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out. Read the free chapter.

## 9. A first pass, kept small

You do not have to make the whole home safe today. Protecting people first means the first pass is the worst few things, handled now.

Take the lens for a slow walk. The smoke alarm you cannot remember testing: press the button, and if it is silent, a new battery goes in first. The cleaning bottles at a small child's height: up high or behind a latch today. The rug at the top of the stairs that has always slid: grip tape underneath, or gone.

Notice what that walk was and was not. Not a deep clean, not a tidy. A few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone. And now they cannot.

Not a tidier home, a safer one. Read the free chapter in the online book.

## 10. The needle takes its first Safety step

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, for a reason it never has before.

Every step until now, the needle moved because the daily friction of living there went down. This time nothing about the daily friction changed. The home is no cleaner.

And yet the needle steps toward calm, because a few real dangers are simply gone. Removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is: not the friction you feel every day, but the friction of the worst day, kept from ever arriving.

The step is small, and honest. The earlier S's already cleared most of what the meter measures. The goal is still ahead, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow.
