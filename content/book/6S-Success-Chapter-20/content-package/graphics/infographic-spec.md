# Infographic Spec: Safety, Protect People First

**Chapter 20, "Safety: Protect People First"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Six · Safety**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: a finished
home, clean and arranged and cared for, can still hold every danger it started with, because tidy and
safe are different questions, so this chapter hands you one new question and asks it of the home you have
built. The concept pairs the hero tool, **the safety lens**, with its frozen rule, **Ask not whether it
is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone.**, and shows the picture at its heart: the same tidy room
seen through two questions, calm and passing under "is it tidy?" and quietly full of hazards under "could
this hurt someone?". Then it shows how you act on what you find: protect people first, worst and soonest
first. So the viewer sees the whole move at once, the new question, what it reveals, and the order it is
handled in.

It stays consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_20_final.html`. Slate carries the rule,
the definition, the headings, the axes, and the tags; a soft green wash marks the tidy-and-passing side
and the calm-dots; a single small warm red is held back for the hazard marks and the fix-first accents
and is used nowhere else on this calm image. "Spotless" is not the point; a home can be cleared,
arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with, so the target here is a home that is
also safe. Anti-shame throughout: a home holds hazards because they are quiet and orderly and have become
furniture to the eye, not because anyone was negligent, and the fix is a new question asked on purpose,
not vigilance or blame. Safety is love, not fear, so the register is calm and reassuring, never an alarm.

## Layout notes

- The rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the whole of the chapter compressed to one
  swapped question: ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone. The eye should read
  the rule, then see the two-lenses picture prove it, the same room passing the tidy question and giving
  up five hazards to the safety question.
- The two-lenses picture is the hero, so make the sameness unmistakable: draw the two rooms identical
  object for object and change only the heading, the green wash, and the five marks. If a viewer suspects
  the second room was tidied or rearranged, the image has failed; the entire point is that nothing moved
  except the question you asked.
- Hold the color discipline: the single small warm red is reserved for the five hazard-marks, the two
  fix-first dots, and the pyramid cap, and is used nowhere else; slate carries the rule, the definition,
  the headings, and the axes; a soft green wash marks the tidy-and-passing side and the calm-dots. This
  image is calm, so no fill-line red anywhere except those small marks.
- This image makes the tool, the safety lens and its rule, and the order you act in, protect people
  first. Do NOT re-teach Sort, Straighten, or Shine; the home is already clean and kept, and the chapter
  simply asks a new question of it. Do NOT run the thorough room-by-room hazard hunt (Chapter 21, the
  next chapter; handoff only), do NOT build lasting or standardized safety (later chapters and the
  Standardize S), and do NOT solve long-term adherence or audits (Sustain, the last S); this is the
  opening pass, the worst few dangers handled today. And name no brand and draw no specific product; the
  bottle, alarm, and cord are plain and generic, teaching the habit, not a shopping list.
- Anti-shame: a home holds hazards because they are quiet and orderly and have become furniture to the
  eye, not because anyone was negligent, and the fix is a new question asked on purpose, not vigilance or
  blame. What you are building is not fear, it is a habit of noticing. Keep the register calm and
  reassuring, safety is love, not fear; no alarm cues, no injury imagery, no scare framing, no force
  framing anywhere. A clean home is a fine thing to have made; this image adds the question that also
  makes it safe.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the room and the axes
  drawn by hand rather than as a clinical chart, the glyphs sketched rather than typeset, the whole image
  the calm, warm register of a good idea rather than a warning poster.
