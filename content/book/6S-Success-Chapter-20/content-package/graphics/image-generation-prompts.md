# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 20

**"Safety: Protect People First"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Six · Safety*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to
hold across all five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`,
terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B`, soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single warm
red `#CB4B36` reserved for the small hazard marks and the fix-first accents only. Natural soft
daylight, shallow but not showy depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the finished home the reader has cleared, arranged, and cleaned across
Chapters 12 to 19: the settled launch pad by the door, the whole home kept clean and read on a short
shared rhythm. This chapter, the FIRST of Part Six, OPENS Safety, the fourth S, the "plus one" added to
the original five, and does one turn: it moves the book from the state of the room to the people in it.
It teaches the tidy-is-not-safe reframe, the safety lens (the single swapped question, could this hurt
someone), protect-people-first triage (rank by how badly and how soon or likely, worst and soonest
first), Heinrich's Principle home-scaled (a pile of small hazards, mostly near misses, rarely one
serious injury), a first small safety pass, and the emotional core, safety is love, not fear. Nothing
of Sort, Straighten, or Shine is re-taught here; the home is already clean and kept, and the chapter
simply asks a new question of it. No thorough room-by-room hazard hunt is done (Chapter 21 · Find and
Remove Hazards, teaser and handoff only), no lasting or standardized safety system is built (later
chapters and the Standardize S), and no long-term adherence or audits are solved (Sustain, the last S).
Keep the tone calm, warm, and reassuring: a home holds hazards because they are quiet and orderly and
have become furniture to the eye, not because anyone was negligent; the fix is a new question asked on
purpose, not vigilance or blame. Safety is love, not fear, so the register is relief and the ease of a
parent breathing out, never lecture, never guilt, never a scare tone. Crucially, describe only the calm,
warm illustrated house style: no photoreal harm, no injury, no accident, no blood, no gore, no distress;
the hazards are patient and unhappening, marked with small quiet symbols, and any child in frame is calm
and safe. No force or violence framing anywhere, no war on hazards, no blitz, no purge. No alarmist
injury statistics. No hype words. Avoid brand names and specific product endorsements; the bottles,
alarms, and cords are plain and generic, teaching the habit, not a shopping list.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical
object in the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real sheet, or the reading on a real
alarm indicator, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental hand-lettered
label: use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 20 · SAFETY`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot
between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: worst-and-soonest, near-miss, home-scaled,
two-axis, fix-first, first-pass, safety-lens, launch-pad, knee-height, low-cupboard, whole-home,
line-and-flat-color, Shine-complete, Straighten-complete, Sort-complete, calm-dot, hazard-mark,
breathe-out.

## 1. The finished home, quietly dangerous (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of a finished, cared-for home, clearly clean and tidy and lovely,
set in soft evening daylight. The home is the cleared, arranged, freshly cleaned one from the end of
Shine, everything in its place and gleaming, and it reads at a glance as finished and safe. Laid quietly
over that tidy scene, small and easy to miss, are five gentle hazard marks on things tidiness never
questioned: a rug curling up at the very top of a staircase, a low cupboard standing open with a plain
cleaning bottle inside and a calm toddler a step away, a smoke alarm on the ceiling with its little
indicator light gone dark, a heavy box perched near the edge of a high shelf, and a cord run taut across
a walkway. Nothing here is a mess, nothing is dirty or misplaced; you simply see, through the small
marks, that a clean home is not the same as a safe one. Warm paper cream and honey wood tones, ink-brown
shadows, one soft warm-red accent held only for the five small quiet hazard-marks. Editorial cookbook-
style illustration. A finished home, lovely, and quietly dangerous.

- Mood: by every measure so far the home is finished and good, and it is also, in five quiet places,
  dangerous; none of these hazards were messy, misplaced, or dirty, so none of them showed up in Sort,
  Straighten, or Shine; seeing them takes a different question, and asking it is the fourth S; tidy is
  not the same as safe.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, floating typeset text overlays, any messy or dirty
  home, any injury, accident, blood, gore, or a distressed or endangered child, a scared or scolded
  person, force or violence cues, an alarm or warning-poster tone, a warm-red accent sprayed anywhere but
  the five small hazard-marks, brand names or recognizable product labels; keep the home genuinely
  finished and lovely and the five hazards small, patient, and quiet, the whole image a gentle second
  look rather than a warning.

## 2. The two lenses (the hero, one room two questions)

A warm editorial illustration of the identical living room drawn twice, side by side, in soft evening
light. On the left, under a soft green wash, the room reads calm and finished, everything apparently
fine, the register of a room that passes a tidiness check. On the right, the exact same room, every
object unmoved and identical, now seen through a different question, with five small soft warm-red hazard
marks resting on things that were there all along: a rug at the top of the stairs, a low cupboard with a
plain bottle in it, a smoke alarm on the ceiling with its indicator gone dark, a cord across the floor,
and a pan handle turned outward over the stove. Between the two panels nothing in the room has moved; only
the question has changed, and with it what you can see. The one object that visibly differs is the smoke
alarm indicator, a small calm green light on the left, gone dark grey on the right, because a dead alarm
is the same object in either lens and only the safety question notices. Warm paper cream background, honey
light, ink-brown line work, a soft green wash on the left panel, soft warm-red for the five hazard-marks
on the right, slate for the two headings. The same tidy room, read through two questions.

- Mood: safety is a matter of the question you ask, not the state of the room; ask whether it is tidy and
  the room passes, ask whether it could hurt someone and the same room gives up five answers the first
  question could never see; nothing moved between the panels except what you are looking for.
- Avoid: drawing the right room as messier or rearranged, any object moved between the two panels, cold
  clinical light, floating typeset text, warm-red anywhere but the five hazard-marks on the right, any
  injury or accident or distressed child, a smug or scolding tone on either panel, brand names or
  recognizable products; keep the two rooms byte-for-byte identical object for object, the only
  differences the heading, the green wash, the darkened alarm indicator, and the five small marks, so the
  reveal is entirely in the changed question.

## 3. Protect people first (the triage plot and the Heinrich pyramid)

A warm editorial illustration of a calm two-axis ranking chart drawn by hand rather than as a clinical
chart, showing how to sort home hazards by two plain measures. The vertical axis, drawn by hand, runs
from minor at the bottom to serious at the top and is labeled how badly it could hurt someone; the
horizontal axis runs from unlikely on the left to imminent on the right and is labeled how soon or
likely. A few sample home hazards sit as small dots: in the top-right corner, ringed by a soft terracotta
loop and marked fix first today, a dead smoke alarm and an unlatched medicine; in the lower-left corner,
marked later, a slightly loose bannister; and in the middle, a cord across a walkway and a wobbling
shelf. To the right, a small calm inset shows a home-scaled pyramid in three gentle bands: a wide green
base of near misses, a thinner honey band of minor hurts, and a single small warm-red cap of serious
injury, with a hand-written note that removing the base shrinks the whole shape. Warm paper cream
background, ink-brown line work, soft slate for the two hand-drawn axes, one warm terracotta for the fix-
first ring, soft warm-red only for the two fix-first dots and the pyramid's single cap, soft green for
the later dot and the pyramid base. A picture of one plain idea: rank by how badly and how soon, and fix
the worst-and-soonest first.

- Mood: not all hazards are equal, so rank by two things, how badly and how soon, and fix the worst-and-
  soonest corner today while the minor-and-unlikely waits; the pyramid is why the top corner cannot wait,
  because the same pile of small hazards that mostly gives near misses is the pile that, once in a rare
  while, gives the serious one, so you remove them while they are still near misses; safety is triage, the
  worst first, without apology.
- Avoid: a busy clinical chart, gridlines everywhere, extra dots or series, cold clinical light, floating
  typeset text, an alarmist or statistical tone on the pyramid, any injury imagery or graphic harm, warm-
  red anywhere but the two fix-first dots and the single pyramid cap, drawing the loose bannister as
  neglect or the fix-first corner as a disaster, harsh contrast; keep it a calm two-axis plot and a small
  gentle pyramid, legible at a glance, the register a quiet proof rather than a warning.

## 4. Safety is love, not fear (the worry set down)

A calm, warm illustration of the chapter's emotional core, drawn as two gentle facing panels of the same
parent in the same finished home, read left to right as before and after the worrying is done, in soft
honey light. The first panel: the parent stands in the lovely home with a soft, faded grey cloud of small
worry-thoughts hovering above them, each thought a tiny quiet reminder of a hazard, the register low and
tiring, the exhausting vigilance of trying to watch for everything forever. The second panel: the same
parent in the same home, the grey cloud gone, the few real hazards now marked with small soft green dots
where they were quietly handled, the parent's shoulders eased, a single soft line reading as a breath let
out. To the right, a quieter, dimmer, unlit panel outline stands for the next chapter, where Safety gets
thorough, with two fainter outlines behind it for the chapters still to come, all kept low-contrast and
clearly ahead. Warm paper cream background, honey light, ink-brown line work, faded grey for the carried
worry, soft green for the handled hazards and the breath let out, the right group dim and unlit. The image
reads as one reassuring idea: handle the few real dangers once, with your hands, and you get to set the
worry down and never pick it up again.

- Mood: none of this is about living afraid or wrapping the home in foam, it is the opposite; you handle
  the few real dangers plainly, once, so you can stop carrying them around as worry; safety done well is
  quiet, it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with
  instead of feared; a safe home is not a fearful one but the one where you have already done the worrying,
  once, with your hands; safety is love, not fear.
- Avoid: a fearful, tense, or minefield tone, a scared or panicked parent, any injury or accident, the
  grey worry-cloud drawn dramatic or dark rather than faded and quiet, drawing the second panel as no
  lighter than the first, teaching any next-chapter content in the dim right panel, spark red anywhere on
  this calm reassuring beat, floating typeset text, hype; keep the first panel gently tiring and the second
  visibly eased, the register relief and love rather than order, a parent breathing out because the real
  dangers have been handled and can finally be set down.

## 5. The needle takes its first Safety step (the friction meter)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge dialing
from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right. This chapter the
dark live needle takes its first Safety step, a small step off Chapter 19's clearly marked Shine-complete
milestone toward the calm side, deliberately small because the earlier S's already removed most of what
this meter measures and what remains is protecting and locking in, in smaller quieter increments. Just
behind the live needle, where it rested last chapter, sits a faint dashed ghost marking Chapter 19's
Shine-complete position, so the eye reads the small ground this step covered; Chapter 18 and the earlier
Shine steps of Chapters 16 and 17 are fainter ghosts behind that. Chapter 15's Straighten-complete
milestone is a small slate marker further back, and a slate marker for the earlier Sort-complete milestone
sits further back still. A soft terracotta marker labels the reached position as the first Safety step, a
step off the Shine-complete milestone rather than a milestone of its own. A slate crosshair labeled the
goal, carrying its purpose flag, still sits further out on the green calm side, clearly ahead of the live
needle, with visible room left between them, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain
remain. Warm paper cream background, ink line work, consistent stroke weight, friendly but not childish.

- Mood: for the first time the needle moves not because daily friction dropped but because the chance of
  harm did, a few real dangers simply removed; removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction
  there is, the near miss that now cannot happen, the worry a parent can finally set down; the step is real
  and it is small on purpose, and the goal is still ahead with clear room left, because Safety has only
  begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow; a real step and a long road still ahead, both true at
  once.
- Avoid: the live needle reaching or passing the goal crosshair, no room left between the needle and the
  goal, the step drawn large or as a milestone of its own rather than a small step off Shine complete, the
  step drawn as the finish of the whole book, a completion glow or celebratory confetti, the dashed ghosts
  drawn solid or bright, hype, neon color, any sense of the whole journey being finished. The needle takes
  a small honest step on purpose, and clear room remains between it and the goal.
