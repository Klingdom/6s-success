### Slide 1 (hook)

**Your home is cleared, arranged, and spotless. It could still send someone to the hospital tonight.**

Not because you did anything wrong. Because nothing you have done so far was ever aimed at that. "By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight."

Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone. Swipe for the one new question that changes what you can see.

At a glance, finished and safe. Look again through the red marks and it is quietly full of danger. Terracotta #BC4B2A chapter kicker, slate #3C5A6B chapter label.

### Slide 2

**A clean home is not a safe home.**

"A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask."

Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing. "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not."

### Slide 3

**So safety is its own S, on purpose.**

The method began with five. "When it spread beyond the factory floor, people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1."

Something this important should never be left to chance. And "first" here is not sequence but priority: you do Safety fourth, but when a safety fix and a tidiness improvement want the same Saturday, safety wins, every time. "Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list."

[VISUAL] A quiet "5S + 1" motif on panel #FBF7EF: five S's in a row in soft #6A625A, and the sixth, SAFETY, pulled out and named in slate #3C5A6B, lifted a step above the line so it sits over the list rather than in it. A small green calm-dot #6E8B5B beside it. Slate #3C5A6B label: "the plus one, pulled out and named so it is never left to chance."

### Slide 4

**Change the question, and you change what you can see.**

For three parts of this book you asked, in various forms, is this in order. Now you ask the same home something else entirely, the rule, frozen:

"Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone."

That single swapped question is the whole tool. The professionals call teaching your eye to ask it developing safety consciousness. "What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing."

[VISUAL] The two lenses: the identical room drawn twice on panel #FBF7EF. LEFT, "Is it tidy?", the room calm and passing, small green calm-dots #6E8B5B, a green tick. RIGHT, "Could this hurt someone?", the same objects unmoved but five now lit with red hazard marks #CB4B36. Slate #3C5A6B frames and labels. Nothing moved between the panels; only the question changed.

### Slide 5

**Most hazards are perfectly tidy.**

"A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy."

We picture hazards as obvious, dramatic, dirty. A few are. "But most of the real dangers in a home are quiet and orderly and have been sitting in plain sight so long that they have become furniture to you." The lens makes them visible again.

[VISUAL] Definition card on panel #FBF7EF, the frozen hazard definition set large in Fraunces, slate #3C5A6B rules. Beside it, four tidy, ordinary objects drawn plainly, a rug, a bottle, a smoke alarm, a shelf, each perfectly neat and each carrying one small red hazard mark #CB4B36. Slate #3C5A6B label: "not a mess. A hazard."

### Slide 6

**You will find more than you expected. So you protect people first.**

The answer to overwhelming is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order, the triage, frozen:

"Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else."

Rank by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. "Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology."

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF, the frozen triage set large in Fraunces, slate #3C5A6B rules. Beneath, a small two-axis plot: how badly up the side, how soon or likely across the bottom, both axes slate #3C5A6B, a few hazards placed as red marks #CB4B36, the worst-and-soonest corner ringed "fix first" and the minor-and-unlikely corner marked "later."

### Slide 7

**Why fix the worst now, while it is only a near miss?**

"Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is exactly why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was."

[VISUAL] The pyramid, home-scaled, on panel #FBF7EF: a wide base of near misses, a thin band of minor hurts, a single cap of serious injury, drawn calmly in slate #3C5A6B with red hazard marks #CB4B36 dotting the base. A slate #3C5A6B arrow shows that removing base hazards shrinks the whole shape. No graphic injury, no statistics, just the shape. Slate #3C5A6B caption: "the aim is to never find out which one it was."

### Slide 8

**Safety is love, not fear.**

If this has started a small knot of anxiety in you, read this next part carefully.

"None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared."

"Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again."

[VISUAL] A warm, quiet scene on paper #F7F2E9: a parent sitting down in the finished home, shoulders lowered, breathing out, a child playing safely nearby. Where hazards were, small green calm-dots #6E8B5B now sit, dangers handled. No alarm anywhere in the frame. Slate #3C5A6B label: "you did the worrying once, with your hands."

### Slide 9

**Take the lens for one slow walk, and keep it small.**

Protecting people first means the first pass is not a thorough hunt; it is the worst few things, handled now. The smoke alarm you cannot remember testing: press the button, and if it is silent, a new battery goes in first, because nothing else you find will matter as much. The cupboard of cleaning bottles at a child's height: everything that could poison someone goes up high or behind a latch today. The rug that slides at the top of the stairs: a strip of grip tape underneath, or the rug gone. Even the launch pad gets the new question.

6S Tip: "Get down to a child's height and look. Most of the worst home hazards, low cupboards, dangling cords, small swallowable things, sit in a band of space adults never look at because they never live there. A lot of danger is at knee height."

[VISUAL] The same home as Slide 1, mid-walk: a hand pressing the smoke alarm's test button, the cleaning bottles moving up behind a latch, a strip of grip tape going under the rug at the stair top. Each handled danger's red mark #CB4B36 turning to a green calm-dot #6E8B5B. The launch pad in terracotta #BC4B2A, getting the new question too. The 6S Tip in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout.

### Slide 10

**Not a tidier home. A safer one.**

Nothing moved for the sake of order. A few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. "That is the fourth S in a single pass: not a tidier home, a safer one."

Quick Win: "Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing today. You just protected people first."

### Slide 11

**The needle takes its first Safety step. Not from a cleaner home, but from a safer one.**

"The needle takes its first Safety step, off the Shine-complete mark toward calm. Nothing was cleaned or tidied this time; a few real dangers were simply removed. And removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction gone: the near miss that now cannot happen, the worry a parent can finally set down. The goal is still ahead, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow."

The step is small on purpose: the earlier S's already cleared most of what the meter measures, so what remains is not big friction but important things to lock down.
