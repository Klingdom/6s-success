**Two fences to hold visually.** First, Chapter 20 opens Safety and does ONE first high-value pass on the worst and most obvious dangers; it is NOT the systematic room-by-room hazard hunt, the full inventory, or the complete safety-questions checklist (that is Chapter 21, teaser only), and it is NOT the ongoing safety systems, emergency preparation, or lasting safety consciousness of Chapter 22. Draw a slow walk that finds and handles the worst few, not an exhaustive checklist or a safety-management system. It is also NOT a documented standard or a long-term adherence framework (the later Standardize and Sustain S's). Second, do not re-teach or rebuild the neighbours: this is a different lens on the same finished home, so do not re-argue Sort, Straighten, or Shine; the home is already cleared, arranged, and kept clean and read, and the chapter only changes the question asked of it. ## Beat 2 · A clean home is not a safe home

The gap made plain: tidiness and safety overlap here and there but are not the same, and where they part ways the gap is dangerous precisely because it is invisible.

- Composition: a split card on paper ground. On the left, the spotless kitchen drawn calm and green-clean, gleaming, passing every earlier test, in soft #6A625A. On the right, the same kitchen unchanged except that the pan handle turned out over the stove edge and the low cupboard now carry small red hazard marks #CB4B36, the danger and the cleanliness simply unrelated.
- Palette: paper ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, soft #6A625A on the passing gleam, friction spark #CB4B36 on the two hazards. No verdict scolding; nobody is failing here.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "tidy and safe are different questions."
- Point: we tend to feel that a tidy home is a safe one, that order and cleanliness add up to protection, and it is a comforting feeling and it is not true. The most immaculate kitchen is not made one bit safer by its gleam if the pan handles turn out where a child can reach them. That is why the fourth S has to exist as its own step: if cleaning made a home safe, it would come free with Shine; it does not. Safety is a separate question that has to be asked on purpose, because the home will never raise it for you.
- The frozen tidy-is-not-safe reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask." Beneath it may run, in Fraunces: "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not."

## Beat 3 · Why safety is its own S

The light 5S+1 history: safety was pulled out and named so it would never be left to chance, and "first" is priority, not sequence.

- Composition: a quiet "5S + 1" motif on paper ground: five S's in a row in soft #6A625A, and the sixth, SAFETY, pulled out and named in slate #3C5A6B, lifted a step above the line so it sits over the list rather than in it, with a green calm-dot #6E8B5B beside it.
- Palette: paper ground, soft #6A625A on the five, slate #3C5A6B on the pulled-out SAFETY and the label, green calm-dot #6E8B5B beside it. Calm; a point of history, not a warning.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "the plus one, pulled out and named so it is never left to chance."
- Point: safety was meant to run through all five steps, and it does; a sorted, arranged, clean workspace really is a safer one. But "safety runs through everything" has a quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one, exactly the Chapter 17 trap. Something that matters as much as whether people get hurt cannot be left as a happy side effect, so it was pulled out and named, made a thing you do on purpose. That is why the S carries the word first, and first here is priority, not sequence: you did Safety fourth, and that order is fine, but when a safety fix and a tidiness improvement and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time.
- The frozen 5S+1 history sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "The method began with five S's. When it spread beyond the factory floor, people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1. It was not that the others ignored safety. It was that something this important should never be left to chance, and making it a step of its own is a way of refusing to assume it." The line "Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list." may run beneath in Fraunces.

## Beat 4 · The safety lens

The hero device made visual: change the question and you change what you can see. The identical room drawn twice, nothing moved, only the question changed.

- Composition: the two lenses on panel #FBF7EF. On the LEFT, headed "Is it tidy?", the room reads calm and passing, small green calm-dots #6E8B5B, a green tick. On the RIGHT, headed "Could this hurt someone?", the same objects sit unmoved but five now carry red hazard marks #CB4B36: the rug at the stair top, the low cupboard, the smoke alarm, the high shelf, the cord across the walkway.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the panel frames and the two headings, green calm-dot #6E8B5B on the passing left panel, friction spark #CB4B36 on the five hazards lit in the right panel. Calm and legible at a glance.
- Caption, from the book: "For three parts of this book you looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely: not is it tidy, but could it hurt someone. A question decides what you are able to see."
- Point: that single swapped question is the whole tool. Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm, which is, after all, perfectly tidy; ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same alarm lights up like a warning, because now you are looking for the right thing. The professionals call teaching your eye to ask it developing safety consciousness, which means something simple: a new question asked until it becomes automatic. What you are building is not fear; it is a habit of noticing. Most hazards are quiet, orderly, and long since become furniture to you, and the lens makes them visible again.
- The rule runs as a Fraunces pull-quote: "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone." The frozen hazard definition sits beneath as a Fraunces box on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy." The frozen figure caption, verbatim: "One room, two questions. Nothing between the panels changed except what you are looking for. Asked whether it is tidy, the room passes. Asked whether it could hurt someone, it gives up five answers the first question could never see."

## Beat 5 · Protect people first

The triage: ask the safety question of a whole home and you find more than you expected, so you rank by how badly and how soon, and fix the worst and soonest first.

- Composition: a two-axis triage plot on paper ground: how badly it could hurt someone up the side, how soon or likely across the bottom, both axes slate #3C5A6B. Sample hazards placed as red marks #CB4B36: a dead smoke alarm and unlatched medicine up in the worst-and-soonest corner, ringed "fix first" in slate #3C5A6B; a slightly loose bannister down in the minor-and-unlikely corner, marked "later."
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the axes, the ring, and the labels, friction spark #CB4B36 on the placed hazards. Calm; the plot is a relief, a way to make a daunting list doable, not a source of alarm.
- Slate #3C5A6B caption: "rank by how badly, and how soon."
- Point: the answer to an overwhelming list is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order. You cannot fix everything at once, and trying to is how you fix nothing, so you rank by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. A dead smoke alarm and pills a small child can open score high on both and get fixed today; a bannister that holds fine can wait its turn. This is not coldness; it is care with its priorities straight, and it gives you permission to leave the small things for later without guilt, precisely so the large things get handled now.
- The frozen triage sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else." The line "Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology." may run beneath in Fraunces.

## Beat 6 · Why act now, the pyramid

The reason the top corner of the triage cannot wait: a large pile of small hazards mostly gives near misses, and, once in a rare while, one serious injury, and you cannot tell in advance which hazard it will be. Rebuild the pyramid faithfully and calmly.

- Composition: the pyramid, home-scaled, on paper ground: a wide base of near misses, a thin band of minor hurts, a single cap of serious injury, drawn calmly in slate #3C5A6B with small red hazard marks #CB4B36 dotting the base. A slate #3C5A6B arrow shows that removing base hazards shrinks the whole shape, cap included.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the pyramid, its bands, and the arrow, friction spark #CB4B36 on the base hazards, rule #D9CDB8 on the band hairlines. No graphic injury, no statistics, only the shape. Calm and plain.
- Caption, from the book: "You remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was."
- Point: the same pile of small hazards that mostly gives near misses is the pile that, once in a rare while, gives the serious one, and you cannot tell in advance which hazard it will be. So you do not wait to find out; you take the hazards away at the base, while they are cheap to remove and have hurt no one, and the whole shape shrinks. This is not alarm; it is the calmest possible reason to handle the worst thing today, the pyramid pairing with the triage plot so that "worst and soonest first" has a reason behind it.
- The frozen pyramid sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is exactly why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was." The frozen figure caption for the combined triage-and-pyramid figure, verbatim: "Rank by two things: how badly, and how soon. The worst-and-soonest corner gets fixed today; the minor-and-unlikely can wait. The pyramid is why the top corner cannot: the same pile of small hazards that mostly gives near misses is the pile that, once in a rare while, gives the serious one."

## Beat 7 · Safety is love, not fear

The emotional core: not vigilance, not a padded home, but handling the few real dangers so you can stop carrying them as worry. This is the heart of how the fourth S is meant to feel.

- Composition: a warm, quiet scene on paper ground: a parent sitting down in the finished home, shoulders lowered, breathing out, a child playing safely nearby. Where hazards were, small green calm-dots #6E8B5B now sit, dangers handled, not watched. No alarm anywhere in the frame, no foam, no padded corners drawn.
- Palette: paper ground, green calm-dot #6E8B5B on the handled dangers, soft #6A625A on the labels, slate #3C5A6B on the caption. Warm and calm; relief, not pressure.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "you did the worrying once, with your hands, so you do not keep doing it with your mind."
- Point: the goal of safety is not vigilance, which is exhausting and does not scale; the attempt just turns a home into a place of low, constant dread. The goal is the opposite: deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and never again have to track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach; you put the working battery in the alarm, and the whole category of "what if there is a fire and we do not wake up" simply closes. That is why safety, of all the S's, is the one most rooted in love rather than order: you are quietly making sure the people you live with come to no harm, and then you get to relax inside it. Draw calm and love, never alarm.
- The frozen safety-is-love line sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared." The lines "Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again." and "A safe home is not a fearful one. It is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands, so you do not have to keep doing it with your mind." may run beneath in Fraunces.

- Palette: paper ground, friction spark #CB4B36 on the hazards still being handled, green calm-dot #6E8B5B on the ones just closed off, terracotta #BC4B2A on the launch pad. Warm; steady hands, no drama.
- Caption, from the book: "A few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. That is Safety in its smallest complete form: not a tidier home, a safer one."
- Point: protecting people first means the first pass is not a thorough hunt; it is the worst few things, handled now. The smoke alarm you cannot remember testing gets a new battery first, because nothing else you find will matter as much; the cleaning bottles at a child's height go up or behind a latch today; the rug that slides at the top of the stairs gets a strip of grip tape, or goes. Even the launch pad gets the new question. It was not a deep clean and it was not a tidy; the home was already both, and nothing moved for the sake of order. Fix what the lens finds at the top of the list, and leave the rest for the thorough pass that is coming. Do not draw an exhaustive checklist; draw a slow walk handling the worst few.
- The 6S Tip sits in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Get down to a child's height and look. Most of the worst home hazards, low cupboards, dangling cords, small swallowable things, sit in a band of space adults never look at because they never live there. A lot of danger is at knee height." The Quick Win sits in a green #6E8B5B bordered callout, verbatim: "Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing today.
