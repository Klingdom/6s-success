### Slide 2 · A clean home is not a safe home

- Stand in the home you have built across this book and look at it with plain pride. It is cleared of what did not belong; everything that stayed has a place; it is clean, and it stays clean, on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight. Not because you did anything wrong, but because nothing you have done so far was ever aimed at that. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten where it should live, Shine whether it was clean, and not one of those is the question that notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, or the medicine at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm quietly dead since spring.
- We tend to feel that a tidy home is a safe one, that order and cleanliness add up to protection. It is a comforting feeling and it is not true. Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap can be dangerous precisely because it is invisible. The most immaculate kitchen is not made one bit safer by its gleam if the pan handles turn out over the stove edge where a child can reach them. The danger and the cleanliness are simply unrelated.
- That is why the fourth S has to exist as its own step. If cleaning made a home safe, it would come free with Shine; it does not. Safety is a separate question that has to be asked on purpose, because the home will never raise it for you. The tidy-is-not-safe reframe, verbatim: "A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask." And, verbatim: "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not."

### Slide 4 · Why safety is its own S

- There is a small piece of history worth knowing, because it is the story of the very "plus one" that makes this book about six S's and not five. The 5S+1 history, verbatim: "The method began with five S's. When it spread beyond the factory floor, people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1. It was not that the others ignored safety. It was that something this important should never be left to chance, and making it a step of its own is a way of refusing to assume it."
- Safety was meant to run through all five steps, and it does; a sorted, arranged, clean workspace really is a safer one. But "safety runs through everything" has a quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one, exactly the trap you met with the cleaning in Chapter 17. Something that matters as much as whether people get hurt cannot be left as a happy side effect of the other steps, so it was pulled out and named, made a thing you do on purpose rather than a thing you hope falls out of the rest.
- That is why this S comes with the word first in its title, and first here is priority, not sequence. You did Safety fourth, after the home was cleared and arranged and clean, and that order is fine. But when a safety fix and a tidiness improvement and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time, without a debate, because the downside on the other two is inconvenience and the downside on safety is somebody you love getting hurt. Verbatim: "Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list."

### Slide 5 · The safety lens

- So how do you see what you have been walking past for years? You change the question. For three parts of this book you looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely, the rule, verbatim: "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone." That single swapped question is the whole tool, and it is more powerful than it sounds, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm, which is, after all, perfectly tidy; ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same alarm lights up like a warning, because now you are looking for the right thing.
- The professionals call this developing safety consciousness, which sounds grand and means something simple: teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. Verbatim: "What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing." And here is what the lens is looking for, the hazard definition, verbatim: "A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy."
- We picture hazards as obvious, dramatic, dirty. A few are. But most of the real dangers in a home are quiet and orderly and have been sitting in plain sight so long that they have become furniture to you. The lens makes them visible again. Nothing in the home has to move for the lens to work; only the question changes, and the same room gives up a set of answers the first question could never see.

### Slide 6 · Protect people first

- Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and the honest first feeling is often a small dismay. The answer to that is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order. The triage, verbatim: "Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else." You cannot fix everything at once, and trying to is how you fix nothing, so you rank.
- Rank by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. A dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a small child can open score high on both and get fixed today; a slightly loose bannister that holds fine can wait its turn. This is not coldness; it is care with its priorities straight. Verbatim: "Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology."
- The triage is what turns a daunting list into a doable one. It gives you permission to leave the small things for later without guilt, precisely so the large things get handled now, and it means the first pass is short: the worst few, today, and the rest for the thorough hunt to come.

### Slide 7 · Why act now: the pyramid

- Why fix the worst now, while it is still only a near miss? Because of a pattern safety work has long noticed. The pyramid, home-scaled, verbatim: "Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is exactly why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was."
- The pyramid is the reason the top corner of the triage cannot wait. The same pile of small hazards that mostly gives near misses is the pile that, once in a rare while, gives the serious one, and you cannot tell in advance which hazard it will be. So you do not wait to find out; you take the hazards away at the base, while they are cheap to remove and have hurt no one, and the whole shape shrinks. This is not alarm. It is arithmetic, and it is the calmest possible reason to handle the worst thing today.

### Slide 8 · Safety is love, not fear

- If this chapter has started a small knot of anxiety in you, a sense that your home is a minefield and you have been negligent, stop and read this next part carefully, because it is the heart of how the fourth S is meant to feel. Verbatim: "None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared."
- The goal of safety is not vigilance. Vigilance is exhausting and it does not scale; you cannot watch everything forever, and the attempt just turns a home into a place of low, constant dread. The goal is the opposite: deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and then you never again have to track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach. You put the working battery in the alarm, and the whole category of "what if there is a fire and we do not wake up" simply closes. Verbatim: "Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again."
- That is why safety, of all the S's, is the one most rooted in love rather than order. You are not tidying here; you are quietly, unglamorously making sure the people you live with come to no harm in the place that is supposed to be their safest, and then, having made sure, you get to relax inside it. Verbatim: "A safe home is not a fearful one. It is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands, so you do not have to keep doing it with your mind."

### Slide 9 · A first safety pass

- So make a start, today, and keep it small, because protecting people first means the first pass is not a thorough hunt; it is the worst few things, handled now. Take the lens for a slow walk through the home you know so well you have stopped seeing it, and let it show you the dangers hiding in plain sight. The smoke alarm you cannot remember ever testing: press the button, and if it is silent, a new battery goes in before anything else on the list, because nothing else you find will matter as much. The cupboard under the sink, tidy and ordinary, full of cleaning bottles at the exact height of a small child: everything that could poison someone goes up high or behind a latch today. The rug at the top of the stairs that has always slid a little: a strip of grip tape underneath, or the rug gone. Even the launch pad, that first small victory by the door, gets the new question.
- Notice what that walk was and was not. It was not a deep clean and it was not a tidy; the home was already both. Nothing moved for the sake of order. A few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. That is Safety in its smallest complete form: one lens, one walk, the worst dangers handled first, and a handful of the ways your home could have hurt someone quietly closed off for good. Fix what the lens finds at the top of the list, and leave the rest for the thorough pass that is coming.
- 6S Tip, verbatim: "Get down to a child's height and look. Most of the worst home hazards, low cupboards, dangling cords, small swallowable things, sit in a band of space adults never look at because they never live there. A lot of danger is at knee height." Quick Win, verbatim: "Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing today. You just protected people first."

### Slide 11 · Safety, only begun

- Stand in your home and take in what one lens did. You looked at a finished, cared-for home through the one question the first three S's do not carry, and it showed you a handful of ways the place could hurt the people in it. You put those findings in order by harm, you handled the worst ones today, and, doing it, you traded a little vague background worry for the solid quiet of a few real dangers dealt with. Verbatim: "That is the fourth S in a single pass: not a tidier home, a safer one." A cluttered counter is an annoyance; a cabinet a child can open is a catastrophe waiting for a quiet afternoon, and you closed a few of those off for good.
- This did not cost you a clean or a tidy, and it did not cost you your peace. It bought peace: every hazard removed is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again. The most important S in the book asked the least tidying of any of them, and it gave back the most quiet. That is the shape of the whole S, and it is worth a moment's satisfaction before the book turns to the thorough part.
- One Idea to Keep, verbatim: "Protect people first. Walk the home asking not whether it is tidy but whether it could hurt someone, fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest before anything else, and you trade a low hum of worry for the quiet of a home whose real dangers have been handled."

### Slide 12 · Safety begun, next the hazard hunt

- A first pass catches the dangers that are obvious once you are finally looking, and it will walk right past the ones that are not, and that is deliberate. The medicine you thought of is handled; the button battery in the old remote a toddler could swallow, the space heater too close to the curtain, the extension cord run under the rug where it can overheat, those take a calmer, more systematic search than a first walk gives. That search is the next chapter, and past it lies the work of making safety not a one-time sweep but a lasting part of how the household lives, which is the chapter after.
- For now, hold on to the lens, because it works in any room, on any day: not is it tidy, but could it hurt someone. Do not build the thorough hunt here; this beat opens Safety and points ahead. Chapter 20, Safety: Protect People First, opens Part Six and takes the first Safety step; Chapter 21, Find and Remove Hazards, makes Safety thorough with a calm, room-by-room search for the hazards a first pass always misses.
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Treating every hazard as equal and trying to fix them all at once, so you fix none. Safety is triage, not a spreadsheet. The dead smoke alarm cannot wait; the slightly loose bannister can. Rank by harm, and start at the top."
