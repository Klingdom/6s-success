# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Safety: Protect People First

*Chapter 20, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter opens by standing in the finished home and saying, "By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight." Think of your own home as you have made it so far. What is one thing in it that is perfectly tidy and never messy, and yet, asked the right question, could hurt someone?

2. The reframe the whole part turns on is that "A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone." Had you quietly assumed that a tidy home was a safe one? Where in your home do tidy and safe come apart most?

3. The chapter says a hazard is different from a mess: "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not." What danger has been sitting in plain sight in your home so long that, in the chapter's words, it has become furniture to you?

4. On why safety earns its own S: "The method began with five S's ... safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1 ... something this important should never be left to chance." The chapter warns that "safety runs through everything" has a failure mode, that what belongs to everyone can end up owned by no one. Where else in your household has an important job gone undone because it was nobody's in particular?

5. "First" is described as priority, not sequence: "Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list." When a safety fix, a tidiness improvement, and an efficiency tweak have all wanted the same Saturday in your home, which usually won, and would you order them differently now?

6. The hero device is one swapped question: "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone." Walk one room in your mind twice, once asking is it tidy and once asking could it hurt someone. What does the second question show you that the first one slid right past?

7. The chapter calls the habit developing safety consciousness, and insists "What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing." Does the idea of teaching your eye a new question, rather than adding a new worry, change how the fourth S feels to you?

8. The hazard definition ends with a line the chapter asks you to read twice: "It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy." Which of your own tidy, orderly, long-ignored things, a cord, a low cupboard, a heavy object up high, does that line make you look at differently?

9. The triage is "Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else," ranked by two measures, how badly and how soon. Take a handful of dangers you can already name. Which one lands in the worst-and-soonest corner, and which can honestly wait its turn?

10. Heinrich's pyramid is the calm reason to act now: "a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one." Does knowing you cannot pick the dangerous one in advance make you more willing to remove the small ones while they are still harmless?

11. The emotional core is that "Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared." Where in your home are you currently doing the worrying with your mind, tracking something, watching for it, that you could do once with your hands and then set down?

12. The chapter reframes the goal as the opposite of vigilance: "Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again." Which single worry would you most like to close for good, and what one action would close it?

13. The Quick Win asks you to prove the whole idea in one move: "Walk one room and find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone ... and fix that one thing today. You just protected people first." Which room would you walk first, and what do you already suspect the one thing will be?

14. The friction meter takes its first Safety step, off the Shine-complete mark, and the chapter is honest that it is small: "removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is," but "the goal is still ahead, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow." Standing at the opening of the fourth S, with the home cleared, arranged, kept clean, and now a little safer, what does it mean to you that a home that is clean "is not the same as a home that is also safe," as the book turns to Chapter 21 and the thorough hazard hunt?
