# Back Cover Copy: Safety: Protect People First

## Back-cover text

Stand in the home you have built across this book and look at it with plain pride. It is cleared of what did not belong, everything that stayed has a place, and it is clean, and it stays clean, on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight. Not because you did anything wrong, but because nothing you have done so far was ever aimed at that. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten where it should live, Shine whether it was clean; not one of them notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, the medicine at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm quietly dead since spring. "A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask."

Chapter 20 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the first chapter of Part Six, and it opens Safety, the fourth of the six S's, the "plus one" that made the method 5S+1 and this book about six S's and not five. For nineteen chapters the book was about the state of the room; Safety turns to the people in it, which is a different thing entirely and, when the two ever compete, the more important one. It teaches no new way to tidy, because "It is the most important S in the book, and it asks the least tidying of any of them." Its whole tool is one swapped question, the rule, three words wide once you strip it down: "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone." Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm; ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same alarm lights up. The professionals call learning that question developing safety consciousness, and what it builds is not fear. It is a habit of noticing. "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not."

Then it says the kind thing, because ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip from useful into overwhelming. The answer is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order. "Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else." Rank by two plain measures, how badly and how soon, fix the dead alarm and the reachable medicine today, and let the slightly loose bannister wait its turn, because "Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology." And none of it is about living afraid. "None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared." Because a part opener that removes real dangers, the friction meter takes its first Safety step off the marked SHINE COMPLETE milestone toward the goal, small but honest, because nothing was cleaned or tidied this time, and yet a few real dangers are gone, and removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is. The goal is still ahead, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow. Today, walk one room, find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, and fix that one thing. You just protected people first. Keep the question close with **The Safety Lens card** and work the triage with **The Protect People First worksheet**. Next, Chapter 21 · Find and Remove Hazards.
