# 6S Success: Home Edition (Sales Page Copy)

## By every measure so far, your home is finished. It could still send someone to the hospital tonight.

Stand in the home you have built across this book and look at it with plain pride. It is cleared of what did not belong, everything that stayed has a place, and it is clean, and it stays clean, on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight.

Not because you did anything wrong. Because nothing you have done so far was ever aimed at that. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten where it should live, Shine whether it was clean; not one of them notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, the medicine at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm quietly dead since spring. "A cluttered counter is an annoyance. A cabinet a child can open is a catastrophe waiting for a quiet afternoon."

## Welcome to the fourth S.

Chapter 20 is the first chapter of Part Six, and it opens Safety, the fourth of the six S's, the "plus one" American practice added to 5S. For nineteen chapters the book was about the state of the room. Safety is about the people in it. It teaches no new way to tidy and rebuilds no list; in fact "It is the most important S in the book, and it asks the least tidying of any of them." It hands you one new question to ask of your whole finished home, a short triage for what the question finds, and one first pass on the worst dangers today.

It is a single swapped question. And it is what turns a home you have made clean into a home that is also safe.

## A clean home is not a safe home.

Let me say plainly the thing this whole part turns on, because it runs against a feeling most of us carry without examining it. We tend to feel that a tidy home is a safe one, that order and cleanliness add up to protection, and it is a comforting feeling and it is not true.

> A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask.

Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap can be dangerous precisely because it is invisible. "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not." That is why the fourth S has to exist as its own step: if cleaning made a home safe, it would come free with Shine. It does not.

## The safety lens: one new question.

So how do you see what you have been walking past for years? You change the question. For three parts of this book you looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely.

> Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone.

That single swapped question is the whole tool, and it is more powerful than it sounds, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm; ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same alarm lights up. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness: teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. "What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing." And here is what the lens goes looking for.

> A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy.

## Protect people first.

Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip from useful into overwhelming. The answer is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order, because not all hazards are equal.

> Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else.

Rank what you find by two plain measures, and each is a lever, and none of them is guilt.

- **How badly · the worst harm goes to the top** · a dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a small child can open could cause terrible harm, so they go to the very top of the list and get fixed today
- **How soon · the most likely and imminent comes first** · a fall down a staircase is precisely the worst-and-soonest kind of hazard, while a bannister that is slightly loose but holds fine can wait its turn without apology
- **Why act now · Heinrich's pyramid** · a large pile of small hazards gives mostly near misses and, rarely, one serious injury, and you cannot tell which is which, so you remove them while they are still harmless, because the cost of doing so is so low there is nothing to weigh

## You do not have a minefield. You have a home.

Stop on how the fourth S is meant to feel, because it is kinder than the fear the subject usually carries.

> None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared.

The goal of safety is not vigilance, which is exhausting and does not scale. The goal is the opposite: deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. "Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again." A home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space; the fix is a new question and a short triage, not guilt. "A safe home is not a fearful one. It is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands, so you do not have to keep doing it with your mind."

## Triage, do not catalogue.

One caution, so the pass stays the thing that makes a home safer rather than the thing that never gets started.

> Treating every hazard as equal and trying to fix them all at once, so you fix none. Safety is triage, not a spreadsheet. The dead smoke alarm cannot wait; the slightly loose bannister can. Rank by harm, and start at the top.

The first pass is not a thorough hunt; it is the worst few things, handled now. Fix what the lens finds at the top of the list, and leave the rest for the thorough pass that is coming in the next chapter. A pass you would try to make exhaustive is a pass you will not finish, and a pass you do not finish protects no one.

## The needle takes its first Safety step.

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, and for a reason it has never moved for before. Every step until now, the needle traveled because the daily friction of living there went down. This time nothing about the daily friction changed at all; the home is no cleaner. And yet the needle steps toward calm, because a few real dangers are now gone, and "removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is." Not the friction you feel every day, but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving.

> Protect people first.

The step is real and it is small, and both are true on purpose. Small, because the earlier S's already removed most of what this meter measures, so what remains is not big friction to clear but important things to lock down. Real, because a hazard removed is a genuine and permanent gain. Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone is the passed marker just behind the live needle now; the goal, the crosshair out on the calm side, is still ahead, because Safety has barely begun, with a thorough hazard hunt and a whole layer of lasting safety still to come, and Standardize and Sustain beyond them.

Today, walk one room, find the single thing most likely to seriously hurt someone, the unlatched medicine, the rug that slides at the top of the stairs, the smoke alarm with a dead battery, and fix that one thing. You just protected people first. "That is the fourth S in a single pass: not a tidier home, a safer one."
