# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

Stand in the home you have built across this book and look at it with plain pride: cleared, arranged, clean, and staying clean on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. Chapter 20 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where you learn that it could still send someone to the hospital tonight, and what to do about it.

"Safety: Protect People First" is the first chapter of Part Six, and it opens Safety, the fourth of the six S's, the "plus one" American practice added to 5S. For nineteen chapters the book was about the state of the room; Safety turns to the people in it, which is a different thing entirely and, when the two ever compete, the more important one. It teaches no new way to tidy. In fact, "It is the most important S in the book, and it asks the least tidying of any of them." Not because you did anything wrong across the first three S's, but because nothing you did there was ever aimed at this. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten where it should live, Shine whether it was clean; not one of them notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, the medicine at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm quietly dead since spring. Those things were never messy, never misplaced, never dirty. They passed every test the first three S's could give, and they were dangerous the whole time.

The chapter names the thing the whole part turns on, because it runs against a feeling most of us carry without examining it. "A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask." Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap can be dangerous precisely because it is invisible. That is why the fourth S has to exist as its own step: if cleaning made a home safe, it would come free with Shine, and it does not. "A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not."

There is a small piece of history worth knowing here, because it is the story of the very "plus one" that makes this book about six S's and not five. "The method began with five S's. When it spread beyond the factory floor, people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1. It was not that the others ignored safety. It was that something this important should never be left to chance, and making it a step of its own is a way of refusing to assume it." The original method, born on the factory floors of Toyota, meant safety to run through all the steps, and it did, but "safety runs through everything" has a quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one. So safety was pulled out and named. That is why this S comes with the word first in its title: not first in sequence, because you did it fourth, and that order is fine, but first in priority. When a safety fix, a tidiness improvement, and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time. "Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list."

So how do you see what you have been walking past for years? You change the question. The hero of the chapter is the safety lens, one swapped question asked of the whole home. "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone." That single question is the whole tool, and it is more powerful than it sounds, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask "is this tidy?" and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm, which is, after all, perfectly tidy; ask "could this hurt someone?" and the same alarm lights up like a warning. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness, which means something simple: teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. "What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing." And here is what the lens goes looking for, straight from the method and shrunk to fit a home. "A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy." Read that last line twice, because it is the thing people miss. "But most of the real dangers in a home are quiet and orderly and have been sitting in plain sight so long that they have become furniture to you."

Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip quickly from useful into overwhelming. The answer is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order. "Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else." Rank what you find by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. A dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a small child can open score high on both, so they go to the very top and get fixed today; a bannister that is slightly loose but holds fine can wait its turn. This is not being casual about the bannister; it is refusing to let it, and forty other small things, crowd out the two that could actually hurt someone this afternoon. "Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology." And the reason you act on the top of that list now rather than someday comes straight from a pattern safety workers have watched for a century. "Safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts, and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is exactly why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was." You are not fixing the alarm because you expect a fire tonight; you are fixing it because the cost of removing a hazard while it is harmless is so low, and the cost of guessing wrong so high, that there is nothing to weigh.

If this has started a small knot of anxiety, a sense that your home is a minefield and you have been negligent, the chapter stops and says the kindest thing in the book. "None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so that you can stop carrying them around as worry. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out, because the things that could go wrong have been dealt with instead of feared." The goal of safety is not vigilance, which is exhausting and does not scale; the goal is the opposite, to deal with the real dangers so thoroughly and so early that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and never again track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach. "Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again." That is why safety, of all the S's, is the one most rooted in love rather than order. "A safe home is not a fearful one. It is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands, so you do not have to keep doing it with your mind."

Then you make a start today, and keep it small, because protecting people first means the first pass is the worst few things handled now, not a thorough hunt. The smoke alarm you cannot remember testing: press the button, and if it is silent, a new battery goes in before anything else, because nothing else you find will matter as much. The cupboard under the sink, full of cleaning bottles at a small child's height: everything that could poison someone goes up high or behind a latch today. The rug at the top of the stairs that has always slid: a strip of grip tape underneath, or the rug gone. Even the launch pad, that first small victory by the door, gets the new question. Notice what the walk was and was not: not a deep clean and not a tidy, the home was already both, and a few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. "That is the fourth S in a single pass: not a tidier home, a safer one."

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, and for a reason it has never moved for before. Every step until now, the needle traveled because the daily friction of living there went down. This time nothing about the daily friction changed at all; the home is no cleaner. And yet the needle steps toward calm, because a few real dangers are now gone, and "removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is." Not the friction you feel every day, but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving. The step is real and it is small, and both are true on purpose. Small, because the earlier S's already removed most of what this meter measures, so what remains is not big friction to clear but important things to lock down. Real, because a hazard removed is a genuine and permanent gain. Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone is the passed marker just behind the live needle now; the goal, the crosshair out on the calm side, is still ahead, and it should be, because Safety has barely begun, with a thorough hazard hunt and a whole layer of lasting safety still to come, and Standardize and Sustain beyond them. You changed the question, and that changes everything that follows. You put the findings in order by harm, handled the worst ones today, and traded a little vague background worry for the solid quiet of a few real dangers dealt with. What you have not done is the thorough part, and that is deliberate: a first pass catches the dangers that are obvious once you are finally looking, and walks right past the ones that are not. For now, hold on to the lens, because it works in any room, on any day: not is it tidy, but could it hurt someone.
