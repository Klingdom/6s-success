## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
By every measure the first three S's gave you, the home is finished, and it is good, and it could still send someone to the hospital tonight. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten where it should live, Shine whether it was clean; not one of them notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, the medicine at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm quietly dead since spring. A clean home is not a safe home, because tidy and safe are different questions, and a hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does: it sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not. That is why there is a fourth S, and why it is a turn, not an afterthought: for nineteen chapters the book was about the state of the room; Safety is about the people in it. Chapter 20 tells the light 5S+1 history of why safety was pulled out and named its own S (something this important should never be left to chance), and it hands you one hero device, the safety lens: change the question, and ask of the whole home not whether it is tidy but whether it could hurt someone. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness, teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. Paired with the lens is one triage, protect people first: rank what you find by two plain measures, how badly it could hurt someone and how soon or how likely it is to, and fix the worst and soonest first. Heinrich's pyramid, home-scaled and referenced lightly, is the calm reason to act now: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and, every so often, one serious injury, and you can rarely tell in advance which one, so you remove them while they are still only near misses. Safety is love, not fear: handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so you can stop carrying them around as worry. Then one first high-value pass, kept small: the smoke alarm gets tested and a fresh battery, the cleaning bottles go up high or behind a latch, the rug at the top of the stairs gets grip tape or goes, and even the launch pad gets the new question. Because a part opener that removes real dangers, the friction meter takes its FIRST Safety step off the Shine-complete milestone toward the goal, small but honest, because nothing was cleaned or tidied but the room's exposure to harm dropped, and removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction gone. Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone is now the passed marker just behind the live needle, and the goal is still ahead, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow. Hands off to Chapter 21 · Find and Remove Hazards, which makes Safety thorough, a calm, room-by-room search for the hazards a first pass always misses.

## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule (hero): **Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone.**
- The triage (support line): **Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else.**
- The definition box: **A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy.**
- The tidy-is-not-safe reframe: **A clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean. None of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. Safety is the question the first three S's never ask.**
- One Idea to Keep: **Protect people first. Walk the home asking not whether it is tidy but whether it could hurt someone, fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest before anything else, and you trade a low hum of worry for the quiet of a home whose real dangers have been handled.**
