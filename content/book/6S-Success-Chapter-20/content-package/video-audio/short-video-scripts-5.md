## SHORT 1: A clean home is not a safe home
**Topic: the reframe the whole part turns on, tidy and safe are different questions. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Your home is spotless. It could still send someone to the hospital tonight.

**Script:**
Stand in the home you have cleared, arranged, and cleaned, and look at it with plain pride. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight. Not because you did anything wrong. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten asked where it should live, Shine asked whether it was clean. Three good questions, and not one of them notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, the medicine at a toddler's eye level, or the smoke alarm that has been quietly dead since spring. Those things were never messy, never misplaced, never dirty. A clean home is not a safe home. Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap is dangerous precisely because it is invisible. A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not.

- 0s: "Your home is spotless."
- 12s: "It could still hurt someone tonight."
- 26s: "A clean home is not a safe home."
- 40s: "Tidy and safe are different questions."

## SHORT 2: Why safety is its own S
**Topic: the 5S+1 history, and first is priority not sequence. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Why is it six S's and not five? Because of the one thing cleaning can never do.

**Script:**
Here is a small piece of history worth knowing. The method began with five S's, born on the factory floors of Toyota, and safety was meant to run through all of them. And it did. But as it spread, people saw the quiet failure mode: safety runs through everything, so it can end up owned by no one. Something that matters as much as whether people get hurt cannot be left as a happy side effect of the other steps. So it was pulled out and named, given its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S plus 1. And that is why this S comes with the word first in its title. It is not first in sequence, you did it fourth, and that order is fine. It is first in priority. When a safety fix and a tidiness improvement and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time. Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The method began with five S's."
- 14s: "Safety runs through everything, so it can end up owned by no one."
- 28s: "So it was pulled out and named. The plus one."
- 40s: "First in priority, not in sequence."

**[VISUAL] direction:** Five S-tiles with safety threaded faintly through all of them, then pulled out and set down as its own sixth tile, the plus one, gaining a clear outline. A light nod to the Toyota Production System behind it. Then a three-way weekend choice, safety stepping above the list. Warm, calm.

## SHORT 3: The safety lens
**Topic: the hero device, one new question changes what you can see. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** You have walked past the most dangerous thing in your home for years. Here is why.

**Script:**
Here is how you see what you have walked past for years. You change the question. For three parts of this book you looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely: could this hurt someone. That single swapped question is the whole tool, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask is this tidy, and your eye slides right over the dead smoke alarm, which is, after all, perfectly tidy. Ask could this hurt someone, and the same alarm lights up like a warning. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness, which just means teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing. And here is the definition: a hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The safety lens."
- 12s: "One room. Two questions. Nothing moves but what you see."
- 30s: "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone."
- 42s: "Most hazards are perfectly tidy."

**[VISUAL] direction:** The identical living room drawn twice, side by side. On the left, "Is it tidy?", a calm green wash, everything fine. On the right, "Could this hurt someone?", the same objects unchanged but now marked with small red hazard marks: the rug at the stair top, a low cupboard with a bottle, the dark smoke alarm, a cord, a pan handle turned out. Nothing moved between the panels. Warm line-and-flat-color style.

## SHORT 4: Protect people first
**Topic: the triage, rank by how badly and how soon, and the pyramid, why act now. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** You found ten hazards and now you are overwhelmed. Do this instead of nothing.

**Script:**
Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip from useful into overwhelming. The answer is not to look away. It is to put the findings in order, because not all hazards are equal. Rank by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. A dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a child can open score high on both, so they go to the very top and get fixed today. A slightly loose bannister that holds fine can wait its turn. Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else. And here is why you act now, not someday. Safety workers have watched a pattern for a century: a large pile of small hazards gives mostly near misses and, every so often, one serious injury, and you can rarely tell in advance which one. So you remove them while they are still near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "You found ten hazards. Now what?"
- 14s: "Rank by two things: how badly, and how soon."
- 30s: "Protect people first: worst and soonest first."
- 42s: "Clear them while they are still near misses."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A two-axis plot, how badly it could hurt someone (up), how soon or likely (across). A dead smoke alarm and unlatched medicine top-right, ringed "fix first"; a slightly loose bannister lower-left, "later"; a cord and a wobbling shelf in the middle. A small pyramid inset in the corner, a wide base of near misses and a single serious cap, the base shrinking as hazards are removed. Calm slate axes, one warm accent.

## SHORT 5: The needle takes its first Safety step
**Topic: the honest friction-meter first step, and safety is love not fear. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** For the first time, the needle moves without anything being cleaned.

**Script:**
Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, and it moves for a reason it has never moved for before. Every step until now, the needle traveled because the home got less cluttered or cleaner, because the daily friction went down. This time nothing about the daily friction changed at all. The home is no tidier than it was this morning. And yet the needle steps toward calm, because a few real dangers are now gone, and removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is. Not the friction you feel every day, but the friction of the worst day, the one you are keeping from ever arriving. Because none of this is about living afraid. It is the opposite. You handle the few real dangers plainly, once, so you can stop carrying them around as worry. Every hazard you remove is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out. The step is small, and the goal is still ahead, because Safety has only begun.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "Nothing was cleaned this time."
- 14s: "A few real dangers were simply removed."
- 28s: "Safety is love, not fear."
- 40s: "The needle takes its first Safety step."

**[VISUAL] direction:** The friction meter taking a small first Safety step. The live needle has moved just past Chapter 19's marked SHINE COMPLETE tick, a short step toward calm. Chapter 19's milestone is a small passed marker just behind; earlier Shine steps and the Straighten-complete and Sort-complete milestones fainter still. The step is deliberately small. The GOAL crosshair, tagged "purpose", still ahead on the green calm side. A parent breathing out beside it.
