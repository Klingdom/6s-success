## SECTION 1: A CLEAN HOME IS NOT A SAFE HOME

[HOST]
Let me say plainly the thing this whole part turns on, because it runs against a feeling most of us carry without examining it.

[VISUAL: The four question-cards laid in a row: belonged (Sort), where (Straighten), clean (Shine), each ticked, and a fourth card, could this hurt someone, still blank. Beside them, the tidy objects the first three questions passed over: the dead smoke alarm, the pan handle turned out over the stove, the medicine at a toddler's height.]

[HOST, voice over]
A clean home is not a safe home. We feel that a tidy home is a safe one, and it is a comforting feeling and it is not true. Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap can be dangerous precisely because it is invisible. The most immaculate kitchen is not made one bit safer by its gleam if the pan handles turn out over the edge of the stove.

[HOST, on camera]
And this is why the fourth S has to be its own step. If cleaning made a home safe, it would come free with Shine. It does not. Safety is a separate question that has to be asked on purpose, because the home will never raise it for you. A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does. It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not.

## SECTION 2: WHY SAFETY IS ITS OWN S

[HOST]
There is a small piece of history worth knowing, because it is the story of the very plus one that makes this book about six S's and not five.

[VISUAL: Five S-tiles with safety threaded faintly through all of them, then pulled out and set down as its own sixth tile, the plus one, gaining a clear outline. A light nod to a factory floor and the Toyota Production System behind it. Then a small weekend scene: a safety fix, a tidiness improvement, and an efficiency tweak, and safety stepping above the list.]

[HOST, voice over]
The method began with five S's, born on the factory floors of Toyota, and safety was meant to run through all of them. And it did. But people noticed the quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one. So safety was pulled out and named, made a thing you do on purpose, the plus one in what was first called 5S plus 1.

[HOST, on camera]
And that is why this S comes with the word first in its title. It is not first in sequence, you did it fourth, and that order is fine. It is first in priority. When a safety fix and a tidiness improvement and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time. Protecting people is not one item on the list competing with the others. It sits above the list.

## SECTION 3: THE SAFETY LENS

[HOST]
So how do you see what you have been walking past for years? You change the question.

[VISUAL: The identical living room drawn twice, side by side. On the left, "Is it tidy?", a soft green wash, everything apparently fine. On the right, "Could this hurt someone?", the same objects unchanged but now carrying small red hazard marks: the rug at the stair top, a low cupboard with a bottle, the dark smoke alarm, a cord across the floor, a pan handle turned outward. Nothing moved between the panels. Beside it, the definition box.]

[HOST, voice over]
Here is the rule. Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone. That single swapped question is the whole tool, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask is this tidy, and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm, which is, after all, perfectly tidy. Ask could this hurt someone, and the same alarm lights up. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness: teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing.]

[HOST, on camera]
And here is the definition, straight from the method and shrunk to fit a home. A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy. Read that last line twice, because it is the thing people miss.

## SECTION 4: PROTECT PEOPLE FIRST

[HOST]
Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip from useful into overwhelming.

[VISUAL: A two-axis plot, how badly it could hurt someone (up), how soon or likely (across). A dead smoke alarm and unlatched medicine top-right, ringed "fix first"; a slightly loose bannister lower-left, "later"; a cord and a wobbling shelf in the middle. A small pyramid inset in the corner: a wide base of near misses, a single serious cap, the base shrinking as hazards are removed.]

[HOST, voice over]
The answer to overwhelming is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order. Rank by two plain measures: how badly it could hurt someone, and how soon or how likely it is to. A dead smoke alarm and pills a small child can open score high on both, so they go to the top and get fixed today; a slightly loose bannister that holds fine can wait its turn. And here is the triage. Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Safety is triage: the worst first, on purpose, without apology.]

[HOST, on camera]
And here is why you act now. Safety workers have watched a pattern for a century, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards gives mostly near misses and, every so often, one serious injury, and you can rarely tell in advance which one. So you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was. The cost of removing a hazard while it is harmless is so low that there is nothing to weigh.

## SECTION 5: SAFETY IS LOVE, NOT FEAR

[HOST]
If this chapter has started a small knot of anxiety in you, stop and read this carefully, because it is the heart of how the fourth S is meant to feel.

[VISUAL: A cabinet latch clicking shut once. A fresh battery going into a smoke alarm, its light coming back on. A parent breathing out in a quiet, warm room, a small worry set down beside them and not picked up again.]

[HOST, voice over]
None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. The goal is not vigilance; vigilance is exhausting and it does not scale. The goal is to deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and never again track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach. You put a working battery in the alarm, and the whole category of worry simply closes.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Every hazard you remove is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again.]

[HOST, on camera]
That is why safety, of all the S's, is the one most rooted in love rather than order. A safe home is not a fearful one. It is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands, so you do not have to keep doing it with your mind.

## SECTION 6: A FIRST SAFETY PASS

[HOST]
So make a start today, and keep it small, because protecting people first means the first pass is the worst few things, handled now, not a thorough hunt.

[VISUAL: The lens taken for a slow walk. A finger presses the smoke alarm button, and a fresh battery goes in first. Cleaning bottles under the sink move up high or behind a latch. Grip tape goes under the rug at the top of the stairs. Even the launch pad by the door gets the new question.]

[HOST, voice over]
The smoke alarm you cannot remember testing: press the button, and if it is silent, a new battery goes in before anything else, because nothing else you find will matter as much. The cupboard of cleaning bottles at a child's height: everything that could poison someone goes up high or behind a latch. The rug at the top of the stairs that has always slid: grip tape underneath, or the rug gone. Even the launch pad gets the new question.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Not a tidier home. A safer one.]

[HOST, on camera]
Notice what that walk was and was not. It was not a deep clean and it was not a tidy; the home was already both. A few things moved, or got latched, or got a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. That is Safety in its smallest complete form: one lens, one walk, the worst dangers handled first.

Now go and look at the friction meter, because it moves again, and it moves for a reason it has never moved for before. Every step until now, the needle traveled because the daily friction went down. This time nothing about the daily friction changed at all. The home is no cleaner. And yet the needle steps toward calm, because a few real dangers are now gone, and removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The needle takes its first Safety step. The goal is still ahead.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the step is real, and it is small, both on purpose. Small, because the earlier S's already removed most of what this meter measures; what remains is not big friction to clear but important things to lock down. Real, because a hazard removed is a genuine and permanent gain. The goal, out on the calm side, is still ahead, because Safety has only begun, with a thorough hazard hunt still to come, and Standardize and Sustain beyond it.

You changed the question, and that changes everything that follows.
