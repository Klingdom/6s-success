**Visual vocabulary to keep consistent (from the chapter):**
- The hero device is the safety lens: one new question asked of the whole home, could this hurt someone. Show it as the same room seen twice, the objects unmoved, only the question changed, and a handful of quiet red hazard marks lighting up on things tidiness never questioned. The habit of asking it is what the source calls developing safety consciousness, teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. What you are building is not fear; it is a habit of noticing.
- The rule carries the whole chapter, frozen, render verbatim: "Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone." Its companion is the triage, also frozen, render verbatim: "Protect people first: fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else." The definition box is also frozen: "A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy."
- The starting point is the cleared, arranged, freshly cleaned and self-keeping home from the end of Shine (Chapters 16 to 19). It is genuinely lovely and, by every measure so far, finished. This chapter does not re-clean it, does not re-tidy it, and does not rebuild anything. Show it as warm, cared-for, in its place, and then let the safety lens reveal that it was quietly dangerous the whole time. Do NOT re-teach the reframe, the list, the methods, or the reset; Shine is settled and closed. Safety is a different lens on the same home.
- The reframe the whole part turns on: a clean home is not a safe home. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean; none of them asked whether it could hurt someone. Show the four questions as cards, three of them ticked, and the fourth, could this hurt someone, blank until this chapter picks it up. Tidy and safe are different questions, and where they part ways the gap is dangerous precisely because it is invisible.
- The five hazards are the recurring visual motif, drawn quiet and easy to miss, laid over a tidy scene: a rug curling at the very top of the stairs, a low cupboard open with a cleaning bottle inside and a toddler a step away, a smoke alarm on the ceiling with its indicator light dark, a heavy box perched near the edge of a high shelf, a cord run taut across a walkway. None of them is messy, misplaced, or dirty. Each is marked only by a small red hazard mark, calm, not alarming. Warm line-and-flat-color style.
- The 5S+1 history is referenced lightly and home-scaled: the method began with five S's, and as it spread beyond the factory floor people saw that not one of the five, on its own, guaranteed the people in the space were safe, so safety was added as its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1. Show it as five S-tiles gaining a sixth, tied gently to Toyota and the Toyota Production System. Reference lightly, do not overclaim.
- "First" is priority, not sequence: you did Safety fourth and that order is fine, but it is first in priority. Show a small three-way weekend choice, a safety fix, a tidiness improvement, an efficiency tweak, and safety stepping above the list, not competing on it. Protecting people is not one item on the list; it sits above the list.
- The triage is the seeing tool for what the lens finds: rank by two plain measures, how badly it could hurt someone (up) and how soon or how likely it is to (across). A dead smoke alarm and unlatched medicine sit top-right, ringed "fix first"; a slightly loose bannister sits lower-left, "later"; a cord across a walkway and a wobbling shelf sit in the middle. Calm slate axes, one warm accent for the "fix first" ring. Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology.
- The pyramid, Heinrich home-scaled, is why you act now: a wide base of near misses, a thinner band of minor hurts, a single cap of serious injury, with a note that removing hazards at the base shrinks the whole shape. Show it small, as an inset to the triage plot. Use it as a calm reason to act while hazards are still near misses, NEVER as a frightening statistic; do not overclaim the precision of the ratios.
- Safety is love, not fear, is the emotional frame: the goal is not vigilance, which is exhausting and does not scale, but dealing with the real dangers so thoroughly and early that you no longer have to watch for them. Show a parent breathing out, a latched cabinet, a fresh battery going into an alarm, a worry set down and not picked up again. No shame: a home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space; the fix is a new question and a short triage, not guilt. No fear-mongering, no graphic injury, no alarmist numbers.
- The worked pass is the safety lens in action, applied to the existing home: the smoke alarm gets its button pressed and, if silent, a fresh battery before anything else; the cleaning bottles under the sink go up high or behind a latch at child height; the rug at the top of the stairs gets grip tape underneath or goes; even the launch pad, that first small victory by the door, gets the new question. Nothing is cleaned or tidied; a few things move, or get latched, or get a fresh battery, purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot.
- The factory-floor provenance is referenced lightly and home-scaled: the 6S Safety Step, develop safety consciousness, identify and eliminate hazards, address issues immediately, home-scaled from the Toyota Production System. Reference lightly, do not overclaim.
- The friction meter TAKES ITS FIRST SAFETY STEP this chapter, a STEP, not a milestone. The live needle steps once, just past Chapter 19's marked SHINE COMPLETE milestone tick, a short move further toward the calm side. This is the first time the needle moves not because daily friction dropped but because the chance of harm did. Chapter 19's Shine-complete milestone is now a small passed marker just behind the live needle; the earlier Shine steps are fainter still; the Straighten-complete and Sort-complete milestones sit further back as small passed markers. The step is deliberately small. The GOAL crosshair, tagged "purpose", stays ahead on the green calm side, clearly not yet reached, because Safety has only begun and Standardize and Sustain still follow. This must read as a real but small first step, honest because removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is, and NOT as a milestone and NOT as arriving.
- Warm daylight, lived-in homes, real households. Not a styled catalog look. Hazard marks calm and quiet, never lurid; this is a steady hand pointing, not an alarm going off.
- Keep the whole thing calm, warm, and loving, and let it carry the note of relief that safety done well brings, because a hazard removed is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again. No force or violence framing. Nothing is attacked, blitzed, blasted, purged, or waged war on; a few real dangers are simply handled. The tone is anti-shame: a home full of unnoticed hazards is the normal case, because adults habituate to their own space, not because anyone was negligent, and the fix is a new question, not more vigilance.

### Shot 4: The safety lens (the hero device)
- The identical living room drawn twice, side by side. On the left, headed "Is it tidy?", the room reads calm and finished, a soft green wash, everything apparently fine. On the right, the same room headed "Could this hurt someone?", the same objects unchanged, but now a handful carry small red hazard marks: the rug at the stair top, a low cupboard with a bottle in it, the dark smoke alarm, a cord across the floor, a pan handle turned outward. Nothing in the room moved between the two panels; only the question changed. Beside it, the definition box.
- The point: you see what you have walked past for years by changing the question. A question decides what you are able to see: ask is this tidy and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm; ask could this hurt someone and the same alarm lights up. This is developing safety consciousness, teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic. What you are building is not fear; it is a habit of noticing. And the sting of the definition is its last line: most hazards are perfectly tidy.
- Coverage: one hold on the two panels side by side; one push from the calm left panel to the same room's red marks on the right; one hold on the definition box, landing on the last line.

### Shot 5: Protect people first (the triage)
- A two-axis plot: how badly it could hurt someone (up), how soon or how likely (across). Sample home hazards placed as small dots: a dead smoke alarm and unlatched medicine top-right, ringed and labeled "fix first"; a slightly loose bannister lower-left, labeled "later"; a cord across a walkway and a wobbling shelf in the middle. A small inset in the corner shows the pyramid: a wide base of near misses, a thinner band of minor hurts, a single cap of serious injury, with a note that removing hazards at the base shrinks the whole shape.
- The point: ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip from useful into overwhelming. The answer is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order. Rank by two plain measures, how badly and how soon. The worst-and-soonest corner gets fixed today; the minor-and-unlikely can wait. This is not being casual about the bannister; it is refusing to let it and forty other small things crowd out the two that could actually hurt someone this afternoon.
- Coverage: one hold on the plot with the dots placed; one push into the top-right "fix first" ring; one on the pyramid inset, the base shrinking as hazards are removed.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Rank by two things: how badly, and how soon." then "The worst and soonest, fixed first. Everything else can wait its turn."
- **Lower third:** Protect people first · worst and soonest first

### Shot 6: Why act now (the pyramid, Heinrich home-scaled)
- The pyramid drawn calm and plain: a wide base of near misses, a thinner band of minor hurts, a single cap of serious injury, all rising from the same pile of small hazards. A hand removes hazards from the base, and the whole shape shrinks, cap included. No graphic injury, no numbers presented as precise; a quiet diagram, not an alarm.
- The point: safety work has long noticed a pattern, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and minor hurts and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one. That is why you do not wait for the serious injury to act: you remove the hazards while they are still only near misses. You are not fixing the smoke alarm because you expect a fire tonight; you are fixing it because the cost of removing a hazard while it is harmless is so low, and the cost of guessing wrong so high, that there is nothing to weigh.
- Coverage: one hold on the whole pyramid rising from the pile; one on the single cap at the top; one on the hand clearing the base and the whole shape shrinking.
- **On-screen text moment:** "The same pile of small hazards, mostly near misses. Rarely, one serious." then "You cannot tell which. So you clear them while they are still near misses."
- **Lower third:** Remove hazards while they are still near misses

### Shot 7: Safety is love, not fear (the emotional core)
- A parent breathing out in a quiet, warm room. A cabinet latch clicking shut once, and then never watched again. A fresh battery going into a smoke alarm, and the whole category of "what if there is a fire and we do not wake up" closing. A small worry, drawn as a weight, set down on a shelf and not picked back up. The register is relief, not dread.
- The point: none of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. The goal is not vigilance, which is exhausting and does not scale; it is to deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and never again track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach. Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again. A safe home is not a fearful one; it is the one where you have already done the worrying, once, with your hands.
- Coverage: one on the latch clicking shut; one on the fresh battery and the alarm's light coming back on; one on the parent breathing out, the worry set down beside them.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Handle the few real dangers once, so you can stop watching for them." then "Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out."
- **Lower third:** Safety is love, not fear

### Shot 8: A first safety pass (the worked walk)
- The lens taken for a slow walk through the finished home. The smoke alarm: a finger presses the button, and if it is silent, a fresh battery goes in first, before anything else. The cupboard under the sink, tidy and ordinary, full of cleaning bottles at a small child's height: the bottles move up high or behind a latch. The rug at the top of the stairs that has always slid a little: a strip of grip tape underneath, or the rug gone. Even the launch pad by the door gets the new question. Nothing is scrubbed; a few things move, or get latched, or get a fresh battery.
- The point: make a start today and keep it small, because protecting people first means the first pass is not a thorough hunt; it is the worst few things, handled now. Notice what the walk was and was not: not a deep clean and not a tidy; the home was already both. A few things moved purely because they could hurt someone, and now they cannot. That is Safety in its smallest complete form: one lens, one walk, the worst dangers handled first, not a tidier home but a safer one.
- Coverage: one on the button pressed and the fresh battery going in; one on the bottles going up high or behind a latch; one on the grip tape under the rug at the stair top; one wide of the finished home, a handful of quiet dangers now closed off for good.
- **On-screen text moment:** "One lens. One slow walk. The worst few dangers, handled today." then "Not a tidier home. A safer one."
