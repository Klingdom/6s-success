# Teleprompter Script: Safety: Protect People First

**Chapter 20, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** safety-protect-people-first
**Length:** about 720 words. Narration only. Short lines for easy reading.

---

[Read at a calm, even pace. Pause at each blank line.]

Last time you finished Shine.

Your home is cleared, arranged, and clean.

It stays clean on fifteen minutes a day.

Three of the six S's, done.

By every measure this book has given you, it is finished.

And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight.

Not because you did anything wrong.

Sort asked whether a thing belonged.

Straighten asked where it should live.

Shine asked whether it was clean.

Three good questions.

Not one of them notices the rug at the top of the stairs.

Or the medicine at a toddler's eye level.

Or the smoke alarm that died quietly last spring.

Those things were never messy. Never dirty.

A clean home is not a safe home.

Tidy and safe are different questions.

A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does.

It sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not.

So there is a fourth S. Safety.

And it is a turn, not an afterthought.

For nineteen chapters, this book was about the room.

Safety is about the people in it.

Here is a little history.

The method began with five S's.

Safety was meant to run through all of them.

But safety that runs through everything can end up owned by no one.

So it was pulled out and named.

The plus one, in what was first called 5S plus 1.

That is why it comes with the word first.

Not first in sequence. You did it fourth.

First in priority.

Protecting people is not one item on the list.

It sits above the list.

So how do you see what you have walked past for years?

You change the question.

Ask not whether it is tidy, but whether it could hurt someone.

That single swapped question is the whole tool.

A question decides what you are able to see.

Ask is this tidy, and your eye slides over the dead alarm.

Ask could this hurt someone, and the same alarm lights up.

The pros call this developing safety consciousness.

Teaching your eye a new question until it becomes automatic.

What you are building is not fear. It is a habit of noticing.

And here is the definition of a hazard.

Anything in a home that could hurt someone.

The loose rug. The pills within reach. The dead alarm.

It does not have to look like a mess.

Most hazards are perfectly tidy.

Ask the question honestly, and you will find a lot.

That can feel overwhelming.

The answer is not to look away. It is to put it in order.

Protect people first.

Fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest, before anything else.

Rank by two things. How badly. And how soon.

A dead alarm and pills a child can reach go to the top.

A slightly loose bannister can wait its turn.

Safety is triage. The worst first, on purpose, without apology.

And here is why you act now.

A pile of small hazards gives mostly near misses.

And, rarely, one serious injury.

You can rarely tell which one is the dangerous one.

So you remove them while they are still near misses.

Because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was.

And none of this is about living afraid.

It is the opposite.

You handle the few real dangers once.

So you can stop carrying them around as worry.

Every hazard you remove is a worry you can set down.

And never pick up again.

Safety done well is quiet. It is what lets a parent breathe out.

So make a start today. Keep it small.

The smoke alarm. Press the button. If it is silent, new battery first.

The cleaning bottles under the sink. Up high, or behind a latch.

The rug at the top of the stairs. Grip tape, or gone.

Even the launch pad gets the new question.

Nothing was cleaned. Nothing was tidied.

A few things moved, purely because they could hurt someone.

That is Safety in its smallest form. Not a tidier home. A safer one.

Now go look at the friction meter.

For the first time, it moves without anything being cleaned.

Nothing about the daily friction changed.

And yet the needle steps toward calm.

Because a few real dangers are simply gone.

And removing the chance of harm is the deepest kind of friction there is.

Not the friction of every day. The friction of the worst day.

The one you are keeping from ever arriving.

The step is real, and it is small. Both, on purpose.

The goal is still ahead. Safety has only begun.

So here is your job.

Walk one room. Find the single most dangerous thing.

The unlatched medicine. The sliding rug. The dead alarm.

And fix that one thing today.

You just protected people first.
