# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 20, Safety: Protect People First

Stand in the home you have built and look at it with plain pride. You cleared what did not belong, you gave everything a place, and you keep the whole of it clean and read on fifteen minutes a day. By every measure this book has given you, it is finished, and it is good. And it could still send someone to the hospital tonight. Not because you did anything wrong, but because nothing you have done so far was ever aimed at that. Sort asked whether a thing belonged, Straighten where it should live, Shine whether it was clean; not one of them notices the rug curling at the top of the stairs, the medicine at a small child's eye level, or the smoke alarm quietly dead since spring. That is why there is a fourth S, and why it is a turn, not an afterthought: for nineteen chapters the book was about the state of the room; Safety is about the people in it.

A clean home is not a safe home. It is a comforting feeling that a tidy home is a safe one, and it is not true. Tidiness and safety overlap here and there, a cleared walkway is both tidy and safer, but they are not the same thing, and where they part ways the gap can be dangerous precisely because it is invisible. The most immaculate kitchen is not made safer by its gleam if the pan handles turn out over the edge of the stove. Sort, Straighten, and Shine asked whether a thing belonged, where it lived, and whether it was clean; none of them asked whether it could hurt someone, so a home can be cleared, arranged, and spotless and still hold every danger it started with. A hazard does not announce itself the way a mess does; it sits there, tidy and patient, until the day it does not. That is why safety has to be its own step, on purpose: if cleaning made a home safe, it would come free with Shine, and it does not.

This is the small piece of history worth knowing, the very "plus one" that makes this book about six S's and not five. The method began with five S's, born on the factory floors of Toyota, and safety was meant to run through all of them. But as it spread, people saw that "safety runs through everything" has a quiet failure mode: what belongs to everything can end up owned by no one. So safety was pulled out and named its own S, the plus one in what was first called 5S+1, made a thing you do on purpose rather than a thing you hope falls out of the rest, because something this important should never be left to chance. That is also why the word first sits in the title. It is not first in sequence; you do it fourth, and that order is fine. It is first in priority: when a safety fix, a tidiness improvement, and an efficiency tweak all want your Saturday, safety wins, every time. Protecting people is not one item on the list; it sits above the list.

The way you see what you have walked past for years is to change the question. For three parts of the book you looked at your home asking, in various forms, is this in order. Now you look at the same home asking something else entirely: could this hurt someone. That single swapped question is the whole tool, and it is more powerful than it sounds, because a question decides what you are able to see. Ask is this tidy and your eye slides over the dead smoke alarm; ask could this hurt someone and the same alarm lights up. The professionals call this developing safety consciousness, teaching your eye a new question until asking it becomes automatic, and what you are building is not fear, it is a habit of noticing. A hazard is any thing or situation in a home that could hurt someone: the loose rug, the pills within reach, the dead smoke alarm, the wobbling shelf. It does not have to look like a mess. Most hazards are perfectly tidy, quiet and orderly and sitting in plain sight so long they have become furniture to you.

Ask the safety question honestly of a whole home and you will find more than you expected, and that can tip from useful into overwhelming. The answer is not to look away; it is to put the findings in order, because not all hazards are equal. Protect people first: rank what you find by two plain measures, how badly it could hurt someone and how soon or how likely it is to, and fix what could hurt someone worst and soonest before anything else. A dead smoke alarm and a bottle of pills a small child can open score high on both, so they go to the very top and get fixed today; a slightly loose bannister that holds fine can wait its turn. Safety is triage, and triage means the worst first, on purpose, without apology. The reason you act on the top of the list now rather than someday comes straight from a pattern safety workers have watched for a century, sometimes called Heinrich's Principle: a large pile of small hazards produces mostly near misses and, every so often, out of the same pile, one serious injury. You can rarely tell in advance which hazard is the dangerous one, so you remove them while they are still only near misses, because the whole aim is to never find out which one it was.

None of this is about living afraid or wrapping your home in foam. It is the opposite. The goal of safety is not vigilance, which is exhausting and does not scale; the goal is to deal with the real dangers so thoroughly, and so early, that you no longer have to watch for them at all. You latch the cabinet once, and never again track where the toddler is in relation to the bleach. You put a working battery in the alarm, and the whole category of worry simply closes. Every hazard you remove is not just a harm prevented; it is a worry you get to set down and never pick up again. Safety done well is quiet: it is what lets a parent breathe out. You just protected people first.
