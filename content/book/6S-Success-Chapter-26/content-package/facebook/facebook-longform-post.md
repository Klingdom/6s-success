For years I treated my home like a project with a finish line. One big push, get everything right, and then, at last, be done. I would manage it for a weekend. Then it drifted back, and I would dread the next big push. I thought the problem was that I needed more discipline.

That is not the honest gap this chapter names.

"Held is not the same as kept."

The fifth S stopped my home from quietly sliding back on its own. But it cannot stop the world, and the world does not rest.

"Nothing you have built will stay built on its own, and not because your standards are weak. It is because life is relentless: new things arrive, seasons change, people come and go, and the pull back toward chaos never takes a single day off."

So there is no finish line to cross. And that is not bad news. It is the thing that finally took the pressure off.

"You do not finish 6S. You keep it. The first five S's build a home worth keeping; the sixth is the habit of keeping it."

Keeping sounds like more work. It is the opposite. The reason my cleanups were so dreaded is that I only ever did them once the place had already gotten bad. A home tended a little every day never gets that bad.

"the household that sustains is not the one doing more work. It is the one that has quietly stopped doing the big, dreaded work at all."

And the fuel is not willpower, which is a relief, because mine runs out exactly when I am busiest and most tired.

"Sustain runs on habit, not willpower: small, normal, shared, and low enough in effort that it survives the weeks when nobody has any energy to spare."

So I picked one surface and one minute, every day, and lowered the bar until I could not fail. Kept up long enough, it stops feeling like a thing you do at all.

"Kept long enough, 6S stops being something you do to your home and becomes simply how your home is."

A home stays good the way a garden stays alive: a little ongoing care from the people who love it, not one heroic effort and then neglect.
