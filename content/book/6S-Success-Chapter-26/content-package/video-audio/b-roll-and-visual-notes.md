# B-Roll and Visual Notes: Sustain: Keeping the Gains

**Chapter 26, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** sustain-keeping-the-gains
**Use:** Shot list, on-screen text moments, and lower-third suggestions for the YouTube, short-form, and any companion video cuts.

**Visual vocabulary to keep consistent (from the chapter):**
- The hero is one living room shown across a single year, split into two parallel rows telling two stories of the same home. The TOP row, "finished, then left", opens on the perfect room exactly as Standardize left it, but across spring, summer, autumn, and winter it slowly fills again, clutter creeping onto the surfaces, a cushion askew, a pile growing in the corner, until the last panel looks worryingly like where the book began. The BOTTOM row, "kept", opens on the same perfect room, but in each following panel a small pair of hands does one tiny ordinary thing, straightening one item, wiping one surface, and because of that quiet regular care the room stays good and warm across all four seasons, essentially unchanged. Nothing separates the two rows but a habit. The register is warm, encouraging, and freeing, this OPENS the final part: keeping is a relief, not an obligation, and the difference between the two rows is not a greater effort but a smaller one that never quite stops.
- The rule carries the whole chapter, frozen, render verbatim: "You do not finish 6S. You keep it. The first five S's build a home worth keeping; the sixth is the habit of keeping it." The practice-not-project line is frozen: "The first five S's are things you do. Sustain is the one you keep doing, because it is not a step with an end but the ongoing life of the home." The definition box is frozen: "Sustain is the sixth S: the ongoing habit of keeping the first five alive, so the home stays sorted, arranged, clean, safe, and standardized not for a week or a season but over the years. It is the one S you never finish, because it is not a task you complete. It is a way of living."
- The three reframes are the spine of the chapter, each frozen. RELENTLESSNESS, frozen, render verbatim: "Nothing you have built will stay built on its own, and not because your standards are weak. It is because life is relentless: new things arrive, seasons change, people come and go, and the pull back toward chaos never takes a single day off. Sustain is not distrust of your standards. It is respect for how hard the world pushes on them." HABIT, NOT WILLPOWER, frozen, render verbatim: "You will not keep your home by resolving to try harder, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. Sustain runs on habit, not willpower: small, normal, shared, and low enough in effort that it survives the weeks when nobody has any energy to spare." SECOND NATURE, frozen, render verbatim, ties to the goal: "Kept long enough, 6S stops being something you do to your home and becomes simply how your home is. That is the destination this whole book has been walking toward: not a home you are forever fixing, but one that mostly keeps itself, because keeping it has quietly become second nature."
- The starting point is the home from the end of Chapter 25: cleared, arranged, kept clean, made safe, and now with written and pictured standards that actually hold their shape without leaning on memory. Five of the six S's are done, and the fifth locked the gains so they cannot quietly slide back through forgetting. It is a real achievement. But held is not the same as kept: Standardize stopped the passive slide, it cannot stop the world, and the world is relentless. This chapter does NOT re-clean, re-tidy, or re-run any earlier S; it does NOT re-teach the concept of a standard or its tools (Chapters 23 to 25); it does NOT build the audit or check-in (Chapter 27), the family problem-solving method (Chapter 28), the daily rhythm (Chapter 29), or the recurring 6S event (Chapter 30). It gives the concept and the mindset of the sixth S only: that you keep 6S, you never finish it.
- The honest distinction, drawn plainly, not as blame. Standardize dealt with the passive slide, the way a system quietly decays when people forget the best way. A written, tool-carried, visible standard fixes that: it holds the shape. But there is a second force no standard can hold back, and it is not passive at all: the active, ceaseless arrival of life. A standard for the coat closet cannot stop three new coats being bought; a beautiful pantry photo cannot stop a new baby turning the household upside down for a year. The point to draw: these are not failures of your standards. They are just what living does, every single day. Show the good standardized home, then the relentless arrivals of ordinary life pressing on it, then the one thing you must never do, decide you are finished.
- The "done" trap is the quiet, fatal moment the sixth S exists to help you avoid: looking at your good home and thinking "there, done." Nothing in a home is ever done. The moment you believe it is, you stop tending it, and the relentless world, which never stopped for a second, begins carrying it back the way it came. A home is not a project you complete. It is a thing you keep. Draw it gently, never as a scolding: a home that slid back after a big push is the normal case, not a failure.
- The flagship reframe is that Sustain is a practice, not a project, and reading that as relief rather than burden. For twenty-five chapters you did projects: bounded pieces of work with a beginning, a middle, and a satisfying end. Sort the drawer, and the drawer is sorted. Sustain is not like that, and treating it like that is why so much hard-won order collapses a few months after the big push. But read it as a relief: you are no longer trying to reach some final, finished, perfect state and then defend it forever, which is exhausting and impossible. You are just living in your home and keeping it as you go, which is ordinary and human and light.
- Habit, not willpower, is the how, and the willpower-vs-habit graph carries it. Willpower is the wrong fuel because it runs out first, and it runs out precisely when you need it most, in the busy, tired, difficult stretches that are also when a home most wants to fall apart. A habit is something you do without deciding to, so it keeps running on the days your motivation is gone, and those are the days that matter. The craft is to lower the bar, deliberately, until the habit is almost impossible to fail: a one-minute reset of a single surface, done every day, keeps more of your home over a year than a two-hour blitz you manage twice and then dread. The graph is a simple two-line panel: the "willpower" line spikes high at the start, wobbles, and crashes to nothing within weeks; the "habit" line starts low and modest and simply continues, nearly flat and unbroken across the whole span; a low dashed "keeping line" sits beneath, and the habit line clears it every day while the willpower line clears it only briefly. Caption strip: "small and relentless beats heroic and brief".
- Keeping is lighter than fixing, drawn as the quiet relief at the center of the part. A home tended a little every day never gets bad enough to need the exhausting overhaul, so the household that sustains is not the one doing more work. It is the one that has quietly stopped doing the big, dreaded work at all. Show the small daily minute of care beside the dreaded weekend overhaul that never has to happen, and the household relaxing in the home rather than working on it.
- Second nature is where all of this is heading, the quiet promise the whole book has walked toward, and the project-vs-practice graph carries it. There is a point, if you keep a home long enough with small shared habits, where the keeping stops feeling like a thing you are doing at all: putting the keys on the hook is just what your hand does, the after-dinner reset is simply part of dinner. At that point the home is maintained not by effort but by identity, and it stays good almost without anyone noticing they are keeping it. It gets easier, genuinely and a great deal, not because you finish but because keeping becomes automatic, and automatic is nearly free. The graph is a two-line time panel, "the gains" up the side and years along the bottom: the "a project" line climbs steeply to a high peak and then decays season by season, ending low; the "a practice" line climbs the same way but levels off and runs flat and high across all the years, held there by small ongoing care, with tiny regular tick-marks showing the little acts of keeping. Note: "the practice, not the peak, is what lasts".
- The friction meter HOLDS at the Standardize-complete mark, because a concept does not move the room, and this is NOT a milestone and NOT a move. The live needle sits exactly where Chapter 25 left it, at the marked STANDARDIZE COMPLETE tick, unmoved, with the anti-backslide catch behind it still drawn fully engaged and locked. What is NEW, and for the FIRST TIME, is a faint dashed path running from the tip of the live needle across the small remaining distance to the GOAL crosshair, labelled softly to the effect of "Sustain closes this last gap, over time". The five completed-S milestones (Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize complete) sit behind as small passed markers. The GOAL crosshair, tagged "purpose", sits just ahead on the green calm side, a step away, but now with its closing path drawn in for the first time. That last gap is not more friction to clear; it is the difference between a home that stays good because you are working at it and a home that stays good on its own. The goal is reached only at Chapter 30.
- Warm daylight, lived-in homes, real households. Not a styled catalog look. The two-row year drawn in a warm line-and-flat-color style, the rows clearly parallel so the only difference the eye reads is the small ongoing care, a honey accent on the "kept" row and the pair of caring hands. Calm slate lines on the graphs, one warm honey accent on "habit" and on "a practice"; green for the calm side of the meter and the goal. The tone is warm, human, encouraging, and freeing, this OPENS the last part: five of six S's are done, and now the book turns to keeping them, which is the lightest work of all.
- Keep the whole thing warm, human, and anti-shame. A home that slid back after a big push, or that lives on one tired person's willpower, is the normal case, not a failing. No corporate or compliance framing, no punitive-discipline or force words (no discipline in the punitive sense, stay on top of it, no excuses, hold the line, enforce, lock down), no hype words (unlock, leverage, elevate, transform your life), no management-dashboard feel, no brand names (say "the fridge", "a phone", not a brand). Sustain is relief and freedom, a shared choice about how to live, never a control imposed on the family and never a scolding for past lapses. The goal is a step away, its closing path drawn for the first time, because the sixth and final S has only just begun.
