**The recurring motif.** The hero of the chapter is the sixth and final S, Sustain, and the single idea that opens it: you do not finish 6S, you keep it, because held is not the same as kept. One image carries the whole set: the same room across a year, shown in two parallel rows, one "finished, then left" and drifting back season by season, one "kept" by a little ordinary care and staying good across the whole year, so the premise is clear in a single picture, the difference between them not a greater effort but a smaller one that never quite stops. The arc of the chapter is the move from treating a good home as a finished project to treating it as a thing you keep, from willpower that spikes and crashes to a habit that never stops, and finally to second nature, the point where keeping stops feeling like a thing you do and becomes simply how your home is. Two supporting devices run through the middle: the habit-vs-willpower graph (the willpower line spiking high and crashing within weeks, the habit line low and modest and unbroken, clearing the low keeping line every day) and the project-to-practice graph (the project line peaking and decaying, the practice line levelling off and running flat and high, held there by small ongoing care). Slate carries the data and the device, the graph lines, the friction meter's fully locked catch, the milestone ticks, and the GOAL crosshair with its new dashed closing path; terracotta is the kicker and the rule; honey is the one warm accent per figure, the keeping hands, the "habit" and "a practice" lines, and the Family Challenge; green is a home kept and running on quiet care, the bottom "kept" row, the second-nature ease, and the calm side of the meter; spark red, used sparingly, is the friction side of the meter, the barred backslide, the willpower crash, and the drifting-back home; soft carries the faded drifting room and the faded passed milestones.

**Two fences to hold visually.** First, Chapter 26 OPENS Sustain, the sixth and final S, with its CONCEPT and its MINDSET only: that a home is kept and not finished, that keeping runs on small shared habits rather than willpower, and that kept long enough it becomes second nature. It does NOT build the mechanisms of Sustain: not the gentle check-in or audit (Chapter 27), not the family problem-solving method (Chapter 28), not the daily 6S rhythm (Chapter 29, "a little, often" as a principle only), and not the recurring 6S event that completes the book (Chapter 30). Draw the mindset and one or two ordinary keeping-acts as illustration, a surface wiped in a minute, keys on a hook, an after-dinner reset, NOT a documented maintenance system, an audit, a checklist regime, a schedule, or a compliance routine. It uses the standards Standardize built without re-teaching them, and it does NOT re-teach Sort, Straighten, Shine, Safety, or Standardize; the first five S's appear only as the settled home now being kept. Second, do not let the register drift corporate or punitive: Sustain can sound like discipline, willpower, staying on top of it, holding the line, or a household running itself like a factory, so every image works to keep it warm, human, anti-shame, and freeing. A home that slid back after a big push is the normal case, not a failure, so there is no scolding; the fix is a smaller, gentler habit, not more discipline or guilt. No force or control framing (no enforce, comply, crack down, stay on top of it, no excuses, hold the line, hold yourself accountable), no hype (no unlock, leverage, elevate, transform your life), no clipboards or inspectors, no brand names. Sustain is relief and permission, not obligation; keeping is lighter than fixing, and a home is kept by the people who live in it choosing to, together, not by one person quietly carrying it. Because this OPENS the sixth S with a mindset rather than a room-changing act, the friction meter HOLDS: the needle does not move from the Standardize-complete position, because a concept does not move the room. What is new, for the first time on the dial, is a faint dashed path from the needle to the GOAL crosshair, the last gap Sustain closes over time by making the keeping so habitual and so shared it becomes second nature. It must read as a HOLD, NOT a milestone and NOT a move, with the anti-backslide catch still fully locked, and the GOAL crosshair staying just ahead on the green calm side because one S remains and the goal is reached only at Chapter 30.

- Composition: a calm home in the centre with the ordinary pressures of life drawn as gentle inward arrows all around it, parcels, seasons turning, people coming and going, never menacing, just constant and unresting. A small faded "there, done" thought-bubble is crossed quietly through, the "done" trap named without scolding.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the calm home, soft #6A625A and honey #DDA63A on the arrows of life, a sparing faded soft #6A625A on the crossed-through "there, done" bubble. Calm and legible; no verdict scolding, because the fault is the relentless world, not the reader.

## Beat 5 · Habit, not willpower

The how of it, with the obvious wrong answer closed off first: you will not keep a home by trying harder, because willpower runs out precisely when you need it most, and a habit keeps running on the days your motivation is gone.

- Composition: two small figures on paper ground. On the LEFT, one straining hard against a heavy load, honey #DDA63A effort flaring bright and then guttering out, labelled "willpower." On the RIGHT, the other doing one tiny effortless thing, keys on a hook, at ease, green #6E8B5B, labelled "habit."
- Palette: paper ground, honey #DDA63A on the flaring-then-guttering willpower effort, green #6E8B5B on the effortless habit, slate #3C5A6B on the labels. Warm; no scolding, because willpower failing is not a character flaw, it is how willpower works.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "willpower runs out first, exactly when you need it most. A habit keeps running when your motivation is gone."
- Point: the obvious question is how, and the obvious wrong answer is "by trying harder," so close that door before you walk through it. You already know, from painful experience, that resolving to do better does not keep a home. Willpower is the wrong fuel because it runs out first, and it runs out precisely when you need it most, in the busy, tired, difficult stretches that are also when a home most wants to fall apart. A habit is something you do without deciding to, which means it keeps running on the days your motivation is gone, and those are the days that matter. So the whole craft of sustaining is to convert keeping from a decision into a habit, and the way you do that is to make each keeping-act small.
- The frozen habit-not-willpower reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "You will not keep your home by resolving to try harder, any more than you kept it tidy by resolving to be neater. Sustain runs on habit, not willpower: small, normal, shared, and low enough in effort that it survives the weeks when nobody has any energy to spare."

## Beat 6 · Small and relentless beats heroic and brief

The counterintuitive craft made visual as the first graph: lower the bar until the habit is almost impossible to fail, because the small, dull, relentless version wins every time over the heroic version, since the small one actually keeps happening.

- Composition: the habit-vs-willpower infographic, a simple two-line graph on panel #FBF7EF, time along the bottom, slate #3C5A6B axes. The "willpower" line spikes up sharply at the start in spark red #CB4B36, wobbles, and crashes to nothing within a few weeks; the "habit" line starts low and modest in honey #DDA63A and simply continues, nearly flat and unbroken across the whole span. A low dashed rule #D9CDB8 "keeping line" sits beneath, and the habit line clears it every day while the willpower line clears it only briefly.
- Palette: paper and panel ground, slate #3C5A6B on the axes and the caption strip, spark red #CB4B36 on the willpower spike and crash, honey #DDA63A on the steady habit line, rule #D9CDB8 on the low dashed keeping line. Calm and legible; the point is legibility, not drama.
- Slate #3C5A6B caption strip: "small and relentless beats heroic and brief."
- Point: this is the counterintuitive heart of it, lower the bar, deliberately, until the habit is almost impossible to fail. A one-minute reset of a single surface, done every day, keeps more of your home over a year than a two-hour blitz you manage twice and then dread. The small, dull, relentless version wins, every time, over the heroic version, because the small one actually keeps happening. A keeping-plan that depends on everyone being motivated works only on the days you least need it; a small habit clears the low bar of keeping every single day.
- Frozen figure caption, verbatim: "Why keeping runs on habit, not willpower. Willpower spikes high and then crashes, usually within weeks, and it fails exactly when life gets hard. A small habit never spikes, but it never stops, so it clears the low bar of keeping every single day. Small and relentless beats heroic and brief." The Quick Win sits in a green #6E8B5B bordered callout, verbatim: "Pick the smallest keeping-habit you could imagine actually doing forever, one minute, once a day, resetting a single surface, and start it today. Sustain is built from habits so small they survive your worst weeks, not from grand resolutions that die inside a month."

## Beat 7 · Keeping is lighter than fixing

- Slate #3C5A6B label: "a little every day means never the exhausting overhaul."
- Point: keeping is genuinely lighter than fixing. A home tended a little every day never gets bad enough to need the exhausting overhaul, so the household that sustains is not the one doing more work. It is the one that has quietly stopped doing the big, dreaded work at all. Start smaller than feels serious, fold it into where the day already goes, and the dreaded overhaul simply never arrives.

## Beat 8 · Until it is second nature

The destination made visual as the second graph, and the rule given the pull-quote treatment: kept long enough, keeping stops feeling like a thing you do and becomes simply how your home is, a project that peaks and decays against a practice that levels off and lasts.

- Composition: the project-to-practice infographic, a two-line time graph on panel #FBF7EF, "the gains" up the side, years along the bottom, slate #3C5A6B axes. - Palette: paper and panel ground, slate #3C5A6B on the axes and the note, spark red #CB4B36 on the peaking-and-decaying project line, honey #DDA63A on the flat, lasting practice line, green #6E8B5B on the little keeping tick-marks. Calm; the practice, not the peak, is what lasts.
- Slate #3C5A6B note: "the practice, not the peak, is what lasts."
- Point: there is a point, if you keep a home long enough with small, shared habits, where the keeping stops feeling like a thing you are doing at all. It gets easier, genuinely and a great deal, not because you finish but because keeping becomes automatic, and automatic is nearly free.
