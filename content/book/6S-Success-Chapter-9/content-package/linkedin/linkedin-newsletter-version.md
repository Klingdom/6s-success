# The Basket That Stays Full After the Surface Is Clear

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Last week we cleared a surface, and it stayed clear. That is the quiet reward of a real first pass: the things you carried off did not creep back overnight, because you did not just tidy them, you sent them home. The console holds a keys tray, a leash, an umbrella, a bag, and tomorrow's mail. Five working things.

So we could stop. But there is a basket on the floor beside it, the one that has been catching the overflow for years, and if we are honest we both know what is in it. Three umbrellas, only one of which you ever open. A wad of folded tote bags. A spare key for a car you sold two summers ago. A fan of takeout menus. A dead charging cable for a phone you replaced ages ago.

Here is the strange part. Every one of those things belongs at an entryway. And you have not touched most of them in a year.

## Belonging is not the problem anymore. Use is.

The first Sort pass asked where a thing lives. This one asks something narrower: whether you need to keep it at all. Everything in that basket passed the belonging test, because umbrellas and bags and keys are exactly what an entryway is for.

And the gap between the two, between what fits here and what you actually reach for, is where the last of the clutter has been hiding in plain sight.

The reason it hides is gentle. Almost everything you own is useful. Point at any object in the basket and you can build a sincere case for why it might matter someday, and every case is valid, because usefulness is easy. Almost anything is useful in some imaginable circumstance. That is exactly the problem. When the test for keeping a thing is whether it could ever be useful, the test passes everything, and a test that passes everything is not a test at all.

So the whole chapter turns on one line: **useful is not the same as used.** Useful is a claim about the future. Used is a fact about the past. A home fills up not with useless things, which are easy to release, but with useful things you never use.

## The Use Test

The fix is one honest question, asked of one object at a time. **When did you last use it?**

If the answer comes quickly, this week, last month, the last time it rained, the thing is doing its job. It is necessary, and it stays. If the answer is a shrug, if you have to invent a scenario rather than recall one, it is only useful in theory. It is unnecessary, and it goes in the out box.

Necessary does not mean you would die without it. It means you actually use it, or would replace it if it vanished. Unnecessary does not mean worthless. It means the thing is only plausibly useful, kept just in case, while the things you truly use get crowded out. Both are readings of your own behavior, not judgments of the object.

For the few genuinely hard cases, a tiebreaker cuts through the fog: **if it vanished today, would you buy it again?** It strips away the sunk cost. An easy yes keeps it. A slow no is your answer.

The Use Test catches a second pattern too, one that hides behind the word backup. You do not need three umbrellas, you need the one you grab. Necessary quietly includes a sensible quantity. And the trap inside the trap is keeping the worse duplicate precisely because it is worse, and keeping the good one too. If two things do one job, one of them is unnecessary, usually the one you never reach for.

## Letting go is not wasting

I want to slow down on the thing that actually stalls people, because it is not the objects. It is cost. You pick up something you do not use, and a small voice reminds you what it cost, so letting it go starts to feel like admitting you wasted money, and the hand stops.

Look straight at the arithmetic, because it is kinder than it feels. The money was spent the day you bought it, and keeping the thing unused does not earn a cent of it back. It only charges you rent in space and attention. The waste, if there is any, already happened. Letting go is not the waste. It is the end of paying twice.

And it is not a confession either. Deciding a thing is unnecessary is not deciding you were foolish to buy it. The reason you bought it passed, that is all. Releasing the object is just catching up to a truth that already changed.

One more fence, so the pass stays quick. Deciding a thing is unnecessary and deciding its fate are two separate acts. The unnecessary things go in an out box, a set-aside spot, not a destination. Where they go from there gets its own chapter later. Today you decide. Later you route.

## The assignment

Stand at your basket, or your junk drawer, or your shelf of gadgets. Pick up one thing you have been keeping just in case. Ask when you last used it. If you cannot remember, put it in an out box today. One honest release is a full pass.

The hardest clutter to see is the useful kind you never actually use. Keep what earns its place by getting used, and let go of what you were only keeping just in case.
