## 1. Useful is not the same as used

Almost everything you own is useful. The spare key could open a car. The extra umbrella could keep a guest dry. Point at any object and you can build a sincere case for why it might matter someday.

That is the trap. When the test for keeping a thing is whether it could ever be useful, the test passes everything, and a test that passes everything is not a test at all.

So try the sharper line instead. Useful is not the same as used. One is a story you tell about an object. The other is a record of what it has done.

## 2. One question sorts what stays

You cleared the surface. What is left all belongs here. That is not the same as needing it.

So run one honest question on each thing that stayed. When did you last use it?

A fast, specific memory, this week, last month, the last time it rained, means the thing is necessary, and it stays. A shrug, or a scenario you have to invent, means it is only useful in theory. It is unnecessary, and it goes in the out box.

That is the whole test. Not whether it is nice, not whether it cost money. Just when you last put it to work.

## 3. The just-in-case pile

A home does not fill up with useless things. Those are easy to spot and easy to release.

It fills up with useful things you never use. The just-in-case thing is a plausibly useful object kept for a someday that rarely comes, quietly taking the place of the things you use now.

It is not stupid to own one. It is only costly to own a basketful, because each one asks for a little space, a little attention, a little of the hum of remembering it is there.

The hardest clutter to see is the useful kind you never actually use.

## 4. Stuck on a hard one

Most objects answer instantly, and you should trust the fast answer, because your hands know the truth before your pride does.

But a few will be genuinely hard. For those there is a tiebreaker. If it vanished today, would you buy it again?

It works because it strips away the sunk cost. You are no longer deciding whether to part with a thing you own, which feels like a loss. You are deciding, with today's needs and today's money, whether you would choose it fresh.

An easy yes keeps it. A slow no, or a wince, is your answer.

## 5. You do not need three umbrellas

You need the one you actually grab when the sky looks wrong. The other two are not spares in any meaningful sense. They are two more things to store, dust around, and step over.

Necessary quietly includes a sensible quantity. One good umbrella is necessary. The third is not, because the job it does is already done by the first.

When you find several of a thing you only need one or two of, keep your favorite and let the rest go. That is not living without. That is picking the one that works.

## 6. The backup that guards nothing

Careful people fall into this one. You keep the worse pair of scissors just in case the good ones wear out. And you keep the good ones too.

Now you own two things to do one job, and the bad pair sits in the drawer for a decade guarding against a failure that costs less to fix than the drawer space did.

If two things do one job, one of them is unnecessary, usually the one you never reach for. Keep the one your hand goes to. Let the understudy retire.

## 7. Decide today, route later

The moment you decide a thing is unnecessary, a second question rushes in. Donate it? Sell it? Store it? And the pass stalls while you try to settle its fate.

So split the two acts. Deciding a thing is unnecessary and deciding where it goes are separate, and doing them at once is how a fifteen-minute pass becomes a lost afternoon.

The unnecessary things go in an out box, a set-aside spot, out of the flow, while you finish. Where they go from there gets its own chapter. Today you decide. Later you route.

## 8. Letting go is not wasting

Here is where a good pass most often dies, and it has nothing to do with the objects.

You pick up something you do not use, and a small voice reminds you what it cost. Suddenly letting it go feels like admitting you wasted money, so the hand stops.

Look at the arithmetic, because it is kinder than it feels. The money was spent the day you bought it. Keeping the thing unused does not earn a cent of it back. It only charges you rent in space and attention.

The waste, if there is any, already happened. Letting go is the end of paying twice.

## 9. It is not a confession

Deciding a thing is unnecessary is not deciding you were foolish to buy it.

You bought it for a reason that likely made sense at the time. The car you still owned. The phone you still used. The guest who might have needed an umbrella. The reason passed, that is all.

Releasing the object is just catching up to a truth that already changed. There is nothing to be ashamed of in owning a thing you no longer need. There is only a small, steady cost in keeping it, and you are allowed to stop paying it whenever you like.

## 10. The clear calls, then the maybes

Run the Use Test and most objects answer fast. The clear yeses and clear noes are almost always the great majority.

But one or two will sit in your hand and refuse to answer. You do not use it, yet you can imagine using it. The honest reply is simply, I do not know.

Do not force those. One or two undecidable things are not a hole in your resolve. They are the normal residue of any careful pass. Set them aside on their own and keep going.
