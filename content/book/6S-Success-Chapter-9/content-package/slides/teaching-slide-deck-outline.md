### Slide 3 · Useful is not used

- Almost everything you own is useful. The spare key could open a car, the extra umbrella could keep a guest dry, the cable could charge a phone. Every case is valid, because usefulness is easy.
- When the test for keeping a thing is whether it could ever be useful, the test passes everything, and a test that passes everything is not a test at all.
- The whole chapter turns on one line: useful is not the same as used.

### Slide 4 · The clutter you cannot see

- A home fills up not with useless things, which are easy to spot and easy to release, but with useful things you never use.
- The hardest clutter to notice is not the junk. It is the sensible surplus, each piece defensible, that together buries the handful of things you reach for every day.
- Name the pattern: the just-in-case thing is a plausibly useful object kept for a someday that rarely comes, quietly taking the place of the things you use now.

[VISUAL] A basket where a few crisp, ink-outlined items sit at the front and a soft crowd of faintly greyed "just in case" items fills the space behind them. Honey #DDA63A tags on the crowd, green calm-dots on the few at the front. Slate label: "THE SENSIBLE SURPLUS."

### Slide 5 · The Use Test

- The fix is one honest question, asked of one object at a time, the same shape as last chapter's gate but narrower and sharper.
- When did you last use it? A fast, specific memory means the thing is necessary, and it stays. A shrug, or an invented scenario, means it is only useful in theory: unnecessary, and it goes in the out box.
- Necessary and unnecessary are not judgments of the object's quality. They are readings of your own behavior.

### Slide 6 · Necessary and unnecessary

- Necessary here does not mean you would perish without it. It means you actually use it, or would replace it if it vanished tonight, because you would feel its absence.
- Unnecessary does not mean worthless or foolish to own. It means the thing is only plausibly useful, kept just in case, while the things you truly use get crowded out.
- Sort by use, not by potential. Keep the first, release the second.

### Slide 7 · For the hard cases

- Most objects answer the question instantly, and you should trust the fast answer, because your hands know the truth before your pride does.
- But a few will be genuinely hard, and for those there is a tiebreaker that strips away the sunk cost: if it vanished today, would you buy it again?
- An easy yes keeps it. A slow no, or a wince, or a well, not really, is your answer. It was never about the object.

[VISUAL] A small honey #DDA63A side gate branching off the main flow with a dashed line, reading "If it vanished today, would you buy it again?" in Newsreader italic. Label: "FOR THE HARD CASES." Honey signals a waypoint, not a verdict.

### Slide 8 · Duplicates and surplus

- A second pattern the Use Test catches, hiding behind the most reasonable word in the language: backup.
- You do not need three umbrellas, you need the one you actually grab. You do not need a brick of forty tote bags, you need a working handful. Necessary quietly includes a sensible quantity.
- When you find several of a thing you need one or two of, keep your favorite and let the rest go. That is not deprivation. That is just picking the one that works.

### Slide 10 · The out box, and the honest maybe

- The out box is a set-aside spot, a box or a bag or a corner, where the things you have decided you do not use wait together while you finish the pass. It is not the donation pile, not the trash, not the garage sale table.
- Deciding a thing is unnecessary and deciding its fate are two separate acts. Today you decide. Later you route. Where the out box goes gets a whole chapter of its own.
- A few things will genuinely refuse to answer. Do not force those. Set an honest maybe aside on its own and keep going; it gets a system of its own next chapter.

### Slide 11 · Letting go is not wasting

- This is where a good pass most often dies, for a reason that has nothing to do with the objects: cost enters the room, and letting the thing go starts to feel like admitting you wasted money, so the hand stops. That is the sunk-cost pull.
- Look straight at the arithmetic: the money was spent when you bought it, and keeping it unused does not earn that back. It only charges you rent in space and attention.
- And letting go is not a confession. The reason you bought it passed, that is all. There is nothing to be ashamed of in owning a thing you no longer need.

### Slide 13 · Two loose ends (close + soft CTA)

- The basket now holds a working umbrella and a couple of bags, and you can see the bottom of it. Every object left has earned its place the only way that counts, by getting used.
- You kept what you use and set aside what you were only keeping just in case, without deciding anyone was foolish and without spending a cent, because the whole move was subtraction.
- But two loose ends remain: an out box full of unnecessary things with nowhere yet to go, and one or two objects you honestly could not call. Those are exactly what comes next.
- One Idea to Keep: The hardest clutter to see is the useful kind you never actually use. Keep what earns its place by getting used, and let go of what you were only keeping just in case.
