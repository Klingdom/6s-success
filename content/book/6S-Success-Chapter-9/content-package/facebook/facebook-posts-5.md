## Post 1 · One question for what stayed

You cleared a surface once and it stayed clear. Nice work. But there is usually a basket, or a drawer, or a shelf, still full of things that all belong there and never actually get used.

That is the trap this catches. Everything left after a first tidy belongs where it is. That is not the same as needing it.

So hold up one thing at a time and ask: When did you last use it?

That is the whole test. If the answer comes fast, this week, last month, the last time it rained, then it is doing its job and it stays. If you have to invent a reason you might use it someday rather than remember a time you did, then it was only useful in theory, and it goes in a set-aside spot for now.

One question, asked object by object, and the surplus quietly sorts itself.

## Post 2 · Useful is not the same as used

Here is the reason a home fills up even when you are careful. It is not the junk. Junk is easy to spot and easy to let go.

It is the useful stuff you never actually use.

Almost everything you own is useful in some imaginable situation. The spare key could open a car. The extra umbrella could keep a guest dry. That stack of bags could carry groceries. Every one of those is a valid reason, which is exactly the problem. When the test for keeping a thing is whether it could ever be useful, the test passes everything, and a test that passes everything is not a test at all.

So try the line the whole chapter turns on:

Useful is not the same as used.

Useful is a claim about the future: this could help me someday. Used is a fact about the past: this actually helped me, recently, in the real world. One is a story you tell about an object. The other is a record of what it has done.

Sort by the record, not the story.

## Post 3 · You need one, not five

Once you start sorting by use, one pattern shows up everywhere, and it hides behind the most reasonable word there is: backup.

You do not need three umbrellas. You need the one you actually grab when the sky looks wrong. The other two are not spares in any real sense. They are two more things to store, dust around, and step over, kept because letting a working umbrella go feels wrong, even though owning three you never open costs you more than one of them ever will.

Same with the wad of tote bags that breeds in every kitchen drawer. A household needs a small, working handful, not a compressed brick of forty.

Necessary quietly includes a sensible quantity. One good umbrella is necessary. The third is not, not because it is a bad umbrella, but because the job it does is already done by the first.

So when you find several of a thing you only need one or two of, keep your favorite and set the rest aside. That is not living without. That is just picking the one that works.

## Post 4 · A quick pass through the basket

Let me run this on one real spot so you can see how fast it goes. Picture the basket by the front door that catches the overflow.

Start with the easy keepers, because it feels good. One umbrella stays, the one you actually grab, and your hand already knows which. A reusable bag or two stays, the ones you reach for on the way to the shop. The keys stay, the leash stays. Each answers the question the same way, with a fast, specific memory of the last time it did its job.

Now the rest, and watch it thin. The two extra umbrellas you never open. The wad of surplus bags. A spare key to a car sold two summers ago. A fan of old takeout menus from before your phone knew every restaurant in town. A tangled dead cable for a phone you no longer own. Not one of them gets used, so each one goes in the set-aside spot.

Here is the lesson in one tipped-out basket. Every single thing in it belonged by a front door. Most of it was still unnecessary, not because it was in the wrong place, but because you do not use it.

## Post 5 · Letting go is not wasting

Here is where a good pass most often dies, and it has nothing to do with the objects.

You pick up something you do not use, and just as your hand moves to set it aside, a small voice reminds you what it cost. The umbrella was not free. The cable came with a phone you paid real money for. Suddenly letting it go stops feeling like clearing space and starts feeling like admitting you wasted money, which is much heavier, so the hand stops and the thing stays another year.

So look straight at the arithmetic, because it is kinder than it feels.

Letting go of what you do not use is not wasteful. The money was spent when you bought it, and keeping it unused does not earn that back. It only charges you rent in space and attention.

The cost is already settled. Keeping the thing does not undo the spending; it only adds a second, ongoing cost on top of the first. And letting go is not a confession either. You bought it for a reason that made sense at the time. The reason passed, that is all.

There is nothing to be ashamed of in owning a thing you no longer need. You are just allowed to stop paying to keep it.
