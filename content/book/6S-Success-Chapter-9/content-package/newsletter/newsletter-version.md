# Newsletter Edition: Necessary vs. Unnecessary

**Subject line (placeholder):** When did you last use it?

**Preview text (placeholder):** Everything left belongs here. That is not the same as needing it.

---

Hi friend,

Come back to the drop zone with me one more time. The console you cleared last issue is still clear, which is its own quiet miracle, because the things you carried off did not creep back overnight. What is left on the surface is exactly what you left there: a keys tray, a leash, an umbrella, a bag, and tomorrow's mail. Five working things. So we could stop here, and the entryway would already be better than it was.

But there is a basket on the floor beside the console, the one that has been catching the overflow for years, and if we are honest we both know what is in it. Three umbrellas, only one of which you ever open. A thick wad of folded tote bags. A spare key for a car you sold two summers ago. A fan of takeout menus. A dead charging cable for a phone you replaced ages ago. Every one of those things belongs at an entryway. And you have not touched most of them in a year.

That gap is what this chapter is about, and it is a different question from the one we just answered. Last chapter asked where a thing lives. This chapter asks whether you need to keep it at all. Everything in that basket passed the belonging test, because umbrellas and bags and keys are exactly what an entryway is for. Belonging is not the problem anymore. Use is.

Here is the trap, and it is a gentle, reasonable, expensive one. Almost everything you own is useful. The spare key could open a car. The extra umbrella could keep a guest dry. Point at any object and you can build a small, sincere case for why it might matter someday, and every case will be valid, because usefulness is easy. Almost anything is useful in some imaginable circumstance. So when the test for keeping a thing is whether it could ever be useful, the test passes everything, and a test that passes everything is not a test at all.

So the whole chapter turns on one short line, and I would put it on the fridge next to the before photo if I were you:

**Useful is not the same as used.**

Read it twice, because the difference is the entire game. Useful is a claim about the future: this thing could do something for me someday. Used is a fact about the past: this thing actually did something for me, recently, in the real world. A home fills up not with useless things, which are easy to spot and easy to release, but with useful things you never use, each one defensible, that together bury the handful of things you reach for every day.

The fix is small, and it is one honest question, asked of one object at a time:

**When did you last use it?**

That is the whole test. If the answer comes quickly, this week, last month, the last time it rained, then the thing is doing its job. It is necessary, and it stays. If the answer is a shrug, if you have to invent a scenario in which you might use it rather than recall a time you did, then it is only useful in theory. It is unnecessary, and it goes in what I call the out box.

Two words are doing careful work there. Necessary does not mean you would die without it. It means you actually use it, or that if it vanished tonight you would go out and get another, because you would feel its absence. Unnecessary does not mean worthless or foolish to own. It means the thing is only plausibly useful, kept just in case, while the things you truly use get less room because of it. Neither word is a judgment of the object. Both are readings of your own behavior, which is the only honest evidence either way.

Most objects answer instantly, and you should trust the fast answer. But a few will be genuinely hard, and for those there is a tiebreaker that cuts through the fog:

**If it vanished today, would you buy it again?**

It works because it strips away the sunk cost. You are no longer deciding whether to get rid of a thing you already own, which feels like a loss. You are deciding, with today's needs and today's money, whether you would go out and choose it fresh. An easy yes keeps it. A slow no, or a wince, is your answer, and it was never really about the object.

There is a second pattern this catches, and it hides behind the most reasonable word in the language: backup. You do not need three umbrellas. You need the one you actually grab. Necessary quietly includes a sensible quantity. When you find several of a thing you only need one or two of, keep your favorite and let the rest go. You are not being asked to live without; you are just picking the one that works.

Now, the out box. It is simply a set-aside spot, a box or a bag or a corner, where the things you have decided you do not use wait together while you finish the pass. That is all it is right now. It is not the donation pile, not the trash, not the garage sale table. Where those things go from here is a real decision with real care behind it, and it gets a whole chapter of its own a little further on. Today the out box has one job: to hold the unnecessary things in one place so the pass keeps moving. Today you decide. Later you route. And if one or two things sit in your hand and honestly refuse to answer, do not force them. An honest maybe is normal, not a failure. Set it aside on its own and keep going; the ones you cannot call get a system of their own next issue.

I want to slow down on one last thing, because this is where a good pass most often dies. You will pick up something you do not use, and just as your hand starts toward the out box, a small voice will remind you what it cost. And the moment cost enters the room, letting the thing go stops feeling like clearing space and starts feeling like admitting you wasted money. So look straight at the arithmetic, because it is kinder than it feels:

> Letting go of what you do not use is not wasteful. The money was spent when you bought it, and keeping it unused does not earn that back. It only charges you rent in space and attention.

The money is already gone. It went the day you bought it, and no amount of storing the thing unopened will return a cent of it. Keeping it does not undo the spending; it only adds a second, ongoing cost on top of the first. And letting go is not a confession, either. You bought it for a reason that likely made sense at the time. The reason passed, that is all. There is nothing to be ashamed of in owning a thing you no longer need.

Now go look at the friction meter, because it has moved a second time. Objects left again, so the needle steps again, further toward calm. But the step is smaller than the last one, and it should be. The first pass cleared the big backlog of what did not belong here at all. This pass cleared a smaller pile, the things that belonged but you did not use. A shorter step for a smaller clearing is not the meter losing steam. It is the meter being accurate. And the goal is still ahead of it, because Sort itself is not even finished.

**To try this week:** Pick up one thing you have been keeping just in case. Ask when you last used it. If you cannot remember, put it in an out box today. Used it recently, or you would replace it, means it stays. One honest release is a full pass. Do more if it feels good, park the honest maybes in their own spot, and stop before it turns into a chore.
