## SECTION 1: A DIFFERENT QUESTION

[HOST]
So we could stop. The entryway is already better than it was. But that basket is where the last of the clutter has been hiding, in plain sight, and it is hiding behind a very reasonable idea.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Last chapter asked where a thing lives. This chapter asks whether you need to keep it at all.]

[HOST, voice over]
Last chapter asked where a thing lives. This chapter asks whether you need to keep it at all. Everything in that basket passed the belonging test, because umbrellas and bags and keys are exactly what an entryway is for. Belonging is not the problem anymore. Use is. And the gap between the two, between what fits here and what you actually reach for, is the whole subject of today.

---

## SECTION 2: USEFUL IS NOT USED

[HOST, on camera]
Here is the trap, and it is a gentle, reasonable, expensive one, which is why nearly everyone falls into it. Almost everything you own is useful. The spare key could open a car. The extra umbrella could keep a guest dry. Point at any object in that basket and you can build a small, sincere case for why it might matter someday.

[HOST]
That is exactly the problem. When the test for keeping a thing is whether it could ever be useful, the test passes everything, and a test that passes everything is not a test at all.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Useful is not the same as used.]

[HOST, voice over]
So the whole chapter turns on one short line. Useful is not the same as used. Read it twice, because the difference is the entire game. Useful is a claim about the future: this thing could do something for me, someday. Used is a fact about the past: this thing actually did something for me, recently, in the real world. A home fills up not with useless things, which are easy to release, but with useful things you never use. The hardest clutter to notice is not the junk. It is the sensible surplus, each piece defensible, that together buries the handful of things you reach for every day.

---

## SECTION 3: THE USE TEST

[HOST]
The fix is one honest question, asked of one object at a time. Where Sort held up each thing and asked whether it belonged, this pass holds up each thing that stayed and asks something narrower.

[VISUAL: The hero infographic, "The Use Test." An object enters a gate. The question sits on the gate. A green branch goes up to "necessary, keep it." A slate branch goes down to "unnecessary, the out box." A small honey side gate reads "would you buy it again?"]

[ON SCREEN TEXT: When did you last use it?]

[HOST, voice over]
When did you last use it? That is the whole test. Not whether it is nice, not whether it cost money, not whether it might come in handy. Just this: when, actually, did you last put it to work? If the answer comes quickly, this week, last month, the last time it rained, then the thing is doing its job. It is necessary, and it stays. If the answer is a shrug, if you have to invent a scenario rather than recall a real one, then the thing is only useful in theory. It is unnecessary, and it goes in the out box, a set-aside spot we will come back to.

[HOST, on camera]
Two words are doing careful work there. Necessary does not mean you would die without it. It means you actually use it, or that if it vanished tonight you would go out and get another. Unnecessary does not mean worthless or foolish to own. It means the thing is only plausibly useful, kept just in case. Necessary and unnecessary are not judgments of the object's quality. They are readings of your own behavior, which is the only honest evidence either way.

[HOST]
Most objects answer instantly, and you should trust the fast answer, because your hands know the truth before your pride does. But a few are genuinely hard, and for those there is a tiebreaker.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: If it vanished today, would you buy it again?]

[HOST, voice over]
If it vanished today, would you buy it again? This one strips away the sunk cost. You are no longer deciding whether to get rid of a thing you already own, which feels like a loss. You are deciding, with today's needs and today's money, whether you would choose it fresh. An easy yes keeps it. A slow no, or a wince, is your answer, and it was never really about the object.

---

## SECTION 4: DUPLICATES AND SURPLUS

[HOST, on camera]
There is a second pattern the Use Test catches, and it hides behind the most reasonable word in the language: backup.

[VISUAL: The duplicates-sweep infographic. A row of five identical tote bags. One is kept with a green dot. The other four are arrowed to the out box. Caption: you need one, not five.]

[HOST]
You do not need three umbrellas. You need the one you actually grab when the sky looks wrong. The same is true of the wad of tote bags. A household needs a small, working handful, not the compressed brick of forty that breeds in every kitchen drawer in the country. Necessary quietly includes a sensible quantity.

[HOST, voice over]
So the Use Test has a companion move, and it is easy: when you find several of a thing you only need one or two of, keep your favorite and let the rest go. Keep the umbrella that opens without a fight, the bags you actually like carrying. You are not being asked to live without. You are being asked to stop keeping the worse copy of a thing you already own a better version of. If two things do one job, one of them is surplus, and it is almost always the one you never reach for.

---

## SECTION 5: A PASS THROUGH THE BASKET

[HOST]
Let us actually run it, over the basket beside the console. This is a small space, so this is a quick pass. Start with the clear keepers, because it is good for morale. One umbrella stays, the one you actually grab. A bag or two stays. The keys stay, the leash stays. Each one answers with a fast, specific memory of the last time it did its job. That fast answer is the sound of a necessary thing.

[HOST, on camera]
Now watch the rest thin. The two extra umbrellas: when did you last open either one? You cannot say, because you always take the good one. Out box. The surplus tote bags: out box. The spare key opens a car you sold, so it is not even useful in theory anymore. The takeout menus are from before your phone knew every restaurant in town. The dead cable charges nothing you own. Out box, out box, out box, with a clear conscience. Every single thing in that basket belonged at an entryway. And most of it was still unnecessary, because you do not use it.

---

## SECTION 6: LETTING GO IS NOT WASTING

[HOST]
Now I want to slow down, because this is where a good pass most often dies, and it dies for a reason that has nothing to do with the objects.

[B-ROLL: A hand lifts the dead cable toward the out box, pauses, hesitates, as if remembering what it cost.]

[HOST, voice over]
You will pick up something you do not use, and a small voice will remind you what it cost. The umbrella was not free. The cable came with a phone you paid real money for. And letting the thing go stops feeling like clearing space and starts feeling like admitting you wasted money. So the hand stops, and the object goes back to keep costing you quietly for another year. That is the sunk-cost pull.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Letting go of what you do not use is not wasteful.]

[HOST, on camera]
So look straight at the arithmetic. The money for the extra umbrella is gone. It went the day you bought it, and no amount of storing it unopened will return a cent. Keeping the thing does not undo the spending. It only adds a second cost on top of the first: the room it takes and the attention it draws. Holding an unused object to honor the money you spent is like leaving a light on in an empty room to get your money's worth from the bulb. The waste, if there is any, already happened. Letting go is not the waste. It is the end of paying twice. And it is not a confession either. The reason you bought it passed. That is all.

## CLOSE AND SOFT CTA

[HOST]
Look at the basket now. It holds a working umbrella and a couple of bags, and you can see the bottom of it, and everything in it is something your hands will reach for this week. Nothing is standing guard against a someday. You kept what you use. You set aside what you were only keeping just in case. And you did it without deciding anyone was foolish, and without spending a cent, because the whole move was subtraction.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Keep what earns its place by getting used.]

[HOST, on camera]
So here is your job. Pick up one thing you have been keeping just in case. Ask when you last used it. If you cannot remember, put it in an out box today. One honest release is a full pass.
