## You are here blurb
You are at Chapter 33, the third chapter of Part Nine, Room-by-Room Playbooks, and the third of twenty room playbooks in the applied half of the book. Behind you are Chapter 31, the entryway, which proved the format on the smallest room, and Chapter 32, the kitchen, which pointed it at the busiest; here the same method steps through into the kitchen's storeroom. The pantry is where the food waits, and it is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin, because a pantry you cannot see grows a layer of forgotten, out-of-date, half-used food at the back, in the dark. It is also, in one specific way, the room where cleaning matters most for safety, and it is not grease here, it is dust, dates, damp, and the things that get in, so the clean is genuinely an inspection: the chewed bag corner, the fine webbing in the flour, the bulging can lid, and the soft dark patch of damp cardboard are all early warnings you find precisely because your hands are on every surface. That is why this chapter, like the two before it, is mostly about exactly how to clean each zone with the precise inputs. A playbook does not re-teach you Sort or Shine, and it does not stop at telling you what to do: for every small zone it gives you the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, dry-first so flour never turns to paste. And there is one rule here that overrides almost everything else, said up front: in a pantry you clean dry before wet, always, because flour and grain dust turn to a grey paste the instant a wet cloth touches them, so vacuum or brush first, wipe second, dry last. The reason a whole pantry feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one jumbled job, and it is not: it is five small zones wearing a single name, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the cupboard and start seeing the zones. The hero move, frozen: the pantry is five small zones, not one big job, the dry goods shelves, the canned and jarred goods, the baking zone, the snack and lunch zone, and the backstock and bulk, and you do them one at a time; start with the dry goods or the cans for the biggest win, because those two hold the most that can spoil and the most that hides. It moves briskly through the quick passes and then slows right down for Shine, recapping the universal nine-rule Shine method and adding the pantry's dry-first layer, the vacuum or the dry brush first, the damp cloth second, and the dry cloth last, every single time, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling. Then, as the heart of the chapter, Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It gives every zone the same three things, the exact inputs to gather into the caddy, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there, because Shine finds the hazards and the safety pass fixes them. It lands the definition, frozen: a finished pantry is one you can shop from at a glance, every opened bag sealed in a dated container you can read the level of from the doorway, cans in rows with the label out and the oldest date at the front, one lift-out baking bin, a child-height snack bin holding only pre-approved snacks, and a backstock shelf where every pack carries its arrival month and nothing is stacked in front of anything else. It names the room's food-guilt calls, runs the pantry's real hazards through the five safety questions, and closes with the Part 9 signature, the before-and-after pair of the open shelves shot head-on from the doorway, the before kept as private proof and the after kept as the standard the shelves have to match. The rule to carry: a pantry is not clean because the shelves are wiped, it is clean because you took every board dry-first, dated every open bag, faced the oldest to the front, and flagged the chewed corner and the bulging lid you found on the way, so point the loop at one zone this weekend, the dry goods or the cans, and shop from a store cupboard you can actually see. You have done the two rooms that feed the house, the kitchen and its storeroom, and Part Nine hands off to Chapter 34, The Dining Room, worked the same way.
