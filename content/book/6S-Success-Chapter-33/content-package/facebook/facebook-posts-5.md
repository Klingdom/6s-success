## Post 1 · In a pantry you clean dry before wet, always

Most of us clean a pantry by grabbing a damp cloth, which is exactly the wrong first move. In here the enemy is not grease, it is dust and damp, and one rule overrides everything else.

"In a pantry, dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it. So the rule is dry before wet, without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling."

Vacuum or brush first, wipe second, dry last. Do it in the other order and the flour smears into a grey skin you then have to scrape. That is the whole difference between a shelf that stays fresh and one that starts to smell. This is from Chapter 33, The Pantry, of 6S Success: Home Edition, the third of the room-by-room playbooks. ## Post 2 · The pantry is five small zones, not one big job

The reason the pantry feels like too much is that you are looking at it as one job. It is not.

"You do not clean "the pantry"; you clean the dry goods shelf, which is done when every board is dust-free and bone-dry and every open bag is sealed and dated or gone."

So here is the map for the whole room:

"The pantry is five small zones, not one big job: the dry goods shelves, the canned and jarred goods, the baking zone, the snack and lunch zone, and the backstock and bulk. Do them one at a time and the storeroom that feeds the kitchen comes back to you a zone at a time."

Pick one. The dry goods shelf or the cans is a good place to start, because those two hold the most that can spoil and the most that hides. Read the free chapter for the exact steps.

## Post 3 · In the pantry, cleaning is genuinely an inspection

Here is the one that changes how you see the whole room. When you wipe a pantry shelf, you are not just tidying, you are checking your food is safe.

"The pantry is the one room where cleaning is genuinely an inspection for safety, because the things you find as you wipe, a chewed corner, fine webbing in the flour, a bulging can lid, a soft dark patch of damp cardboard, are early warnings you cannot afford to miss. You are cleaning the shelf, yes, but really you are checking that nothing has got in and nothing is quietly turning."

So lift every container off the board rather than wipe around it, and read what you find as you go. The clean is the inspection. More in Chapter 33, The Pantry, of 6S Success: Home Edition, free to read online.

## Post 4 · The sticky ring under the honey jar is an invitation

Here is the small thing that quietly turns a store cupboard into a feeding station. A tacky ring you left behind is not just untidy.

"The sticky ring under a honey jar is not just untidy, it is an invitation. A tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so in the pantry you clean until the shelf squeaks, because clean here is the difference between a store cupboard and a feeding station."

That last line is the one that changes how people clean. Lift the honey and the jam off, wipe under them, and clean until the shelf actually squeaks. A sticky edge is how the ants find the row, so this is the one place a wipe-around genuinely costs you. Read the free chapter.

## Post 5 · A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time

Here is the money in this room, and it is not about willpower. A pantry you cannot see is one you keep paying for twice.

"A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time, at the back, in the dark. The fix is not more willpower, it is a rule the shelf keeps for you: every container dated the day you open it, every row loaded from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and nothing bought until you can see that you are out."

So date every open bag the day you open it, load every row from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and buy nothing until you can see you are out. The shelf keeps the rule for you.
