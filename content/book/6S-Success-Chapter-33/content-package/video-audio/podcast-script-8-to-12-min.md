## INTRO

Hey, welcome back. Last time we did the kitchen, and now we step out of it into its storeroom. The pantry is where the food waits, and it is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin, because a pantry you cannot see is a pantry that grows a layer of forgotten, out-of-date, half-used food at the back, in the dark, where good intentions go to expire. Clean it and sort it well, and you stop wasting food, you stop buying what you already own, and you gain back a surprising amount of a weekly shop.

And here is the thing that makes the pantry different from the kitchen next door. It is not grease here. It is dust, dates, damp, and pests, and, in one specific way, the clean is genuinely an inspection. The chewed bag corner, the fine webbing in the flour, the bulging can lid, the soft dark patch of damp cardboard are all early warnings you find precisely because your hands are on every surface. So this episode, like the kitchen, is mostly about exactly how to clean each zone, with the right inputs and the steps in the right order, but with one rule that overrides everything else, and I want to say it up front. In a pantry you clean dry before wet, always. Flour and grain dust turn to a grey paste the instant a wet cloth touches them, so a wet wipe on a floury shelf does not clean it, it smears it into a skin you then have to scrape off. Vacuum or brush first, wipe second, dry last. Hold that one rule and the pantry is easy. Break it and you make more work than you started with.

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: FIVE ZONES, NOT ONE CUPBOARD

First, why the whole pantry feels like too much. A pantry looks like one jumbled job, and faced that way it is the sort of thing you close the door on. Faced as its zones, it is five short, finishable pieces, and the worst of them, the dry goods shelf, is also the one that pays back most. As at the door and in the kitchen, the trick is to stop seeing the cupboard and see the zones. Here is the map, frozen. The pantry is five small zones, not one big job: the dry goods shelves, the canned and jarred goods, the baking zone, the snack and lunch zone, and the backstock and bulk. Do them one at a time and the storeroom that feeds the kitchen comes back to you a zone at a time.

Each of those has a clear edge and a clear finish. You do not clean the pantry, you clean the dry goods shelf, which is done when every board is dust-free and bone-dry and every open bag is sealed and dated or gone. And if you want the highest payback for the least effort, start with the dry goods or the cans, because those two hold the most that can spoil and the most that hides.

Now, as before, four of the passes are quick, so let me move through them fast. Sort each zone before you clean it: bin or give away the open bag you cannot name a meal for, the can two years past its date, the baking kit for the baker you never became. Straighten the survivors by use and by date: staples in one container style, cans by category on risers, one lift-out baking bin, snacks split into a child-height bin and an adult shelf. And Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short. Standardize is actually the pantry's secret weapon, because this is the room a good standard runs almost by itself: every container dated the day it is opened, every can row labels-out with the oldest at the front, a maximum written on the shelf edge for each backstock line. That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: THE PANTRY SHINE METHOD

You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one of them still holds: work top to bottom, dry or clear before wet, back to front and clean to dirty, mist the cloth not the surface, two cloths one to clean and one to dry, colour-code your cloths, match the cleaner to the surface, give it dwell time, and clean to inspect and flag rather than fix. As the book puts it, you know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and they all hold here. The pantry adds one overriding principle and one shift in what you are really doing.

Here is the principle, frozen. In a pantry, dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it. So the rule is dry before wet, without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling. That governs every zone below: the vacuum or the dry brush comes first, the damp cloth second, and the dry cloth last, every single time. It is the opposite of the instinct to reach for a wet cloth, and it is the single thing that separates a pantry that stays fresh from one that turns musty.

And here is the shift in what you are really doing, frozen. The pantry is the one room where cleaning is genuinely an inspection for safety, because the things you find as you wipe, a chewed corner, fine webbing in the flour, a bulging can lid, a soft dark patch of damp cardboard, are early warnings you cannot afford to miss. You are cleaning the shelf, yes, but really you are checking that nothing has got in and nothing is quietly turning. So keep your eyes open as your hands move.

And keep one more thing in mind, because it is where most pantry problems literally begin. The sticky ring under a honey jar is not just untidy, it is an invitation. A tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so in the pantry you clean until the shelf squeaks, because clean here is the difference between a store cupboard and a feeding station. The good news is the kit is simple and cheap, because the pantry's enemy is dust rather than grease, so there is no degreaser and no heavy chemistry. The most important tool in the room is a vacuum with a brush and a crevice attachment, not a bottle of anything.

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: ZONE BY ZONE, EXACTLY HOW TO CLEAN IT

Now the centerpiece. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather into your caddy, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Let me walk the dry goods shelves in full, because they are the showcase, then take you briskly through the other four.

The dry goods shelves: the grains, pasta, and cereals you cook from every week. Grain dust is the whole story here, so this is a dry-first zone top to bottom. Your inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush and crevice attachments, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber set, a small detail brush, mild dish soap and warm water for scoops, and your caddy. The steps, in order. One, start at the top shelf and the wall behind it, and vacuum first, so nothing you knock loose lands on shelves you have already done, then follow with a barely damp cloth and take it dry before you move down. Two, clear and do each board in turn, take everything off rather than wiping around it, vacuum the loose flour and grain dust first, then wipe with a well-wrung cloth, then change to a dry cloth and take the board bone-dry, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling. Three, do the front lip and shelf edge, where flour dust drifts forward and hands brush every time they reach in. Four, wipe the containers, lids, and scoops, paying attention to the lid seal and the pour spout, and wash any scoop that lives in the grain in warm soapy water and dry it fully. Five, clear the back corners and the seam with the crevice tool or a dry detail brush, dry brush first always, because a wet cloth just turns flour to paste in the corner. Six, do the floor last, vacuum under the lowest board where spilled grains gather, then wipe and take it dry.

Now the other four, same shape, and I will flag the one move in each that people miss. Zone two, the canned and jarred goods: lift the cans off in rows and vacuum the board and the risers, and here is the move, work the sticky rings under the honey and syrup and sauce jars with a warm damp cloth until the shelf squeaks, because a tacky edge left behind is exactly how ants find the row, then wipe the can rims and set every can back label-out with the oldest date at the front. Zone three, the baking zone: brush or vacuum the loose flour off the board completely while it is dry before anything wet touches it, because wetting flour first just smears it into a grey skin you have to scrape, then wipe the containers and the one-handed lid, wash the scoop rather than burying it back damp, and here is the one people skip, wipe the allergen containers, the nut flours and sesame, with their own dedicated cloth and wash their scoops separately, so no trace travels down the shelf. Zone four, the snack and lunch zone: do the higher adult shelf first, then empty the low snack bin completely and wipe right into the bottom corners where crushed crackers and pouch drips collect, match the containers to their lids, and here is the move, wash the drink bottles and run a bottle brush down the spout, the straw, and the screw threads where a sour film hides, then turn every lunch bag inside out and wash the liners. And zone five, the backstock and bulk: wipe the shelf-edge labels so the numbers stay readable, wipe the tops of the cases and sacks, and here is the whole point of the zone, lift the bottom case and feel the underside and the board beneath it for damp, because the cardboard is your early warning that a concrete floor or an outside wall is sweating, then sweep the floor from the back wall out to the door.

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: THE CALLS, AND THE HAZARDS YOU FLAGGED

Cleaning to a method makes the pantry fresh, but a few honest decisions decide whether it stays readable, and the pantry's are mostly about food you feel guilty binning. The first is the bag you opened for one recipe, the half kilo of buckwheat, the coarse polenta, the rice flour from a week of enthusiastic bread. Settle it with a rule: if it is open and you cannot name the specific meal you will cook with it in the next fourteen days, it leaves now; if it is unopened and in date, give it away while it is still good. The second is the best-before you keep arguing with, and here is the reframe, frozen. A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time, at the back, in the dark. The fix is not more willpower, it is a rule the shelf keeps for you: every container dated the day you open it, every row loaded from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and nothing bought until you can see that you are out. Best-before is about quality, not safety, so anything past it goes into a meal in the next seven days or it goes out. And the third is the warehouse pack that was not a saving: for each bulk line, write down the largest quantity you have ever genuinely finished before it turned, and make that your maximum, because a cheaper unit price you throw half away at is not a saving.

And remember, all through that clean you were flagging hazards, not fixing them. This is where the safety pass acts on them. Run the five safety questions once directly. For poison, choke, or strangle, treat a bulging, leaking, or hissing can as spoiled and bag it, because no amount of cooking makes it safe; keep allergen flours sealed with their own scoop so nothing cross-contacts; and keep hard sweets, whole nuts, and small round cheeses out of an open bin a small child can reach. For fall, cut, or crush, keep dense rows of cans and heavy glass grain jars off shelves above eye level, where a dropped tin is a broken toe and a dropped jar is broken glass on a hard floor, and lift a heavy sack with your legs and no twist. And for the plain fall, reach the top shelf from a stable step stool rather than a chair, keep a child from climbing the shelving toward a high snack, and wipe spilled flour and sugar off the floor at once because they are genuinely slick. Shine found them; the safety pass fixes them.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So here is your one takeaway, frozen. A pantry is not clean because the shelves are wiped. It is clean because you took every board dry-first, dated every open bag, faced the oldest to the front, and flagged the chewed corner and the bulging lid you found on the way. Do one zone this weekend, the dry goods or the cans, and shop from a store cupboard you can actually see.

And here is your assignment, the smallest possible proof of the whole idea. Do the dry goods shelf dry-first. Clear one board completely, vacuum the flour and grain dust off it while it is dry, then, and only then, wipe it and take it bone-dry. Reseal or bin every open bag as it comes off, dating the keepers. One board, and the shelf stops smelling and starts reading at a glance. Take the before photo first, from the doorway, because you will want the proof.
