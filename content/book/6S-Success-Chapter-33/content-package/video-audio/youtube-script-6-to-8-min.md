As at the door and in the kitchen, the trick is to stop seeing the cupboard and see the zones. Each one has a clear edge and a clear finish. You do not clean the pantry, you clean the dry goods shelf, which is done when every board is dust-free and bone-dry and every open bag is sealed and dated or gone.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The pantry is five small zones, not one big job.]

Do them one at a time and the storeroom that feeds the kitchen comes back to you a zone at a time. And if you want the highest payback for the least effort, start with the dry goods or the cans, because those two hold the most that can spoil and the most that hides. Sort each zone first, straighten what survives by use and by date, and then we slow right down for the part that matters most: Shine.

## SECTION 2: THE PANTRY SHINE METHOD

[HOST]
You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one still holds. The pantry simply adds one overriding principle and one shift in what you are really doing.

[VISUAL: A pantry shelf with the order of work as a numbered path: dry first, a vacuum brush and a dry hand brush lifting dust off a bare board with a "flour plus water equals paste, so never wet first" warning; then top to bottom, an arrow down from the top shelf to the floor; then wipe, a barely-damp cloth; then dry, a dry cloth taking the board bone-dry. Small icons for "hunt the sticky ring" and "the clean is an inspection". A foot line reads "dry before wet, vacuum then wipe then dry, date it, oldest to the front".]

[HOST, voice over]
Here is the principle, frozen. In a pantry, dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it. So the rule is dry before wet, without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling. That governs every zone: the vacuum or the dry brush comes first, the damp cloth second, the dry cloth last, every single time.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Dry before wet, always. Vacuum or brush, then wipe, then dry.]

[HOST, on camera]
And here is the shift. The pantry is the one room where cleaning is genuinely an inspection for safety, because the things you find as you wipe, a chewed corner, fine webbing in the flour, a bulging can lid, a soft dark patch of damp cardboard, are early warnings you cannot afford to miss. One more thing to keep in mind: hunt the sticky ring, because a tacky edge under a honey jar is exactly how ants find the row, so you clean until the shelf squeaks. And the good news is the kit is simple and cheap. The most important tool in the room is a vacuum with a brush and a crevice attachment, not a bottle of anything, because the pantry's enemy is dust, not grease. Shine finds the hazards; the safety pass fixes them.

## SECTION 3: ZONE BY ZONE, EXACTLY HOW TO CLEAN IT

[HOST]
So let me show you the method cleaning the showcase zone, the dry goods shelves, and then take you fast through the rest.

[VISUAL: The dry goods shelf cleaned in numbered sequence, 1 to 6. A caddy set down first with its inputs as labelled icons: a compact vacuum with brush and crevice tools, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber set, a detail brush, dish soap and warm water for scoops. Then the top shelf and back wall vacuumed, each board cleared and the flour vacuumed off before any cloth touches it, the board taken bone-dry with a second dry cloth, the scoop washed and dried, the back corners dry-brushed, the floor last.]

[HOST, voice over]
Gather the inputs into a caddy first, and hold the rule above every board: dry first, wipe second, dry last. Then, in order. One, start at the top shelf and the back wall, and vacuum first, so nothing you knock loose lands on shelves you have done, then wipe and take it dry before you move down. Two, clear each board, take everything off, vacuum the flour and grain dust first, wipe with a well-wrung cloth, then a dry cloth bone-dry, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling. Three, do the front lip and shelf edge. Four, wipe the containers, lids, and scoops, and wash any scoop that lives in the grain and dry it fully. Five, clear the back corners and the seam with the crevice tool or a dry detail brush, dry brush first always. Six, the floor last.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Vacuum first. Wipe second. A dry cloth, bone-dry. Floor last.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the other four run the same way. The canned and jarred goods, where you work the sticky rings under the honey and syrup jars until the shelf squeaks, then set every can label-out with the oldest date at the front. The baking zone, where you brush the board dry before anything wet touches it, because flour turns to paste, and give the allergen containers their own cloth and a separate scoop. The snack and lunch zone, where you empty the low bin and wipe its bottom corners, then run a bottle brush down the spout, the straw, and the screw threads where a sour film hides. And the backstock and bulk, where you lift the bottom case and feel the underside for damp, because the cardboard is your early warning, then sweep the floor from the back wall out to the door.

## SECTION 4: THE CALLS, AND THE HAZARDS YOU FLAGGED

[HOST]
Cleaning to a method makes the pantry fresh, but a few honest decisions decide whether it stays readable, and while you cleaned, you were flagging hazards to act on now.

[VISUAL: Three quick beats, the open bag settled with the fourteen-day rule, the best-before argued with over a can then given a seven-day deadline, the bulk line given a written maximum on the shelf edge. Then the safety pass acting on what the clean flagged: the bulging can bagged, the allergen flour sealed with its own scoop, the shelving a child climbs anchored.]

[HOST, voice over]
The bag you opened for one recipe is settled with a rule: if it is open and you cannot name the specific meal you will cook with it in the next fourteen days, it leaves now. The best-before you keep arguing with: best-before is about quality, not safety, so anything past it goes into a meal in the next seven days or it goes out. Here is the frozen reframe. A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time, at the back, in the dark. The fix is not more willpower, it is a rule the shelf keeps for you: every container dated the day you open it, every row loaded from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and nothing bought until you can see that you are out.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Date it the day you open it. Load from behind. Oldest to the front.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the pantry's hazards are quieter than the kitchen's but no less real, so run the five safety questions once directly. Poison, choke, or strangle: treat a bulging, leaking, or hissing can as spoiled and bag it, because no cooking makes it safe, keep allergen flours sealed with their own scoop, and keep hard sweets and whole nuts out of an open bin a small child can reach. Fall, cut, or crush: keep dense rows of cans and heavy glass jars off shelves above eye level, and lift a heavy sack with your legs and no twist. And the plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool, never a chair, keep a child from climbing the shelving, and wipe spilled flour and sugar off the floor at once because they are genuinely slick. Shine found them; the safety pass fixes them.
