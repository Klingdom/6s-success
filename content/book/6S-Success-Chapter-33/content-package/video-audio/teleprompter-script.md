# Teleprompter Script: The Pantry

**Chapter 33, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** the-pantry
**Length:** about 950 words. Narration only. Short lines for easy reading. This is the THIRD room of Part Nine, and its heart is HOW TO CLEAN each pantry zone DRY-FIRST, so read it warm, capable, and a little proud; you already own the method, now clean the cupboard that feeds you so you can actually see it. No hype, no shame about the before.

---

[Read at a calm, even pace. Pause at each blank line.]

You have done the kitchen. Now step out into its storeroom.

The pantry is where the food waits.

And it is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin.

Because a pantry you cannot see grows a layer of forgotten food.

At the back, in the dark, where good intentions go to expire.

Clean it and sort it well, and you stop wasting food.

You stop buying what you already own.

Now, the pantry is different from the kitchen next door.

Here it is not grease. It is dust, dates, damp, and pests.

And, in one specific way, the clean is genuinely an inspection.

The chewed corner. The webbing in the flour. The bulging can lid.

The soft dark patch of damp cardboard.

All early warnings you find because your hands are on every surface.

And there is one rule here that overrides everything else.

In a pantry, you clean dry before wet, always.

Flour and grain dust turn to a grey paste the instant a wet cloth touches them.

So a wet wipe on a floury shelf does not clean it. It smears it.

Vacuum or brush first. Wipe second. Dry last.

Hold that one rule, and the pantry is easy.

Now, a whole pantry feels like too much.

Because you are looking at it as one jumbled job.

It is not.

The pantry is five small zones, not one big job.

The dry goods shelves. The canned and jarred goods.

The baking zone. The snack and lunch zone.

And the backstock and bulk.

Do them one at a time, and the storeroom comes back to you.

Before the clean, four passes are quick.

Sort each zone first, binning the bag you cannot name a meal for.

Straighten what survives by use and by date.

Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine.

Now we slow right down, for Shine.

You already know the nine rules from the door.

Top to bottom. Dry or clear before wet. Back to front, clean to dirty.

Mist the cloth, not the surface. Two cloths, one to clean, one to dry.

Colour-code your cloths. Match the cleaner to the surface.

Give it dwell time. And clean to inspect and flag, do not fix.

The pantry adds one overriding principle, and one shift.

The principle is the dust rule.

Dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste when wet.

So, dry before wet, without exception.

Vacuum or brush the board dry, wipe only after, take it bone-dry.

The shift is this. Here, the clean is genuinely an inspection.

You are cleaning the shelf, yes.

But really you are checking that nothing has got in, and nothing is turning.

And hunt the sticky ring.

A tacky edge under a honey jar is exactly how ants find the row.

The kit is simple and cheap, because the enemy is dust, not grease.

The most important tool is a vacuum with a brush and a crevice tool.

Not a bottle of anything.

Now watch that method clean the showcase zone, the dry goods shelves.

Gather the inputs into your caddy first.

A vacuum with brush and crevice tools. A multi-surface cleaner.

A colour-coded cloth set. A detail brush. Dish soap for the scoops.

Step one. Start at the top shelf and the back wall.

Vacuum first, so nothing lands on shelves you have done.

Step two. Clear each board. Take everything off.

Vacuum the flour and grain dust first.

Then wipe with a well-wrung cloth. Then a dry cloth, bone-dry.

Step three. Do the front lip and the shelf edge.

Step four. Wipe the containers, lids, and scoops.

And wash the scoop that lives in the grain. Dry it fully.

Step five. Clear the back corners and the seam.

Dry-brush first, always, because a wet cloth just makes paste.

Step six. Do the floor last.

That is the shelf that pays back the most, cleaned dry-first.

The other four zones run the very same way.

The cans, where you work the sticky rings until the shelf squeaks.

And set every can label-out, with the oldest date at the front.

The baking zone, where you brush the board dry before anything wet.

And give the allergen containers their own cloth and a separate scoop.

The snack and lunch, where you empty the low bin and wipe its corners.

And run a bottle brush down the spout, the straw, and the threads.

And the backstock, where you lift the bottom case.

And feel the underside for damp, the warning that arrives before the food spoils.

Then a few honest calls hold the room.

The open bag: name a meal in fourteen days, or it leaves now.

The best-before you keep arguing with, given a seven-day deadline.

And the warehouse pack, capped at what you have ever actually finished.

When the five zones are done, take the second photograph.

Stand in the doorway, framing the open shelves head-on.

The before is the valuable one, the proof.

The after is the standard, the picture the shelves have to match.

A pantry is not clean because the shelves are wiped.

It is clean because you took every board dry-first.

Because you dated every open bag and faced the oldest to the front.

And flagged the chewed corner and the bulging lid you found.

It is the room that quietly hands back a chunk of your grocery budget.

And now it is clean and legible.
