**The recurring motif.** The hero of the chapter is HOW to clean the kitchen's storeroom to a professional standard, one small zone at a time, and the single element that makes the pantry different from the kitchen: not grease but dust, dates, damp, and the things that get in. Dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it, so the one rule that overrides all others is dry before wet, always: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe second, take it bone-dry, the floor last, hunt the sticky ring, and read the clean as an inspection for pests and damp. The deeper hero, the thing this chapter spends most of its length on, is the universal nine-rule Shine method carried from Chapter 31 with the dry-first layer added on top: nine rules learned once and applied five times, zone by zone, each with its exact inputs and its ordered, numbered steps, the dry goods shelves and the baking zone given the most detail. That distinction, a tidy pantry versus a genuinely clean, dated, readable one, is the point: anyone can straighten a shelf, but a genuinely clean pantry is one where every board is dust-free and bone-dry, every open bag is sealed and dated, the sticky ring is gone, and the chewed corner and the bulging lid have been flagged. One image carries the whole set: the pantry drawn in two states side by side, on the left "before", open bags slumped and shedding dust, cans facing every way so no date reads, a floury baking shelf with a buried scoop, a backstock leaning into the doorway, the whole side in a muted graying tone, and on the right "after", the same shelves in green, staples in matching airtight dated containers whose levels read from the doorway, cans in labelled rows on risers with the oldest date at the front, a dust-free baking shelf with one lift-out bin, a backstock at waist height with the arrival month on every pack, so the promise of the whole chapter is clear in a single picture, the same cupboard, two states, transformed not by buying anything new but by pointing the loop at it and cleaning each zone dry-first. The arc of the chapter is the move from seeing the pantry as one jumbled job you close the door on to seeing it as five small finishable zones, through the case that the pantry is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin and the room where cleaning matters most for safety, to the freeing idea itself (five zones, not one cupboard) with the rule and the definition of a finished pantry, to the four quick passes and then the deep slow-down for Shine, to the nine-rule method and the dust rule taught once (the memorable card), to the method-and-kit figure with dry before wet at its centre, to the zone-by-zone procedures walked one at a time with their inputs and ordered steps, to clean-to-inspect-and-flag and the safety pass that fixes, to the genuinely hard calls (the bag opened for one recipe, the best-before you keep arguing with, the baking kit for the baker you meant to become, the warehouse pack that was not a saving), and finally to the Part 9 signature that closes every room, the before-and-after pair and the two photos to take. And the difference the whole chapter turns on: a tidy pantry and a genuinely clean, dated, readable one are not the same thing, and the difference is method plus the dry-first rule.

## Beat 10 · Clean to inspect, and flag; the safety pass fixes

The idea that runs under every zone procedure, given its own beat: cleaning is how you inspect, so while your hand is on every surface your eye is inspecting it, and when you find the thing you never normally see you flag it and keep cleaning. The pantry's hazards are quieter than the kitchen's but no less real.

- Composition: a hazard-map image on panel #FBF7EF, the finished pantry drawn calm with a handful of slate #3C5A6B flag marks pinned to the exact spots the cleaning turns them up: a flag on the fine webbing in the flour, on the bulging can lid, on the sticky drip an ant has found, on the soft dark cardboard at the base of a case, on the rust creeping up a metal shelf leg. - Slate #3C5A6B label: "clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix; the safety pass fixes them."
- Point: your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the chewed corner, the fine webbing, the bulging can lid, the fresh sticky drip, the soft dark cardboard, or the rust on a wire shelf, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Then, after the shine, run the five safety questions once directly. Poison, choke, or strangle: treat a bulging, leaking, or hissing can as spoiled and bag it, because no cooking makes it safe, keep allergen flours sealed with their own scoop, and keep hard sweets and whole nuts out of an open bin a small child can reach. Fall, cut, or crush: keep dense rows of cans and heavy glass jars off shelves above eye level, and lift a heavy sack with the legs and no twist. The plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool, keep a child from climbing the shelving, wipe spilled flour and sugar off the floor at once, and keep a floor-stacked case out of the dark doorway.
