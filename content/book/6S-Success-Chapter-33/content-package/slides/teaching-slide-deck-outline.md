### Slide 2 · This chapter, like the two before it, is about the clean

- Step out of the kitchen and into its storeroom. The pantry is where the food waits, and it is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin, because a pantry you cannot see is a pantry that grows a layer of forgotten, out-of-date, half-used food at the back, in the dark, where good intentions go to expire. Clean it and sort it well, and you stop wasting food, you stop buying what you already own, and you gain back a surprising amount of a weekly shop.
- The pantry is also, in one specific way, the room where cleaning matters most for safety, and it is not the way you might think. It is not grease here. It is dust, dates, damp, and pests, and the clean is genuinely an inspection: the chewed bag corner, the fine webbing in the flour, the bulging can lid, the soft dark patch of damp cardboard are all early warnings you find precisely because your hands are on every surface. You already have the method from the door, the nine rules of Shine; the pantry adds one overriding rule, said up front.

### Slide 4 · Five zones, not one cupboard

- A pantry looks like one jumbled job, and faced that way it is the sort of thing you close the door on. Faced as its zones, it is five short, finishable pieces, and the worst of them, the dry goods shelf, is also the one that pays back most. As in the door and the kitchen, you stop seeing the cupboard and see the zones. Each has a clear finish: you do not clean "the pantry"; you clean the dry goods shelf, which is done when every board is dust-free and bone-dry and every open bag is sealed and dated or gone. Start with the dry goods or the cans for the biggest win, because those two hold the most that can spoil and the most that hides.
- Here is the standard the whole cupboard is working toward, the finished state every zone is aiming at. A finished pantry is not one that merely looks neat for an afternoon; it is one you can shop from at a glance.
- The five zones, frozen: "The pantry is five small zones, not one big job: the dry goods shelves, the canned and jarred goods, the baking zone, the snack and lunch zone, and the backstock and bulk. ### Slide 5 · The quick passes, then slow down for Shine

- Before the deep clean, four of the other five passes are quick, so we move through them briskly and then slow right down for Shine, which is where the real work is. Sort each zone before you clean it: bin or give away the open bag you cannot name a meal for, the can two years past its date, the baking kit for the baker you never became. Straighten the survivors by use and by date: staples in one container style, cans by category on risers, one lift-out baking bin, snacks split into a child-height bin and an adult shelf. Those two passes clear the ground so the clean can happen.
- Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short. Safety acts on what the clean turned up: bagging the bulging can, anchoring the shelf a child climbs, moving allergen flours to their own sealed spot. Standardize is the pantry's secret weapon, because this is the room a good standard runs almost by itself: every container dated the day it is opened, every can row labels-out with the oldest at the front, a maximum written on the shelf edge for each backstock line. And Sustain hangs it on what already happens: when you unpack the shopping, new stock loads in from behind the open one; when you carry the recycling past, you face up the can rows. That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

### Slide 6 · How to Shine the pantry: the nine rules, and the dust rule

- You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one of them still holds. The pantry adds one overriding principle and one shift in what you are really doing. Every step-by-step procedure in this chapter is these nine rules applied to a particular zone, with the dry-first layer on top. Learn the method once here and you use it in all twenty room chapters.
- Dust and damp are the whole job in a pantry, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it, so you clean dry before wet without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back. That is the principle the whole clean turns on, and it is the single thing that separates a pantry that stays fresh from one that turns musty.
- The nine rules, frozen, in order: "work top to bottom / dry or clear before wet / back to front and clean to dirty / mist the cloth not the surface / two cloths one to clean and one to dry / colour-code your cloths / match the cleaner to the surface / give it dwell time / clean to inspect and flag rather than fix." The dust rule, frozen: "In a pantry, dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it. So the rule is dry before wet, without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling."

### Slide 7 · The pantry Shine method and the kit

- The principle governs every zone below: the vacuum or the dry brush comes first, the damp cloth second, and the dry cloth last, every single time. Two things sit alongside it. The first is a shift in what you are doing: the pantry is the one room where cleaning is genuinely an inspection for safety, so you are cleaning the shelf but really checking that nothing has got in and nothing is quietly turning. The second is where most pantry problems literally begin: the sticky ring under a honey jar is an invitation to ants, so you clean until the shelf squeaks. The good news is the kit is simple and cheap, because the pantry's enemy is dust rather than grease, so there is no degreaser and no heavy chemistry.
- The kit, by type. Tools: a compact vacuum with a brush and a crevice attachment, the most important tool in the room, a dry hand brush and dustpan, a small detail brush, a bottle and spout brush, and a broom. Cleaners: a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner and mild dish soap. Cloths: a colour-coded microfiber set with one colour flagged for the allergen shelf. And the storage-and-date inputs: airtight dated containers, can risers, and date labels. Types, not brands. Buy for the job.
- The inspection reframe, verbatim: "The pantry is the one room where cleaning is genuinely an inspection for safety, because the things you find as you wipe, a chewed corner, fine webbing in the flour, a bulging can lid, a soft dark patch of damp cardboard, are early warnings you cannot afford to miss. You are cleaning the shelf, yes, but really you are checking that nothing has got in and nothing is quietly turning." The sticky-ring reframe, verbatim: "The sticky ring under a honey jar is not just untidy, it is an invitation. A tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so in the pantry you clean until the shelf squeaks, because clean here is the difference between a store cupboard and a feeding station."

### Slide 8 · Zone by zone: the dry goods shelves, and the cans

- Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Gather the inputs into your caddy first, and remember the rule above every board: dry first, wipe second, dry last, the floor last.
- Zone 1, the dry goods shelves, the grains, pasta, and cereals you cook from every week, where grain dust is the whole story, so it is dry-first from top to bottom, and the biggest grocery-saving win. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush and crevice attachments, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a surface cloth and a dry cloth, a small detail brush, mild dish soap and warm water for scoops, the caddy. Steps: start at the top shelf and the wall behind it, vacuuming first so nothing you knock loose lands on shelves already done, then wipe and dry before moving down; clear and do each board in turn, vacuuming the loose flour and grain dust first, then wiping, then taking the board bone-dry; do the front lip and shelf edge where flour dust drifts forward; wipe the containers, lids, and scoops, washing any scoop that lives in the grain and drying it fully; clear the back corners and the seam with the crevice tool, dry brush first, always; do the floor last.
- Zone 2, the canned and jarred goods, the cans and jars that turn an empty-fridge week into dinner, where the thing you are really hunting is the sticky ring. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a surface cloth and a dry cloth, a small detail brush, the caddy. Steps: clear the boards in rows and vacuum, then wipe and dry so labels do not sit damp and lift; work the sticky rings under the honey, syrup, and sauce jars until the shelf squeaks; clean the jar threads and lids; wipe the can exteriors and rims and set them back label-out with the oldest date at the front; do the front and back of the shelf; do the floor last.

### Slide 9 · Zone by zone: the baking zone, and the snack and lunch zone

- Zone 3, the baking zone, everything you need to start a bake without a shop trip first, where flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it, so this zone is dry-first without exception, and the allergen containers get their own cloth and their own scoop. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, a dry hand brush and dustpan, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set including a dedicated cloth for the allergen shelf, mild dish soap and warm water for scoops, the caddy. Steps: brush the board dry before anything wet, because wetting flour first just smears it into a grey skin you have to scrape; wipe the flour and sugar containers, paying attention to the one-handed lid, and dry the rim; wash the scoop and dry it fully rather than pushing it back damp, where a damp scoop seeds a rock-hard clump; do the lift-out bin of cutters, cases, and tips, checking each is dry so it does not spot with rust; do the allergen containers with their own dedicated cloth and wash their scoops separately; clear the floor as you go, since spilled flour is genuinely slick underfoot.
- Zone 4, the snack and lunch zone, what goes into a lunchbox and what a child is allowed to take without asking, where the mess hides in the bottom corners of the bin and in a lunch bag nobody emptied. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a surface cloth and a dry cloth, a bottle and spout brush, mild dish soap and warm water, the caddy. Steps: do the higher adult shelf first; empty and wipe the low snack bin right into the bottom corners; match the containers to their lids, setting aside any warped or missing; wash the drink bottles and spouts where a sour film hides in the seam, drying them fully upside down and open; do the lunch bags, turned inside out and washed; wipe the bin exterior and shelf edge so no sugary film is left to draw ants.

### Slide 10 · Zone by zone: the backstock and bulk

### Slide 11 · Clean to inspect, and flag; the safety pass fixes

- Cleaning is how you inspect. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the thing you never normally see, a chewed corner, fine webbing in the flour, a bulging can lid, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Each zone's inspect-and-flag list is exactly the set of things your hands are already near: the chewed bag corner and dark droppings, the fine webbing and tiny moths in an opened grain, the container lid that no longer seals, the bowing shelf board, the domed or bulging can, the ant trail following a sugary edge, the soft dark damp cardboard at the base of a case, the rust creeping up a metal shelf leg.
- Then, after the shine, run the five safety questions once directly. Poison, choke, or strangle: treat a bulging, leaking, or hissing can as spoiled and bag it, because no amount of cooking makes it safe; keep allergen flours sealed with their own scoop so nothing cross-contacts; and keep hard sweets, whole nuts, and small round cheeses out of an open bin a small child can reach. Fall, cut, or crush: keep dense rows of cans and heavy glass grain jars off shelves above eye level, and lift a heavy sack with your legs and no twist rather than off the floor with your back. And the plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool rather than a chair or the edge of the bottom shelf, keep a child from climbing the shelving toward a high snack, wipe spilled flour and sugar off the floor at once because they are genuinely slick, and keep a floor-stacked case out of the dark doorway.
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Wiping a floury shelf with a wet cloth, which smears the dust into a grey skin you then have to scrape, and wiping around the containers instead of lifting them off, which leaves the dust band and the sticky ring exactly where the pests are looking. Dry-first, lift everything off, and clean until the shelf squeaks."

### Slide 12 · The calls that matter here

- Cleaning to a method makes the pantry fresh, but a few honest decisions decide whether it stays readable, and the pantry's are mostly about food you feel guilty binning. The first is the bag you opened for one recipe, the half kilo of buckwheat, the coarse polenta, the rice flour from a week of enthusiastic bread: if it is open and you cannot name the specific meal you will cook with it in the next fourteen days, it leaves now; if it is unopened and in date, give it away while it is still good. The second is the best-before you keep arguing with: best-before is about quality, not safety, so anything past it goes into a meal in the next seven days or it goes out.
- The third is the baking kit for the baker you meant to become, the piping set used once, the third tart tin, the specialty flour: keep the tools for what you have baked twice, and let go of the tools for what you baked once and imagined often. And the fourth is the warehouse pack that was not a saving: for each bulk line, write down the largest quantity you have ever genuinely finished before it turned, and make that your maximum, because a cheaper unit price you throw half away at is not a saving.
- The dates reframe, frozen: "A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time, at the back, in the dark. The fix is not more willpower, it is a rule the shelf keeps for you: every container dated the day you open it, every row loaded from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and nothing bought until you can see that you are out." ### Slide 13 · Watch for in the pantry

- The pantry's hazards are quieter than the kitchen's but no less real, and cleaning to inspect will surface most of them, so the safety pass mostly acts on what the clean already flagged. Run the five safety questions once directly. For poison, choke, or strangle: treat a bulging, leaking, or hissing can as spoiled and bag it, because no amount of cooking makes it safe; keep allergen flours sealed with their own scoop so nothing cross-contacts; and keep hard sweets, whole nuts, and small round cheeses out of an open bin a small child can reach.
- For fall, cut, or crush: keep dense rows of cans and heavy glass grain jars off shelves above eye level, where a dropped tin is a broken toe and a dropped jar is broken glass on a hard floor, and lift a heavy sack with your legs and no twist. For the plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool rather than a chair or the edge of the bottom shelf, keep a child from climbing the shelving toward a high snack, wipe spilled flour and sugar off the floor at once because they are genuinely slick, and keep a floor-stacked case out of the dark doorway where it is a shin-height trip.
- The sticky-ring reframe, verbatim: "The sticky ring under a honey jar is not just untidy, it is an invitation. A tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so in the pantry you clean until the shelf squeaks, because clean here is the difference between a store cupboard and a feeding station."
