### Slide 1 (hook)

**Step out of the kitchen and into its storeroom. The pantry is where the food waits, and it is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin. Watch the loop clean it, one zone at a time, dry-first.**

The pantry's enemy is not grease. It is dust, dates, damp, and the things that get in, and here the clean is genuinely an inspection: the chewed bag corner, the fine webbing in the flour, the bulging can lid, the soft dark patch of damp cardboard are all early warnings you find precisely because your hands are on every surface. So this playbook, like the two before it, is mostly about exactly how to clean each zone, and for every one of the five pantry zones it gives you the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, dry-first so flour never turns to paste.

The book line, verbatim: "The pantry is where the food waits, and it is the room that quietly decides how much of your grocery money you throw in the bin, because a pantry you cannot see is a pantry that grows a layer of forgotten, out-of-date, half-used food at the back, in the dark, where good intentions go to expire." Swipe, and watch it change.

### Slide 2

**Anyone can straighten a shelf. A pantry that is genuinely clean is one where every board is dust-free and bone-dry, every open bag is sealed and dated, the sticky ring under the honey jar is gone, and the chewed corner has been flagged. That is the standard, and this chapter is about the clean.**

The pantry is, in one specific way, the room where cleaning matters most for safety, and it is not the way you might think. It is not grease here. It is dust, dates, damp, and pests. You already have the method from the door, the nine rules of Shine, and the habit of inspecting every surface your hand passes over. The pantry keeps all of that and adds one overriding rule, said up front.

The book line, verbatim: "And there is one rule here that overrides almost everything else, so we will say it up front: in a pantry you clean dry before wet, always." And, verbatim: "You do not clean "the pantry"; you clean the dry goods shelf, which is done when every board is dust-free and bone-dry and every open bag is sealed and dated or gone."

### Slide 3

**You do not 6S the whole pantry. You 6S one zone of it, then the next. The pantry is five small zones, not one big job.**

Faced as one job, the pantry is the sort of thing you close the door on. Faced as its zones, it is five short, finishable pieces, and the worst of them, the dry goods shelf, is also the one that pays back most. Start with the dry goods or the cans for the biggest win, because those two hold the most that can spoil and the most that hides.

The five zones, frozen:

### Slide 4

**Four of the six passes are quick. Then you slow right down for Shine, which is where the pantry stops being tidy and becomes clean, dated, and readable.**

Sort each zone before you clean it: bin or give away the open bag you cannot name a meal for, the can two years past its date, the baking kit for the baker you never became. Straighten the survivors by use and by date: staples in one container style, cans by category on risers, one lift-out baking bin, snacks split into a child-height bin and an adult shelf. Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short: act on what the clean turned up, then Standardize, the pantry's secret weapon, dating every container the day it is opened, facing the oldest to the front, writing a maximum on the shelf edge, and hang the upkeep on what already happens, when you unpack the shopping new stock loads in from behind the open one.

That is the frame. The heart of it is Shine, and in the pantry Shine carries one rule that overrides all the others: dry before wet, always.

### Slide 5

**Learn one method once and use it in every zone: the nine rules of Shine, carried from the door. The pantry adds one overriding rule, dry before wet, always. This is the slide to photograph.**

Every step-by-step procedure in this chapter is these nine rules applied to a particular zone, plus the pantry's dry-first layer on top. Learn them once and they carry through all twenty room chapters.

The nine rules, frozen, in order:

- work top to bottom
- dry or clear before wet
- back to front and clean to dirty
- mist the cloth not the surface
- two cloths one to clean and one to dry
- colour-code your cloths
- match the cleaner to the surface
- give it dwell time
- clean to inspect and flag rather than fix

The dust rule, frozen, the pantry Shine principle:

"In a pantry, dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it. So the rule is dry before wet, without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling."

The book line, verbatim: "You know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and they all hold here. The pantry adds one overriding principle and one shift in what you are really doing."

### Slide 6

**The pantry keeps the nine rules and adds one that overrides them all here: dry before wet, always. Vacuum or brush the dust off dry, then wipe, then take it bone-dry. The key tool is a vacuum with a brush attachment, not a bottle of anything.**

That is the principle that governs every zone: the vacuum or the dry brush comes first, the damp cloth second, and the dry cloth last, every single time. Two things sit alongside it. Hunt the sticky ring, because a tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so you clean until the shelf squeaks. And read the clean as an inspection, because you are cleaning the shelf, yes, but really you are checking that nothing has got in and nothing is quietly turning.

The sticky-ring reframe, verbatim: "The sticky ring under a honey jar is not just untidy, it is an invitation. A tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so in the pantry you clean until the shelf squeaks, because clean here is the difference between a store cupboard and a feeding station."

### Slide 7

**Now the core: each zone gets the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect as you go. Zone 1, the dry goods shelves, is the biggest win, and grain dust is the whole story, so it is dry-first from top to bottom.**

Zone 1, the dry goods shelves. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush and crevice attachments, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a surface cloth and a dry cloth, a small detail brush, mild dish soap and warm water for scoops, the caddy. Steps: start at the top shelf and the wall behind it, vacuuming first so nothing you knock loose lands on shelves already done, then wipe and dry before you move down; clear and do each board in turn, vacuuming the loose flour and grain dust first, then wiping, then taking the board bone-dry with a dry cloth; do the front lip and shelf edge where flour dust drifts forward; wipe the containers, lids, and scoops, washing any scoop that lives in the grain and drying it fully; clear the back corners and the seam with the crevice tool, dry brush first, always; do the floor last.

Zone 2, the canned and jarred goods. Steps: clear the boards in rows and vacuum, then wipe and dry so labels do not sit damp and lift; work the sticky rings under the honey, syrup, and sauce jars until the shelf squeaks; clean the jar threads and lids; wipe the can exteriors and rims and set them back label-out with the oldest date at the front; do the front and back of the shelf; do the floor last.

The book line, verbatim: "Here is the core of the chapter."

### Slide 8

**The same method, the bake shelf and the lunchbox. Flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it, so the baking zone is dry-first, without exception, and the allergen containers get their own cloth and their own scoop.**

Zone 3, the baking zone. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, a dry hand brush and dustpan, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set including a dedicated cloth for the allergen shelf, mild dish soap and warm water for scoops, the caddy. Steps: brush the board dry before anything wet, because wetting flour first just smears it into a grey skin you have to scrape; wipe the flour and sugar containers, paying attention to the one-handed lid; wash the scoop and dry it fully rather than pushing it back damp; do the lift-out bin of cutters, cases, and tips; do the allergen containers with their own cloth and wash their dedicated scoops separately; clear the floor as you go, since spilled flour is genuinely slick underfoot.

Zone 4, the snack and lunch zone. Steps: do the higher adult shelf first; empty and wipe the low snack bin right into the bottom corners; match the containers to their lids; wash the drink bottles and spouts where a sour film hides in the seam; do the lunch bags, turned inside out and washed; wipe the bin exterior and shelf edge so no sugary film draws ants.

### Slide 9

**The last zone, the reserve that feeds the working shelves. This is a floor-and-underside zone, and the cardboard is your damp warning system: soft or dark at the bottom of a case is the moisture warning that arrives before the food spoils.**

Zone 5, the backstock and bulk. Inputs: a compact vacuum with the brush attachment, or a broom and dustpan, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a surface cloth and a dry cloth, the caddy. Steps: wipe the shelf edges and their written max-count labels so the numbers stay readable from the doorway; wipe the cases and sacks, taking the dust off the tops, and set them back with the arrival month facing out; check the underside of the bottom case for damp, the spot a concrete floor or an outside wall sweats into; sweep the floor from the back wall out to the door; do the back wall and the doorway so nothing sits at shin height in the dark.

Keep the sticky ring in mind here too, and everywhere: a tacky edge is an invitation, so clean until the shelf squeaks.

### Slide 10

**While your hand is on every surface, your eye is inspecting it. When you find the fine webbing in the flour or the bulging can lid, you flag it and keep cleaning. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it.**

Each zone's inspect-and-flag list is exactly the set of things your hands are already near: the chewed bag corner and small dark droppings, the fine webbing and tiny moths in an opened grain, the container lid that no longer seals, the bowing shelf board, the domed or bulging can that says spoiled rather than old, the ant trail following a sugary edge, the soft dark damp cardboard at the bottom of a case. You note each and keep going.

Then run the five safety questions once. Poison, choke, or strangle: treat a bulging, leaking, or hissing can as spoiled and bag it, because no amount of cooking makes it safe; keep allergen flours sealed with their own scoop; keep hard sweets and whole nuts out of an open bin a small child can reach. Fall, cut, or crush: keep dense rows of cans and heavy glass jars off shelves above eye level, and lift a heavy sack with your legs and no twist. The plain fall: reach the top shelf from a stable step stool, keep a child from climbing the shelving, and wipe spilled flour and sugar off the floor at once because they are genuinely slick.

### Slide 11

**Cleaning to a method makes the pantry fresh, but a few honest decisions decide whether it stays readable, and the pantry's are mostly about food you feel guilty binning. Here are the ones people dodge longest.**

The bag you opened for one recipe: if it is open and you cannot name the specific meal you will cook with it in the next fourteen days, it leaves now; if it is unopened and in date, give it away while it is still good. The best-before you keep arguing with: best-before is about quality, not safety, so anything past it goes into a meal in the next seven days or it goes out. The baking kit for the baker you meant to become: keep the tools for what you have baked twice, and let go of the tools for what you baked once and imagined often. And the warehouse pack that was not a saving: for each bulk line, write down the largest quantity you have ever genuinely finished before it turned, and make that your maximum.

The dates reframe, verbatim: "A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time, at the back, in the dark. The fix is not more willpower, it is a rule the shelf keeps for you: every container dated the day you open it, every row loaded from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and nothing bought until you can see that you are out."
