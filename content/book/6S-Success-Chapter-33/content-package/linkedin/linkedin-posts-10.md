## 1. Dry before wet, always

Part Nine points the finished 6S loop at one real room at a time. After the kitchen, we open the door beside it: the storeroom that feeds it.

Most of us reach for a wet cloth. In a pantry, that is the one thing you must not do first.

"In a pantry, dust and damp are the whole job, and flour turns to paste the instant a wet cloth touches it. So the rule is dry before wet, without exception: vacuum or brush every board clean while it is dry, wipe only afterward, and take the board bone-dry before anything goes back, because a damp board plus grain dust is what starts a pantry smelling."

## 2. Keep the nine rules, add two pantry moves

You already know the nine rules of Shine from the door, and every one still holds in the pantry. It adds one overriding principle and one shift in what you are really doing.

The principle: dry before wet, always. The vacuum or the dry brush comes first, the damp cloth second, the dry cloth last, every time. It is the opposite of the instinct to grab a wet cloth.

The shift: the clean is genuinely an inspection for pests and damp. Your hand passes over every board, so your eye should too.

Match those two to the nine you carried from the door, and the pantry cleans the way every other room does: to a method, one zone at a time.

## 3. Five small zones, not one big job

A pantry looks like one jumbled job you close the door on. Faced as its zones, it is five finishable pieces.

"The pantry is five small zones, not one big job: the dry goods shelves, the canned and jarred goods, the baking zone, the snack and lunch zone, and the backstock and bulk. Do them one at a time and the storeroom that feeds the kitchen comes back to you a zone at a time."

You do not clean the pantry. You clean the dry goods shelf, which is done when every board is dust-free and bone-dry and every open bag is sealed and dated or gone.

## 4. In the pantry, the clean is really an inspection

Every other room, cleaning is cleaning. Here it does a second job, and it is the one that pays.

"The pantry is the one room where cleaning is genuinely an inspection for safety, because the things you find as you wipe, a chewed corner, fine webbing in the flour, a bulging can lid, a soft dark patch of damp cardboard, are early warnings you cannot afford to miss. You are cleaning the shelf, yes, but really you are checking that nothing has got in and nothing is quietly turning."

So you do not stop to fix what you find. You note it, or flag it, and keep cleaning. Shine finds the hazards; the safety pass fixes them.

## 5. Hunt the sticky ring

If you learn one thing about cleaning a pantry, learn to chase the tacky edge you would normally wipe past.

"The sticky ring under a honey jar is not just untidy, it is an invitation. A tacky edge left on a shelf is exactly how ants find the row, so in the pantry you clean until the shelf squeaks, because clean here is the difference between a store cupboard and a feeding station."

So on the canned and jarred shelf, lift every jar off, do not wipe around it, and work the ring under the honey, the syrup, and the sauces until the board actually squeaks under the cloth.

## 6. Date it, and face the oldest to the front

Here is the money move in the whole room, and it is not about willpower.

"A pantry does not go off all at once, it goes off one forgotten item at a time, at the back, in the dark. The fix is not more willpower, it is a rule the shelf keeps for you: every container dated the day you open it, every row loaded from behind so the oldest stands at the front, and nothing bought until you can see that you are out."

Date every open bag the day you open it. Load each can row from behind so the oldest stands at the front. The shelf then tells everyone what is running low without anyone counting.

## 7. Wash the scoop, do not bury it damp

The pantry has no degreaser and no heavy chemistry. Its most important tool is a vacuum, not a bottle. But one small habit saves you a rock-hard clump every time.

"Dry before wet, every time, in this room: flour and grain dust turn to a grey paste the instant a wet cloth touches them, so vacuum or brush first, wipe second, dry last. And wash the scoop that lives in the flour rather than burying it back damp, because a damp scoop seeds a rock-hard clump."

So when you do the baking zone, brush the board dry first, wash the scoop, and dry it fully before it goes back in the flour.

## 8. A pantry you can shop from at a glance

Cleaning to a method gets the room clean, but it helps to name what you are cleaning toward.

"A finished pantry is one you can shop from at a glance: every opened bag sealed in a dated container you can read the level of from the doorway, cans in rows with the label out and the oldest date at the front, one lift-out baking bin, a child-height snack bin holding only pre-approved snacks, and a backstock shelf where every pack carries its arrival month and nothing is stacked in front of anything else."

That is the room that quietly hands back a chunk of your grocery budget: legible enough that you stop buying what you own and stop binning what you forgot.

## 9. Five zones, one move each

The pantry is five small zones. Gather the inputs into a caddy, keep dry before wet above every board, then:

- Dry goods shelf: vacuum the flour and grain dust off dry, then wipe, then take it bone-dry.
- Canned and jarred: work the sticky ring under the honey and syrup until the shelf squeaks, labels out and oldest to the front.
- Baking zone: brush the board dry first, wash the scoop, and dry it fully rather than burying it damp.
- Snack and lunch zone: wash the drink bottles and spouts with a bottle and spout brush, and dry them upside down.
- Backstock and bulk: lift the bottom case and check its underside for damp, because the cardboard is your warning system.
