# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 13, Store by Frequency of Use

Stand at the door one more time. Every keeper from the last chapter has a home now. The keys hang on a hook, the leash beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook over, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a small tray below. Nothing is loose. Every thing has a place. And every place is treated exactly the same. That last part is the whole of this chapter, because you grab the keys a dozen times a day and reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, yet both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not.

Welcome to the second step of Straighten. Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds; none of Chapter 12 comes undone here. But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. Giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts, just as deciding what to keep and deciding where it lives were two separate acts a chapter ago. So the work turns again, from assigning homes to placing them well, and it does so quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

Here is the idea the rest of the chapter rests on. Not all storage is equal. A small band of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you at hand height, and a great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf or down at the back of the low cupboard. The pleasant part is small, and that is the fact that changes everything. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly. Think of it as real estate: the spot at the front of the shelf, at the height of your hand, is the corner shop on the busy street, and the deep back of the cabinet is the storage unit across town. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each.

That scarce, easy band gets a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it. Call it the prime zone, the small stretch of space from about your hips to your shoulders, within an arm's length, at the front of whatever holds it, measured from where you actually stand when you use the thing. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it. So what earns a place in it? Not size, not category, not how nice a thing looks lined up with its cousins. Only one thing, and it is worth committing to memory: the best spot goes to the thing you reach for most. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their tidiness. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins.

The placing itself is a rough sort, not a spreadsheet. The everyday things, the coffee and the pans and the coat you actually wear, go in the prime zone, front and center. The regular but not constant things, the good scissors and the mixing bowl, go near and still handy, one small reach away. The rare and the seasonal, the roasting pan and the holiday box and the good umbrella in a dry month, go a step further off, up high or down low or at the back, where the cost of the occasional stretch barely registers. The more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. And if you guess wrong, the room tells you: a busy thing stuck in a far spot nags within a day or two, and sliding it one shelf over costs nothing. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing.

One honest note before you start, said without any blame in it, because none is owed. In most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it, the waffle iron at the front of the cabinet, the wedding china at eye level. Nobody placed them there as a character flaw. They landed in the good spot the day they arrived and simply never moved, the way water sits wherever the ground is lowest. Finding one there is not a verdict on you; it is just information, and useful information, because it tells you exactly which expensive spot is about to come free.

Read Chapter 13 free in the online book, then take the smallest possible first step. Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day, find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster. Then, in Chapter 14, every home you just placed gets made clear at a glance, so anyone can find and return a thing without having to ask.
