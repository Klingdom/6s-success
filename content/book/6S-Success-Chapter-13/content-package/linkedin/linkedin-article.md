# Your Best Storage Is Scarce. Stop Spending It on Things You Touch Twice a Year.

Every keeper at the door has a home now. The keys hang on a hook, the leash beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook over, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a tray below. Nothing is loose. Every thing has a place. And every place is treated exactly the same.

That last part is the whole of this chapter. Look at what your hand actually does at that door. You grab the keys a dozen times a day. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, and only when the sky turns. Yet both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, in the same good light, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not. One is doing a dozen jobs a day from a first-rate spot; the other is sitting in that same first-rate spot to do almost nothing.

Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds. But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. This chapter fixes that, quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

## Not all storage is equal

Here is the idea the rest of this rests on, because most of us have never thought of our own shelves this way.

In any room, a small amount of storage is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you, at hand height, no bending and no stretching. A great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf, down at the back of the low cupboard, behind two other things. The pleasant part is small. That is the fact that changes everything. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly.

Think of it as real estate. The spot at the front of the shelf, at the height of your hand, is the corner shop on the busy street. It is expensive, and it should be, because everything placed there gets used without effort. The high shelf and the deep back of the cabinet are the storage unit across town: cheap, roomy, and a genuine bother to visit. Neither is good or bad on its own. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each.

You can feel this in a single cabinet. Picture the mug you drink from every morning parked at the back of a high shelf, and a novelty teapot you have poured from exactly once sitting front and center at eye level. Swap the two, and the cabinet changes character overnight, not because it holds any less but because the tenant in the expensive spot finally matches the traffic through it.

> Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off.

Read that as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of good, easy reach, and far more of the awkward kind. Frequency tells you how to allocate it.

## The prime zone

Let me give the good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it.

There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it rather than the back. Reach into that band and you take a thing without bending, stretching, climbing, or moving anything aside. Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it.

Two things make it easy to misjudge. First, it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from a tidy category on a chart. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker; the prime zone for your reading glasses is at the chair where you read. Second, it is small on purpose. If you try to make everything prime, nothing is, because the band has room for only so much, and stuffing it full just buries the everyday things behind the rare ones again. Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work.

So what earns a place in that scarce band? Not size, not category, not how nice a thing looks lined up with its cousins. Only one thing earns it.

> The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.

That is the entire rule, and it quietly settles a hundred small arguments about where things go. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins.

## Match the reach to the use

Once you can see the prime zone, placing things becomes a matter of sorting, and the sort is refreshingly rough. You do not need a spreadsheet. You need three loose piles, and most things fall into one at a glance.

The everyday things · the coffee, the pans you cook with, the coat you actually wear. These go in the prime zone, front and center, nothing to move aside. They earn the expensive spot because they collect on it many times over, every day.

The regular but not constant things · the good scissors, the mixing bowl, the second jacket, reached for across a week rather than a day. These go near and still handy, a shelf up or over, one small reach away.

The rare and the seasonal · the roasting pan, the holiday box, the spare duvet, the good umbrella in a dry month. These can go a step further off with no loss at all: up high, down low, at the back, in another room. You pay the cost of reaching them only once in a long while, so it barely registers.

That is the whole method, and it fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off.

Do not agonize over the borderline cases. Put them near and watch. A busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two; a rare thing hogging a prime spot just sits there looking increasingly silly. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs.

## Audit your best spots first

Before you place anything, look at what is already sitting in your best spots, because in most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it. The waffle iron at the front of the cabinet. The wedding china at eye level. The gadget you bought with real hope and have opened twice.

> Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.

Say that without any blame in it, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot the day it arrived and simply never moved, the way water sits wherever the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is just information, and useful information, because it tells you exactly which expensive spot is about to come free.

The other half of the audit is a small correction to how we usually think. We tend to store by where a thing seems to belong, its proper category, its natural shelf with its own kind. But belonging on a list is not the same as being useful to your hand. The point of use beats the tidy category every time, because you live at the point of use and only visit the category.

> Store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list.

## Placing the keepers by frequency

Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks and the mail sits in its tray. The homes are good; now we place them by how often the hand goes to each.

Start with the keys, because nothing here is touched more. They take THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height, the first thing your hand meets on the way in. The leash goes out just as often, morning and evening, so it stays right up front beside the keys. The reusable bag comes next, but its rhythm is different: a few times a week, not a few times a day, so it moves near rather than prime, a hook just to the side. The umbrella has to move the furthest. It works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the many dry ones, so it steps a little off the main run, into a stand in the corner or onto a lower hook. Last is tomorrow's mail, brief but constant, which wants to sit right in the exit path so it rides out with you.

The entryway is small and full of mostly frequent things, so the tiering here is gentle; the differences are inches. The principle earns its keep in fuller rooms. In the kitchen cabinet, the mugs and plates you use every morning come to the front at eye-to-waist height, and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top. In the coat closet, the jackets you actually wear this week hang at the front and the out-of-season coats get slid to the ends. Same principle, more room to breathe, and the same quiet payoff: the things you reach for most became the easiest to reach.

## Nothing left, and the room sped up again

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves once more, and again nothing had to leave the house for it to move. The room holds precisely what it held this morning. All that changed is that the homes slid to match how often each thing is used, and somehow the room got easier.

It got easier because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most. Before, the keys and the umbrella cost you the same small effort, and the keys made you pay it a dozen times a day. Now the keys sit exactly where your hand already goes, so that toll drops to nearly nothing, twelve times over, while the umbrella pays its slightly higher toll only on the rare day it rains. Multiply that across a kitchen, a closet, a desk, and the whole room quietly speeds up.

This is the second step of Straighten. Chapter 12's position is now the faint ghost just behind the live needle, the Sort-complete milestone sits further back still, and the goal on the calm side is still a fair way off, because two more Straighten chapters and four more S's remain.

> A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule, quietly, in seconds you never notice losing anymore.

You did not add storage or buy a system. You looked at how often your hand actually moves and let that spend your scarce, valuable reach on your behalf. Every time you grab the keys without thinking, you collect a little of what you just saved, and you collect it again tomorrow, and the day after.
