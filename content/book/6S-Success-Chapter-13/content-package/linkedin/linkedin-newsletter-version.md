# Spend Your Best Space on Your Busiest Things, and the Room Speeds Up

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Last chapter, every keeper at the door got a home. The keys hang on a hook, the leash beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook over, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a tray below. Nothing is loose. Every thing has a place. And every place is treated exactly the same.

That last part is the whole of this chapter. You grab the keys a dozen times a day. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, and only when the sky turns. Yet both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not.

Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds. But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. This chapter fixes that, quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

## Not all storage is equal

Most of us have never thought of our own shelves this way. In any room, a small amount of storage is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you at hand height. A great deal of it is a nuisance, up high or at the back. The pleasant part is small, and that is the fact that changes everything. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly.

Think of it as real estate. The front of the shelf at hand height is the corner shop on the busy street, expensive and worth it. The high shelf and the deep back are the storage unit across town, cheap and a bother to visit. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each.

**Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off.**

Read that as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of good, easy reach, and far more of the awkward kind. Frequency tells you how to allocate it.

## The prime zone

Let me give the good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it. There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use: roughly from your hips to your shoulders, within an arm's length, at the front rather than the back. Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it.

It is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from a chart. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker; for your reading glasses, at the chair where you read. And it is small on purpose. If you try to make everything prime, nothing is. Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work.

So what earns a place in that scarce band?

**The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.**

That is the entire rule. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins.

## Match the reach to the use

Placing things is refreshingly rough. You need three loose piles, and most things fall into one at a glance.

- Everyday things · the coffee, the pans, the coat you actually wear. These go in the prime zone, front and center.
- Regular but not constant · the good scissors, the mixing bowl, the second jacket. These go near and still handy, one small reach away.
- Rare and seasonal · the roasting pan, the holiday box, the good umbrella in a dry month. These step a little further off, up high, at the back, in another room.

The more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Do not agonize over the borderline cases. Put them near and watch. A busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two; a rare thing hogging a prime spot just sits there looking silly. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing until each thing lands where it belongs.

## Audit your best spots first

In most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it. The waffle iron at the front. The wedding china at eye level. The gadget opened twice.

**Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.**

Say that without any blame, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed there and never moved, the way water sits where the ground is lowest. Finding it is not a verdict on you; it is just useful information about which expensive spot is about to come free.

The other half of the audit is a small correction. We tend to store by where a thing seems to belong, its proper category. But belonging on a list is not the same as being useful to your hand. **Store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list.** The point of use beats the tidy category every time, because you live at the point of use and only visit the category.

## Placing the keepers by frequency

Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks. The homes are good; now we place them by how often the hand goes to each.

- Keys · THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height, the first thing your hand meets.
- Leash · prime too, right beside the keys, out and back with the dog morning and evening.
- Reusable bag · near rather than prime, a hook just to the side, because you take it a few times a week.
- Umbrella · a step further off, into a stand in the corner or a lower hook, because it works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the dry ones.
- Tomorrow's mail · right in the exit path, so it rides out with you.

The entryway is small and mostly frequent, so the tiering is gentle, the differences are inches. The principle earns its keep in fuller rooms. In the kitchen cabinet, the daily mugs come to the front at eye-to-waist height and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top. In the coat closet, this week's jackets hang at the front and the out-of-season coats get slid to the ends. Same principle, more room to breathe.

## Nothing left, and the room still got easier

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves once more, and again nothing had to leave the house for it to move. The room holds precisely what it held this morning. It got easier because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most: the keys now sit exactly where your hand already goes, so that toll drops to nearly nothing twelve times over, while the umbrella pays its slightly higher toll only on the rare rainy day. This is the second step of Straighten, and the goal is still ahead, because two more Straighten chapters and four more S's remain.

**A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule, quietly, in seconds you never notice losing anymore.**

## The assignment

Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day. Find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster.
