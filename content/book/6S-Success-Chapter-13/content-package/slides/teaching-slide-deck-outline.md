### Slide 2 · Every home gets the same prime reach

- Stand at the door one more time. Every keeper from the last chapter has a home now. Nothing is loose, every thing has a place, and every place is treated exactly the same.
- Look at what your hand actually does. You grab the keys a dozen times a day, every coming and going. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, and only when the sky turns. Yet both hang at the same height, the same easy arm's length, in the same good light.
- One of them is doing a dozen jobs a day from a first-rate spot; the other is sitting in that same first-rate spot to do almost nothing.

### Slide 3 · A good home can be in the wrong place

- The last chapter did one load-bearing thing: it gave every keeper a single clear home. That was real progress, and none of it comes undone here. The keys still have their hook. We are not reopening any of those decisions.
- But giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts. A home can pass every test we set for it, close, open, obvious, and still sit in a spot that is better than the thing deserves or worse than the thing needs.
- So the work turns again, from assigning homes to placing them well, by a question we have not asked yet: how often does your hand actually go to this thing? Keep the fences honest. We are not reopening Sort, not re-deciding whether a thing gets a home, not adding a label or an outline, and not mapping a whole room. Those are the next two chapters.

### Slide 4 · Prime space is expensive

- Here is the idea the rest of the chapter rests on. Not all storage is equal. A small amount of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you at hand height, no bending and no stretching. A great deal of it is a nuisance, up high or at the back. The pleasant part is small.
- The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly.
- Read the principle as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of the good, easy reach. Frequency tells you how to allocate it: the things your hand wants many times a day belong in the expensive spots; the things you want twice a year can sit in the cheap seats.

### Slide 5 · Think of it as real estate

- The spot at the front of the shelf, at the height of your hand, is the corner shop on the busy street. It is expensive, and it should be, because everything placed there gets used without effort.
- The high shelf and the deep back of the cabinet are the storage unit across town: cheap, roomy, and a genuine bother to visit. Neither is good or bad on its own. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each.
- Picture the mug you drink from every morning parked at the back of a high shelf, and a novelty teapot you have poured from once sitting front and center at eye level. Swap the two and the cabinet changes character overnight, not because it holds any less, but because the tenant in the expensive spot finally matches the traffic through it.

### Slide 6 · The Prime Zone

- Let me give the good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it. There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it rather than the back.
- Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it. Two things make it easy to misjudge: it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from a tidy category on a chart; and it is small on purpose, because if you try to make everything prime, nothing is.
- Definition, verbatim: "Storing by frequency of use means placing each home by how often you reach for it: daily things in the prime, easy-reach space, weekly things near, and rare or seasonal things a step further off. The more you use it, the closer and easier it goes."

### Slide 7 · The rule

- So what earns a place in that scarce band? Not size, not category, not how nice a thing looks lined up with its cousins. Only one thing earns it.
- The rule, verbatim: "The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most."
- That is the entire rule, and it quietly settles a hundred small arguments about where things go. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness or which shelf they came in on. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins. The needle we care about is your own reach, measured across an ordinary week.

### Slide 8 · Match the reach to the use

- Placing things becomes a matter of sorting, and the sort is refreshingly rough. You need three loose piles, and most things fall into one at a glance. Everyday things, the coffee and the pans and the coat you actually wear, go in the prime zone, front and center. Regular but not constant things, the good scissors, the mixing bowl, go near and still handy, one small reach away. Rare and seasonal things, the roasting pan, the holiday box, the good umbrella in a dry month, go a step further off.
- That is the whole method, and it fits in a line, verbatim: "the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off."
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Storing by category instead of by use. Putting all the baking gear together on one high shelf looks tidy, but if you reach for the measuring cup every day and the cake tin once a year, they should not share a hard-to-reach spot. Sort the shelf by frequency, not by kind."

### Slide 9 · Not a test you can fail

- Do not agonize over the borderline cases. If a thing sits somewhere between weekly and rare, put it near and watch what happens. The items you place wrong tend to announce themselves quickly.
- A busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two, and a rare thing hogging a prime spot just sits there looking increasingly silly. The room tells you where you guessed wrong, and sliding a thing one shelf over to fix it costs nothing.
- The reframe, verbatim: "Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs."

### Slide 10 · Audit your prime space

- Before you place anything, look at what is already sitting in your best spots, because in most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it: the waffle iron at the front, the wedding china at eye level, the gadget you opened twice. They moved into the good real estate early and have been charging you a small toll ever since.
- Say it without blame, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot the day it arrived and simply never moved, the way water sits wherever the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is useful information, because it tells you which expensive spot is about to come free.
- 6S Tip, verbatim: "Store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list. Coffee lives by the coffee maker, not across the kitchen with the other pantry goods, because the prime spot is measured from your hand, not from a category."

### Slide 11 · Placing the keepers by frequency

- Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks and the mail sits in its tray. The keys are touched more than anything, so they take THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height. The leash goes out just as often, so it stays right up front beside them. The reusable bag comes next, but you take it a few times a week, not a few times a day, so it moves near, a hook just to the side. The umbrella has to move the furthest, into a stand in the corner, close enough to grab when the sky turns and out of the way the rest of the time. Tomorrow's mail rides the exit path, in your hand for two seconds on the way out.
- The entryway is small and mostly frequent, so the tiering here is gentle; the differences are inches. Scale it up: in the kitchen cabinet the daily mugs come to the front at eye-to-waist height and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top. In the coat closet the jackets you wear this week hang at the front and the out-of-season coats get slid to the ends.
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day. Find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster."

### Slide 13 · A room that speeds up (close + soft CTA)

- Look at the door one last time. The keys and the leash sit right at hand, first thing in and first thing out. The bag hangs a step to the side. The umbrella waits in its corner for a rainy day. The mail rides the exit path. Nothing left, nothing arrived, and yet the whole entryway is quicker to use than it was an hour ago, because your busiest things now hold your best spots and your rarest things stepped politely aside.
- You did not add storage or buy a system. You looked at how often your hand actually moves and let that spend your scarce, valuable reach on your behalf. The placement is right; it is just still silent, obvious only to the person who set it up. Making each home announce itself is where we go next.
- One Idea to Keep, verbatim: "Match the reach to the use. The things your hand wants a dozen times a day should be the easiest to reach; the things you want once a year can afford to be a stretch. Spend your best space on your busiest things, and the room quietly speeds up."
