# Visual Storyboard

## Store by Frequency of Use

*6S Success: Home Edition, Chapter 13. A visual storyboard tying the teaching deck and the LinkedIn carousel to the book's design system.*

## Design system reference

Pulled directly from chapter_13_final.html :root variables. Use these exactly so the slides read as part of the book.

- Paper #F7F2E9: slide and card backgrounds
- Panel #FBF7EF: card fill, definition and idea boxes
- Ink #2B2622: primary text, object outlines
- Soft #6A625A: captions, secondary labels, the far zones (high, low, deep back)
- Terracotta #BC4B2A: the chapter kicker
- Honey #DDA63A: the near tier, the middle of the meter
- Slate #3C5A6B: chapter labels, the GOAL crosshair and PURPOSE flag, the milestone ticks
- Green calm-dot #6E8B5B: the prime zone, the easy reach, the daily tier, the calm side of the meter
- Friction spark #CB4B36: the friction side of the meter
- Ghost needle #8A8073: the faint dashed ghost of Chapter 12's position, and the "same reach" guide note
- Rules and hairlines #E2D8C4 and #D9CDB8: dividers, dashed lines
- Display font Fraunces: titles, pull quotes, definition and idea boxes, column headers
- Body font Newsreader: worked pass, captions, running text
- Label font Inter: uppercase, wide letter-spacing, for kickers, tags, axis labels

**The recurring motif.** The hero device of the chapter is The Prime Zone: the small band of genuinely easy reach, roughly from your hips to your shoulders, within an arm's length, at the front, and measured from where you actually use the thing. It is scarce and it is the most valuable storage you own. Green #6E8B5B marks the prime zone, the easy reach, and the daily tier that earns it; honey #DDA63A marks the near tier and the middle of the meter; soft #6A625A and the ghost tone #8A8073 carry the far zones, high, low, and deep at the back, which are cheaper to use rather than bad. Slate #3C5A6B carries the chapter labels, the GOAL crosshair, and the milestone ticks. Friction spark #CB4B36 is reserved for the friction side of the meter. Terracotta is spent only on the chapter kicker. The ghost needle #8A8073 is Chapter 12's position on the meter, now the dashed ghost.

**Two fences to hold visually.** First, this is placing by frequency, not re-assigning homes: every keeper already has a home from Chapter 12, so never redraw the giving-a-home pass or reopen Sort; this chapter only decides WHERE each home sits by how often the hand goes to it. Second, do not add labels, outlines, or colour-coding to the homes (that is Chapter 14, a teaser only), and do not map or plan a whole room's storage at once (that is Chapter 15, a teaser only). Keep the focus on matching the reach to the use.

## Beat 1 · Every keeper home, every home the same reach

The chapter and both decks open the same way: the settled door where every keeper has a home, yet every home gets the same prime reach.

- Composition: full-bleed, warm daylight. The entryway from the last chapter, settled now. Four identical hooks in a tidy row by the door hold the keys, the dog's leash, the reusable bag, and the folding umbrella, each given the same easy arm's-height spot. A small tray below holds tomorrow's mail. Everything is placed, nothing loose.
- Palette: paper #F7F2E9 ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, a faint ghost #8A8073 note by the keys and umbrella, soft #6A625A on the mail label and the floating question.
- Motif: the homes are done and good, but they are all treated the same, when the things in them are used wildly differently. A faint note points to the keys and umbrella together: "same hook · same height · same reach." A quiet question floats above the row: "which of these do you actually reach for most?"
- Caption line, from the book: "Every keeper has a home now, and every home gets the same prime reach. But the keys go out a dozen times a day and the umbrella twice a month. The question is no longer where does this live, but which of these has earned the best spot?"

## Beat 2 · The same reach for very different work

The gap made plain: identical spots, wildly different traffic.

- Composition: the four keepers hung in a row at one identical height, the keys and the umbrella side by side. A green #6E8B5B note by the keys reads "used a dozen times a day"; a honey #DDA63A note by the umbrella reads "used twice a month." Both hang at the same arm's length in the same good light.
- Palette: paper #F7F2E9 ground, green on the busy thing, honey on the rare thing, ghost #8A8073 on the "same reach" guide. No spark; nothing is a bad home here, only a mismatched one.
- Point: one of them is doing a dozen jobs a day from a first-rate spot; the other is sitting in that same first-rate spot to do almost nothing. The keys and the umbrella are paying the same rent for very different work.
- The frozen line may sit beneath, in Fraunces medium #2B2622: "But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place."

## Beat 3 · From assigning homes to placing them well

The turn the chapter makes, drawn as a continuation, not a redo.

- Composition: a quiet two-panel on paper ground. Left panel, labelled in Inter "ASSIGNED," shows the settled hooks from Chapter 12, greyed and finished, each keeper on its hook. Right panel, labelled "PLACED BY USE," shows the same keepers with soft arrows re-sorting to tiered spots.
- A slate #3C5A6B rule between the panels: "two separate acts, and only the first is done." Footer, Newsreader italic soft #6A625A: "the umbrella has a fine home; it just happens to be occupying a reach the keys would put to better use."
- Palette: greyed ink on the finished left panel, live ink on the right, slate on the rule. Green and honey arrive only as tier hints on the right.
- Point: giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts. Name the fence in the frame: not reopening Sort, not re-deciding whether a thing gets a home, not adding a label or an outline, not mapping a whole room. Today matches each home to how often the hand goes to it.

## Beat 4 · Prime space is expensive

The reframe the rest of the chapter rests on, given the pull-quote treatment.

- Composition: the pull-quote card. Panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the principle in Fraunces medium #2B2622: "Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off."
- Beneath it, a single green calm-dot #6E8B5B and a small Inter tag: "read it as a budget, not a rule of neatness."
- Palette: green marks the scarce good reach; otherwise slate does the work. No sparks.
- Point: not all storage is equal. A small amount is genuinely pleasant to use, and a great deal is a nuisance. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and can be spent well or badly. You have a fixed amount of good, easy reach, and frequency tells you how to allocate it.

## Beat 6 · The Prime Zone

The chapter's hero device and the centerpiece of both decks. Rebuild it faithfully from the HTML SVG.

- Composition: a standing figure beside a tall shelf or cabinet in cross-section. A green #6E8B5B band spans the shelf from hip height to shoulder height, at the front, labelled "THE PRIME ZONE · easy reach." Above it, a lighter zone reads "a stretch or a step stool"; below it, "a bend"; behind the front row, a deep zone in soft #6A625A and ghost #8A8073 reads "deep at the back · hard to reach." A small arm's-length arc shows the reach measured from where the person stands.
- Along the bottom runs the rule in slate #3C5A6B: "the best spot goes to the thing you reach for most."
- Palette: green on the prime band, soft and ghost tones on the far zones, slate on the rule. Paper ground.
- Caption, verbatim: "The prime zone is the small green band you can use without bending, stretching, or reaching past anything, measured from where you actually stand. It is the best storage you own, and there is never much of it, so the rule decides who gets in."
- Fence: the prime zone is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from a tidy category, and it is small on purpose. Do not draw it large or full; scarcity is the point.

## Beat 7 · The rule

The single rule that settles who gets the best spot, given the pull-quote treatment.

- Composition: the pull-quote card. Panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the rule in Fraunces medium #2B2622: "The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most."
- Beneath it, a single green calm-dot #6E8B5B and a small Inter tag: "when two things want the good spot, the busier hand wins."
- Palette: green marks the winning reach; otherwise slate does the work. No sparks.
- Point: not size, not category, not tidiness. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins. This is the old Straighten idea, plain and home-sized: store a thing by how often you use it, and store it where you use it.

## Beat 8 · Match the reach to the use

The three tiers mapped to a real, familiar space. Rebuild from the HTML SVG.

- Composition: a kitchen cabinet in cross-section, three shelves. The middle shelf, pulled to the front at eye-to-waist height, is a green #6E8B5B prime band holding the daily mugs and plates, labelled "DAILY · PRIME." The shelf just above or to the side, in honey #DDA63A, holds the weekly mixing bowl and good glasses, labelled "WEEKLY · NEAR." The top shelf, up high in soft #6A625A, holds the once-a-year roasting pan, labelled "RARE · FAR." Down the side, three tiers listed: daily to prime, weekly to near, rare to far.
- Beneath, the Common Mistake in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Storing by category instead of by use. Putting all the baking gear together on one high shelf looks tidy, but if you reach for the measuring cup every day and the cake tin once a year, they should not share a hard-to-reach spot. Sort the shelf by frequency, not by kind."
- Palette: green on the daily prime band, honey on the weekly near shelf, soft on the rare far shelf, slate on the callout. Paper ground.
- Caption, from the book: "The same cabinet, sorted by use rather than by kind. The daily mugs sit at the front at hand height; the weekly bowl is one small reach up; the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top, where the rare cost of reaching it barely counts."
- The frozen line may run along the base in Newsreader italic: "the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off."

## Beat 9 · Not a test you can fail

The permission beat: the room edits your first draft for you.

- Composition: a single shelf on paper ground with one item mid-slide, a green #6E8B5B arrow moving a daily mug from a far corner into the front prime band, and a soft #6A625A arrow sliding a rare platter one shelf up. A gentle Inter tag: "the room edits your first draft for you."
- The frozen line may sit beneath, in Fraunces medium #2B2622: "Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs."
- Palette: green on the correcting move, soft on the far shelf, no verdict colours beyond the calm green. The frame is patient, never scolding.
- Point: a busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two, and a rare thing hogging a prime spot just looks silly. The room tells you where you guessed wrong, and sliding a thing one shelf over costs nothing.

## Beat 10 · Audit your prime space

The audit reframe, the anti-shame center of the chapter, given the pull-quote treatment.

- Composition: the pull-quote card. Panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the reframe in Fraunces medium #2B2622: "Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn."
- Beneath, the waffle iron drawn at the front of a cabinet with a small honey #DDA63A "premium rent" tag, and the 6S Tip in a slate-bordered callout with a slate #3C5A6B dot, verbatim: "Store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list. Coffee lives by the coffee maker, not across the kitchen with the other pantry goods, because the prime spot is measured from your hand, not from a category."
- Palette: honey on the premium-rent tag, slate on the tip callout. No verdict colours; this beat should feel like information, not blame.
- Point: nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot and never moved, the way water sits where the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is useful information, because it tells you which expensive spot is about to come free.
