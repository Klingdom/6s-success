# Diagram Ideas: Chapter 13

**"Store by Frequency of Use"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Four · Straighten*

Five diagrams that carry the chapter's logic. All use the warm book palette and the two chapter
fonts (Fraunces display, Newsreader body, a clean sans for labels). These are working specs, not
finished art. They stay consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_13_final.html`:
the settled entryway with all four keepers hung on identical hooks at the same prime height under
one quiet question, the prime zone drawn as a green easy-reach band on a shelf cross-section with
the far zones marked harder, the kitchen cabinet sorted so daily mugs sit front while the
once-a-year roasting pan goes up high, and the friction meter stepped one notch past Chapter 12
into the second step of Straighten.

Palette tokens (from the chapter HTML :root): paper `#F7F2E9`, panel `#FBF7EF`, ink `#2B2622`,
soft `#6A625A`, terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate `#3C5A6B`, calm green `#6E8B5B`,
friction spark `#CB4B36`, rule `#E2D8C4` / `#D9CDB8`, wood fill `#E7C58B`.

Scope fence: this chapter is the second S's second chapter. Chapter 12 already gave every keeper
one clear home; those homes are settled and stay. Chapter 13 does one narrow thing: it places
each home by how often your hand goes to it, so the busiest things get the easiest reach and the
rare things step a little further off. It does NOT re-assign homes or reopen that decision (that
was Chapter 12), it does NOT add a label, an outline, or a color-coded cue to any home (Chapter
14), and it does NOT map how a whole room's homes fit together (Chapter 15). It does not reopen
Sort. Separator discipline: labels use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 13 · STRAIGHTEN`, never a
floating hyphen or a floating dot between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen:
once-a-year, eye-to-waist, arm's-reach, easy-reach, Sort-complete, anti-shame. Anti-shame
throughout: a busy thing stuck in a far spot is a placement to slide over, not a verdict on the
reader; the waffle iron in the good spot is information, not a character flaw. Keep it calm and
non-coercive, no guilt cues, no pressure framing, and no force or violence framing anywhere.

## 1. The Same Prime Reach for Everything (the opener)

**Purpose:** Signal the chapter's starting point in one image. Every keeper now has a home, and
that is real progress, but all four hang on identical hooks at the same easy height, as though
the keys you grab a dozen times a day and the umbrella you reach for twice a month needed the
same good spot. Mirrors Figure 1 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** The settled entryway from Chapter 12. Four identical hooks in a tidy row hold
the keys, the dog's leash, the reusable bag, and the folding umbrella, each at the same arm's
height, with tomorrow's mail in a small tray below. A faint how-often note sits under each
keeper, and a quiet question floats over the row.

**Rough layout:**
- Warm daylight wash, a low floor line, the wall in gentle slate, the surface calm and settled.
- A wall rail across the frame in soft wood fill `#E7C58B`, rounded corners, a small terracotta
  panel label `THE ENTRYWAY, HOMES ASSIGNED`.
- Four identical hooks evenly spaced, each holding one keeper drawn calmly and well kept: the
  keys, the coiled leash, the reusable bag, the folding umbrella. Below the row, a small tray
  holds tomorrow's mail.
- A single dashed guide line in soft gray runs across all four hooks with a small sans note
  `same hook · same height · same reach`, so the eye reads the equal treatment at once.
- Under each keeper, a faint Newsreader italic how-often note in muted `#6A625A`: under the keys
  `a dozen times a day`, under the leash `twice a day`, under the bag `a few times a week`, under
  the umbrella `twice a month`.
- Above the row, a quiet Fraunces italic question in soft gray: `which of these do you actually
  reach for most?`
- Caption idea: "Every keeper has a home now, and every home gets the same prime reach. But the
  keys go out a dozen times a day and the umbrella twice a month. The question is no longer where
  does this live, but which of these has earned the best spot?"

**Note for the illustrator:** the homes here are good, not a mess, so draw the row as tidy and
settled, not as clutter. The gentle tension is that four very different rhythms are being given
one identical spot. Do not add any label, outline, or color cue to a hook, and do not yet move
or re-rank anything; that placement is the body of the chapter. The how-often notes and the one
floating question do all the work. Keep it warm and matter-of-fact, no scolding about the equal
treatment, just a quiet noticing.

## 2. The Prime Zone (the hero device)

**Purpose:** Make the prime zone concrete and spatial, and show that it is small, at the front,
and measured from the body. The easy-reach band is the best storage you own, and there is never
much of it, so the rule decides who gets in. The far zones are not bad, only cheaper. Mirrors
Figure 2 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A simple standing figure beside a tall shelf drawn in cross-section. A green
band spans the front of the shelf from about hip height to shoulder height, labeled the prime
zone. Above, below, and behind it, three far zones are marked as harder to reach. An arc shows
the reach measured from where the person stands, and the rule runs along the bottom.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: small sans caps label, soft gray, `THE PRIME ZONE`.
- A standing figure on the left in soft ink line, an arm reaching forward. A tall shelf on the
  right in wood fill `#E7C58B`, drawn in cross-section so its depth is visible.
- The prime band: a calm green `#6E8B5B` block at the front of the shelf, spanning hip to
  shoulder height, with a green calm-dot and a sans label `THE PRIME ZONE · EASY REACH`, and a
  small helper line `hips to shoulders · arm's reach · front`.
- The far zones drawn plainer and cooler, each with a small slate tag, none in green: up top
  `up high · a stretch or a step stool`; near the floor `down low · a bend`; the deep part behind
  the front row `deep at the back · hard to see, hard to reach`.
- Dashed slate guides mark the shoulder line and the hip line; a light arc from the figure's
  shoulder shows the arm's-length reach, tagged `measured from where you stand`.
- Along the bottom, a Fraunces slate line carrying the rule: "The best spot goes to the thing you
  reach for most."
- Caption idea: "The prime zone is the small green band you can use without bending, stretching,
  or reaching past anything, measured from where you actually stand. It is the best storage you
  own, and there is never much of it, so the rule decides who gets in."

**Note for the illustrator:** keep the green band small on purpose, clearly less than half the
shelf, so scarcity reads at a glance. The far zones are cheaper, not bad, so draw them calm and
neutral, never as danger or failure, and never with a friction-spark. The reach arc is the point
of the frame, so make it obviously measured from the standing body, not from the shelf. No labels
or color-coded cues get added to the contents; the band and the tags carry the whole idea.

## 3. Match the Reach to the Use (the worked example)

**Purpose:** Show the daily / weekly / rare tiering in a real, familiar space, and prove that
placing by frequency reads instantly. The daily things take the prime band, the weekly things sit
near, and the rare things step a little further off. Mirrors Figure 3 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A kitchen cabinet in cross-section, three shelves, sorted by how often each
thing is used. The middle shelf, pulled to the front at eye-to-waist height, is the green prime
band holding the daily mugs and plates. A shelf holds the weekly mixing bowl and glasses one
small reach away. The top shelf, up high, holds the once-a-year roasting pan. A side key maps the
three tiers.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: sans caps label, soft gray, `MATCH REACH TO FREQUENCY`.
- A cabinet in wood fill `#E7C58B`, cross-section, three shelves clearly stacked.
- Top shelf, up high: the once-a-year roasting pan and a rarely used platter, a small slate tag
  `RARE · A STEP FURTHER OFF`, sitting calmly out of the way.
- Middle shelf, eye-to-waist and pulled forward: a green `#6E8B5B` prime band holding the daily
  mugs and plates, each with a small green calm-dot, a green sans tag `DAILY · PRIME REACH`.
- Lower shelf: the weekly mixing bowl and good glasses, one small reach from the prime band, a
  slate tag `WEEKLY · NEAR`.
- Down the right side, a small three-tier key, top to bottom: `daily to prime`, `weekly to near`,
  `rare to far`, each led by a small marker matching its shelf.
- Caption idea: "The same cabinet, sorted by use rather than by kind. The daily mugs sit at the
  front at hand height, in the green prime band; the weekly bowl is one small reach down; the
  once-a-year roasting pan goes up top, where the rare cost of reaching it barely counts."

**Note for the illustrator:** the lesson is that use, not category, sets the address, so the
sort should read instantly even to someone who cannot read the labels: the everyday mugs are
plainly the easiest to reach. The rare pan up top is not banished or in trouble; it simply sits
where the occasional reach costs almost nothing, so draw it calm, not exiled. Green calm-dots go
only on the daily prime items. Do not add a label, an outline, or a color-code to any item as a
visual cue; that is Chapter 14. Do not turn this into a whole-room plan; it is one cabinet.

## 4. Prime Space Is Expensive (the reframe)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's central reframe as a picture: your easy-reach space is scarce
and valuable, like real estate, so spend it on what earns it. This diagram has no direct HTML
twin; it renders the "Prime Space Is Expensive" section as a paired-panel figure, the busy-street
corner shop against the storage unit across town, then the cabinet that proves it.

**What it shows:** Two facing panels. The left is the expensive spot, the front-of-shelf,
hand-height corner shop on the busy street, best given to the thing your hand wants many times a
day. The right is the cheap spot, the high shelf and the deep back, the storage unit across town,
fine for the thing you want twice a year. Below them, a small before-and-after of one cabinet
makes the arithmetic real.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: sans caps label, soft gray, `PRIME SPACE IS EXPENSIVE`, with a Fraunces ink line beneath:
  "spend your scarce, easy reach on your busiest things".
- Left panel, green header bar `THE EXPENSIVE SPOT`: a small front-of-shelf glyph at hand height,
  a green calm-dot, sans helper `front · hand height · no bend, no stretch`. Newsreader italic
  beneath: "the corner shop on the busy street". A green sans caps line at the base
  `GIVE IT TO WHAT YOU USE MOST`.
- Right panel, slate header bar `THE CHEAP SPOT`: a small high-shelf-and-deep-back glyph, sans
  helper `up high · down low · at the back`. Newsreader italic beneath: "the storage unit across
  town". A slate sans caps line at the base `FINE FOR THE ONCE-A-YEAR THINGS`.
- Beneath the two panels, a small before-and-after of one cabinet: on the left, the everyday mug
  parked at the dark back of a high shelf while a novelty teapot sits front and center; on the
  right, the two swapped, the mug now at the front with a green calm-dot, the teapot moved up and
  back. A single small honey arrow between them tagged `swap`.
- Footer, Newsreader italic, slate: "Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every
  day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off."

**Note for the illustrator:** neither spot is good or bad on its own; the only misstep is the
wrong tenant in each, so keep both panels calm and neutral, no friction-spark and no danger cue
on the cheap spot. The before-and-after is the payoff, so make the swap read as an easy slide,
not a big effort. Anti-shame: a thing landing in the good spot by accident is ordinary and
blameless, just information about which expensive spot is about to come free. Keep it warm, plain,
and non-coercive.

## 5. The Friction Meter, Second Step of Straighten (the milestone)

**Purpose:** Close the chapter with the honest second-Straighten-step move. Nothing left the
house this time either, but placing the homes by frequency cut the room's friction again, so the
live needle steps one more notch past Chapter 12's position toward calm, the second step of the
second S, with the goal still ahead. Mirrors Figure 4 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** The recurring half-circle gauge with the live needle stepped a little further
toward calm than Chapter 12's spot, Chapter 12's position now a faint dashed ghost just behind the
live needle, the Sort-complete milestone sitting further back as a passed marker, and the goal
crosshair still further ahead on the calm side.

**Rough layout:**
- Half-circle gauge, arc colored calm green (left) through honey (middle) to friction spark
  (right), on a pale `#EDE3CE` track.
- The `SORT COMPLETE` milestone in slate, marked on the band with a small slate chip, now sitting
  further back as ground covered two chapters ago.
- The live needle in ink, stepped one clear notch past Chapter 12's position toward calm, its new
  resting spot flagged by a green marker `SECOND STRAIGHTEN STEP` with a small green dot.
- Behind the live needle, a faint dashed gray needle at Chapter 12's position, tagged in small
  sans `Ch 12`, set to about 55 percent opacity so it clearly reads as the ground just covered,
  not the live reading.
- A slate `GOAL` crosshair fully on the green calm side, carrying its `PURPOSE` flag, still
  clearly ahead of the live needle, because two more Straighten chapters (14 and 15) and four more
  S's still follow.
- Bottom band: `CALM` (green, left) and `FRICTION` (spark, right).
- Footer, Newsreader italic, soft gray: "The needle steps again, the second step of Straighten.
  The goal is still ahead."

**Honesty note for the illustrator:** this is the SECOND step of Straighten, not the first, not a
hold, and not an arrival. The live needle must sit one clear notch past Chapter 12's position,
with Chapter 12's spot drawn as the faint dashed ghost just behind it and the Sort-complete
milestone sitting further back still. The needle must NOT reach the goal crosshair; the goal
stays clearly ahead on the green side, because Chapters 14 and 15 and then Shine, Safety,
Standardize, and Sustain all still lie ahead. Do not draw a held needle with no move, and do not
swing it all the way to calm. The honest message is exact: nothing left the house, yet matching
the homes to how often each thing is used made the room easier again, so the needle stepped once
more, plainly, and stopped well short of the end. No confetti, no completion glow.
