## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
Chapter 12 gave every keeper a home, but the homes are all treated the same, so the keys you touch a dozen times a day and the umbrella you reach for twice a month hang at the same easy height. Chapter 13 keeps the same keepers and the same room and places each home by how often you reach for it. The hero device is **the prime zone**, the small band of genuinely easy reach, roughly hips to shoulders, within an arm's length, at the front, and measured from where you actually use the thing. The prime zone is scarce and valuable, so the rule is **the best spot goes to the thing you reach for most**, and the principle reads it as a budget: spend prime space on daily things, send once-a-year things a step further off. Placing is a rough sort into three tiers (daily to prime, weekly to near, rare or seasonal a step off), and the point of use beats the tidy category, so coffee lives by the coffee maker. Nothing is added or removed, yet the friction meter takes its **second Straighten step** toward calm, because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most. ## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule: **The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.**
- The principle: **Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off.**
- The audit reframe: **Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.**
- Point of use: **Store it where you use it, not where it "belongs." The prime spot is measured from your hand at the moment you need the thing, not from a tidy category on a list.**
- One Idea to Keep: **Match the reach to the use. The things your hand wants a dozen times a day should be the easiest to reach; the things you want once a year can afford to be a stretch. Spend your best space on your busiest things, and the room quietly speeds up.**
