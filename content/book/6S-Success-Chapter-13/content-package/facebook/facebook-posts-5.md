## Post 1 · A good home can still be in the wrong place

Stand at the door for a second. The keys hang on a hook. The leash hangs beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a tray below. Every thing has a place. And every place is treated exactly the same.

Look at what your hand actually does here, though. You grab the keys a dozen times a day. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month. Yet both hang at the same easy arm's length, in the same good light, as though you needed them the same amount.

"But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place."

Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds. This chapter just matches each home to how often your hand goes to it. Nothing is added, nothing is carried out.

## Post 2 · Prime space is expensive

Here is the idea most of us have never applied to our own shelves. Not all storage is equal. A small band of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you at hand height, no bending and no stretching. A great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf or down at the back. The pleasant part is small, and that is the fact that changes everything.

Think of it as real estate. The front of the shelf at hand height is the corner shop on the busy street. The deep back of the cabinet is the storage unit across town. Neither is good or bad on its own. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each.

"Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off."

Read that as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of the good, easy reach. Frequency tells you how to spend it.

## Post 3 · The prime zone

There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it. Reach into that band and you take a thing without bending, stretching, climbing, or moving anything aside. Call it the prime zone.

Two things make it easy to misjudge. First, it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker; the prime zone for your reading glasses is at the chair where you read. Second, it is small on purpose. If you try to make everything prime, nothing is.

So what earns a place in that scarce band? Only one thing.

"The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most."

That is the entire rule, and it quietly settles a hundred small arguments about where things go.

## Post 4 · Match the reach to the use

Once you can see the prime zone, placing things is a rough sort into three loose piles.

The everyday things, the ones your hand goes to most, go in the prime zone, front and center. The regular but not constant things, reached for across a week rather than a day, go near and still handy, one small reach away. The rare and seasonal things, touched a few times a year, go a step further off, up high, down low, at the back, or in another room.

That is the whole method, and it fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off.

Do not agonize over the borderline cases either. A busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two, and a rare thing hogging a prime spot just sits there looking increasingly silly. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs.

## Post 5 · The room quietly speeds up

Back to the door one last time. The keys and the leash sit right at hand, first thing in and first thing out. The bag hangs a step to the side. The umbrella waits in its corner for a rainy day. The mail rides the exit path. Nothing left, nothing arrived, and yet the whole entryway is quicker to use than it was an hour ago.

It got easier because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most. The keys now sit exactly where your hand already goes, so that small toll drops to nearly nothing, twelve times over, while the umbrella pays its slightly higher toll only on the rare day it rains.

"Match the reach to the use. The things your hand wants a dozen times a day should be the easiest to reach; the things you want once a year can afford to be a stretch. Spend your best space on your busiest things, and the room quietly speeds up."

Nothing left the house, yet the friction meter takes its second Straighten step, and the goal is still ahead, because two more Straighten chapters and four more S's remain.
