## Search intent
Placement, not removal. Readers arriving here have already decluttered and given their things homes, and now want the everyday things within easy reach and the rare things out of the way, so the room works with less effort. Many have never thought of their own shelves as scarce, valuable real estate. The chapter answers with a reframe and a rule: prime space is expensive, so spend it on what you use every day and send the once-a-year things a step further off, and the best spot goes to the thing you reach for most. It names the prime zone (the small band of easy reach, measured from where you use the thing), sorts each home into daily, weekly, and rare, audits the best spots for things that never earned them, and stores each thing at its point of use. The worked example places the entryway keepers (keys, leash, bag, umbrella, mail) by frequency, then scales up to the kitchen cabinet and the coat closet.

## Content gaps this page fills (vs typical search results)
- Reframes easy-reach space as scarce, valuable real estate you spend well or badly, instead of treating all shelf space as equal
- Gives one plain, testable rule, the best spot goes to the thing you reach for most, rather than generic bins-and-baskets advice
- Names and defines the prime zone (hips to shoulders, arm's reach, front, measured from where you stand), which most guides leave vague
- Separates assigning a home (already done in Chapter 12) from placing it well by frequency (this chapter's only job), which most guides blur
- Delivers the anti-shame audit directly (the waffle iron just landed and never moved, it is not a character flaw) instead of implying the reader is disorganized
- Corrects the store-by-category habit with store-by-use, the point of use beats the tidy category every time
- Keeps scope tight: this places homes by frequency; it does not re-assign homes (Chapter 12), add labels or visual cues (Chapter 14), or map the whole room (Chapter 15)
