## 1. Chapter Opening: Every place is treated exactly the same
Stand at the door one more time. Every keeper from the last chapter has a home now, and nothing is loose, yet every place is treated exactly the same. You grab the keys a dozen times a day and reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, but both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not. Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds, but a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. This chapter fixes that quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- See why a good home in the wrong place still slows a room down
- Recognize your prime zone, the small band of genuinely easy-to-reach space
- Sort each keeper into a rough tier by how often you use it, and place it to match
- Audit the best spots you already have and free the ones being wasted
- Put the busiest things within quick reach and send the rare ones a step further off

## 3. Not Every Home Is in the Best Spot
The last chapter gave every keeper a single clear home, and none of it comes undone here; we are not reopening any of those decisions. But giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts, just as deciding what to keep and deciding where it lives were two separate acts a chapter ago. A home can pass every test we set for it and still sit in a spot that is better than the thing deserves or worse than the thing needs. So the work turns again, from assigning homes to placing them well, decided by a single question we have not asked yet: how often does your hand actually go to this thing? The chapter fences itself off: we are not reopening Sort, not re-deciding whether a thing gets a home, not adding a label or a colored outline yet, and not mapping how a whole room fits together. Those are the next chapters. Today has one narrow job: put each home where the reaching is cheapest for the things you reach for most.

## 4. Prime Space Is Expensive
The idea the rest of the chapter rests on: not all storage is equal. A small amount of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you at hand height, and a great deal of it is a nuisance, up high or down at the back. The pleasant part is small, and that is the fact that changes everything, because the best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce it is valuable and can be spent well or badly. Think of it as real estate: the front of the shelf at hand height is the corner shop on the busy street, expensive and worth it; the high shelf and the deep back are the storage unit across town, cheap and a bother to visit. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each. You feel the arithmetic in a single cabinet: the everyday mug at the back of a high shelf while a once-poured novelty teapot sits front and center. Swap the two and the cabinet changes character overnight.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the principle): Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off. Read it as a budget, not a rule of neatness: frequency tells you how to allocate your fixed amount of good, easy reach.

## 5. The Prime Zone
Give the good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it. There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use: roughly from your hips to your shoulders, within an arm's length, at the front of whatever holds it. Call it the prime zone, the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it. Two things make it easy to misjudge: it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from a tidy category (the prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker), and it is small on purpose, because if you try to make everything prime, nothing is. Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work. Only one thing earns a place in that scarce band.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the rule): The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most. That is the entire rule. When two items want the same good spot, you ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins. This is the old Straighten idea, plain and home-sized: store a thing by how often you use it, and store it where you use it.
- Infographic (The Prime Zone): a standing figure beside a shelf in cross-section, a green band from hip to shoulder at the front labeled the prime zone, easy reach; the far zones (a stretch, a bend, hard to reach) marked as cheaper, with the rule along the bottom.

## 6. Match the Reach to the Use
Once you can see the prime zone, placing things becomes a rough sort into three loose piles. The first is the everyday things your hand goes to most, the coffee and the pans and the coat you actually wear; these go in the prime zone, front and center. The second is the regular but not constant things, reached for across a week, the good scissors, the mixing bowl, the second jacket; these go near and still handy, one small reach away. The third is the rare and the seasonal, the roasting pan, the holiday box, the good umbrella in a dry month; these can go a step further off with no loss at all. The whole method fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off. Do not agonize over borderline cases; put them near and watch, because the room announces where you guessed wrong and sliding a thing one shelf over costs nothing.
- Infographic (Match reach to frequency): a kitchen cabinet in cross-section, the middle shelf a green prime band with daily mugs and plates, the shelf above holding weekly things near, the top shelf holding the once-a-year roasting pan a step further off.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Storing by category instead of by use. Putting all the baking gear together on one high shelf looks tidy, but if you reach for the measuring cup every day and the cake tin once a year, they should not share a hard-to-reach spot. Sort the shelf by frequency, not by kind.
- Definition box (Storing by frequency of use): Storing by frequency of use means placing each home by how often you reach for it: daily things in the prime, easy-reach space, weekly things near, and rare or seasonal things a step further off. The more you use it, the closer and easier it goes.

## 7. Audit Your Prime Space
Before placing anything, look at what is already sitting in your best spots, because in most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it: the waffle iron at the front of the cabinet, the wedding china at eye level, the gadget opened twice. They moved into the good real estate early and have been charging a small toll ever since. So do a quick, honest look and ask the plainest question of the best spots. Say it without blame, because none is owed; nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw, it just landed and never moved, the way water sits where the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict, just useful information about which expensive spot is about to come free. The other half of the audit is a correction: we tend to store by where a thing seems to belong, but belonging on a list is not the same as being useful to your hand. When those two disagree, let the hand win.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the audit reframe): Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.
- Pull-quote (frozen, point of use): Store it where you use it, not where it "belongs." The prime spot is measured from your hand at the moment you need the thing, not from a tidy category on a list.
- Callout (6S Tip): Store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list. Coffee lives by the coffee maker, not across the kitchen with the other pantry goods, because the prime spot is measured from your hand, not from a category.

## 8. Placing the Keepers by Frequency
Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks and the mail sits in its tray. The homes are good; now place them by how often the hand goes to each. Keys take THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height, the first thing your hand meets, because nothing here is touched more. The leash stays right up front beside the keys, prime and quick, because it goes out and back with the dog morning and evening. The reusable bag moves near rather than prime, a hook just to the side, because you take it a few times a week. The umbrella has to move the furthest, stepping off the main run into a stand in the corner or a lower hook, because it works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the dry ones. Tomorrow's mail wants the exit path, brief but constant, so it rides out with you. The entryway is small and mostly frequent, so its tiering is gentle; scale up for a moment to the kitchen cabinet (daily mugs to the front, once-a-year roasting pan up top) and the coat closet (this week's jackets at the front, out-of-season coats slid to the ends). Same principle, more room, the same quiet payoff.
- Callout (Quick Win): Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day. Find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Everyone names the one thing they use every single day that lives in an annoying spot, and together move it to the easiest reach in that space. The most-used things should be the least trouble to get.

## 10. Chapter Close: A Room That Speeds Up
Look at the door one last time. The keys and the leash sit right at hand, the bag hangs a step to the side, the umbrella waits in its corner, and the mail rides the exit path. Nothing left, nothing arrived, and yet the whole entryway is quicker to use than it was an hour ago, because your busiest things now hold your best spots and your rarest things stepped politely aside. That is the reward of the second S's second step, and it compounds: every time you grab the keys without thinking, you collect a little of what you just saved, again tomorrow and the day after. A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule. But look once more and the next gap shows itself: a good spot is obvious to you, the person who set it up, but not to anyone else, or to you on a distracted Tuesday. The placement is right; it is just silent. Making each home announce itself is where we go next.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): Match the reach to the use. The things your hand wants a dozen times a day should be the easiest to reach; the things you want once a year can afford to be a stretch. Spend your best space on your busiest things, and the room quietly speeds up.
