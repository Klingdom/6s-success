## SECTION 1: A GOOD HOME IN THE WRONG PLACE

[HOST]
Let me name the turn we are making, because it is a small one and it is easy to miss.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: A home can be well built and still be in the wrong place.]

[HOST, voice over]
Last chapter gave every keeper a single clear home, and none of that comes undone here. The keys still have their hook. We are not reopening any of those decisions. But giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts. A home can pass every test we set for it and still sit in a spot that is better than the thing deserves, or worse than the thing needs.

[HOST, on camera]
So the work turns again, from assigning homes to placing them well. And it turns on a single question we have not asked yet: how often does your hand actually go to this thing? That answer sorts the whole room.

## SECTION 2: PRIME SPACE IS EXPENSIVE

[HOST]
Here is the idea the rest of this rests on, and it is worth slowing down for.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Not all storage is equal. The good part is scarce.]

[HOST, voice over]
In any room, a small amount of storage is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you at hand height, no bending and no stretching. And a great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf, down at the back of the low cupboard. The pleasant part is small. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly.

[VISUAL: Real-estate metaphor. The front-of-shelf spot at hand height glows like a corner shop on a busy street. The high shelf and deep back fade back like a storage unit across town.]

[HOST, on camera]
Think of it as real estate. The front of the shelf, at the height of your hand, is the corner shop on the busy street. The high shelf and the deep back are the storage unit across town: cheap, roomy, and a bother to visit. Neither is good or bad. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off.]

[HOST]
Read that as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of good, easy reach. Frequency tells you how to spend it.

## SECTION 3: THE PRIME ZONE

[HOST]
Let me give that good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it.

[VISUAL: A standing figure beside a tall shelf in cross-section. A green band spans the front from hip height to shoulder height, labeled the prime zone. Up high reads a stretch; down low reads a bend; the deep back reads hard to reach.]

[HOST, voice over]
There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it. Reach into that band and you take a thing without bending, stretching, climbing, or moving anything aside. Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it.

[HOST, on camera]
Two things make it easy to misjudge. First, it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker. Second, it is small on purpose. If you try to make everything prime, nothing is. Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work. So what earns a place in that scarce band? Only one thing.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.]

[HOST]
That is the entire rule. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins.

---

## SECTION 4: MATCH THE REACH TO THE USE

[HOST]
Once you can see the prime zone, placing things becomes a rough sort into three loose piles.

[VISUAL: A kitchen cabinet in cross-section. The middle green band holds daily mugs and plates, labeled prime. A shelf holds the weekly bowl, labeled near. The top shelf holds a once-a-year roasting pan, labeled a step further off.]

[HOST, voice over]
The first pile is the everyday things, the ones your hand goes to most, the coffee and the pans you cook with. These go in the prime zone, front and center. The second is the regular but not constant, reached for across a week rather than a day, the good scissors, the mixing bowl. These go near and still handy, one small reach away. The third is the rare and the seasonal, the roasting pan, the holiday box, the good umbrella in a dry month. These can go a step further off with no loss at all.

[HOST, on camera]
The whole method fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off. And do not agonize over the borderline cases. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs.

## SECTION 5: AUDIT YOUR PRIME SPACE

[HOST]
Before you place anything, look at what is already sitting in your best spots.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: The prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it.]

[HOST, voice over]
In most homes the best spots are taken by the waffle iron at the front of the cabinet, the wedding china at eye level, the gadget you opened twice. They moved into the good real estate early and have been charging you a small toll ever since, because every time you reach past them, you pay.

[HOST, on camera]
So do a quick, honest look around one cabinet, and ask the plainest question of its best spots.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.]

[HOST]
Say that without any blame in it, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot and never moved, the way water sits where the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is just useful information about which expensive spot is about to come free.

## SECTION 6: PLACING THE KEEPERS BY FREQUENCY

[HOST]
Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks. Now we place them by how often the hand goes to each, and the row rearranges itself.

[B-ROLL: The keys sliding to the closest hook at the door; the leash staying beside them; the bag moving a hook to the side; the umbrella stepping off to a corner stand; the mail riding out in a hand.]

[HOST, voice over]
Start with the keys, because nothing here is touched more. They take THE prime spot, the hook closest to the door at hand height, the first thing your hand meets. The leash goes out just as often, so it stays right up front beside them. The reusable bag comes next, but you take it a few times a week, so it moves near rather than prime. The umbrella works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the dry ones, so it steps a little off the main run, into a stand in the corner. And tomorrow's mail rides the exit path, in your hand for two seconds on the way out.

[HOST, on camera]
The entryway is small and mostly frequent, so the tiering here is gentle. Scale it up and it breathes. In the kitchen cabinet, the daily mugs come to the front at eye-to-waist height, and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top. Same principle, more room, the same quiet payoff: the things you reach for most became the easiest to reach.
