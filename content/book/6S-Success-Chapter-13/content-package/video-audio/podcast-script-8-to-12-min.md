# Podcast Script: Store by Frequency of Use

**Chapter 13, 6S Success: Home Edition (Part 4, Straighten)**
**Slug:** store-by-frequency-of-use
**Estimated runtime:** 8 to 12 minutes (roughly 1,550 spoken words)
**Format:** Conversational, audio-first, single host. No reliance on visuals.

---

## INTRO

Hey, welcome back. Come stand with me at the door one more time, because something is settled now that was loose last time, and it is worth looking at squarely before we start.

Every keeper from the last chapter has a home. The keys hang on a hook. The leash hangs beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook over, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a small tray below. Nothing is loose. Every thing has a place. And here is the odd part. Every place is treated exactly the same.

That is the whole of this episode, so let me make it plain. Look at what your hand actually does at that door. You grab the keys a dozen times a day, every coming and going. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, and only when the sky turns. Yet both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, in the same good light, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not. One of them is doing a dozen jobs a day from a first-rate spot. The other is sitting in that same first-rate spot to do almost nothing.

So giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds. But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. This chapter fixes that, and it does so quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: A GOOD HOME IN THE WRONG PLACE

Let me draw the line before we start. The last chapter did one plain and load-bearing thing: it gave every keeper a single clear home, a spot where the thing lives and returns to in one easy motion. That was real progress, and none of it comes undone here. The keys still have their hook. The leash still has its. We are not going to reopen any of those decisions.

But giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts, just as deciding what to keep and deciding where it lives were two separate acts a chapter ago. A home can pass every test we set for it, close, open, obvious, and still sit in a spot that is better than the thing deserves or worse than the thing needs. The umbrella by the door has a fine home. It just happens to be occupying a reach that the keys would put to far better use.

So the work turns again, from assigning homes to placing them well. Think of it as the difference between owning a set of good tools and hanging them in the right order on the wall. Last chapter got each keeper onto its hook. This chapter decides which hook, by a single question we have not asked yet: how often does your hand actually go to this thing?

And let me fence this off so we stay on the one task. We are not reopening Sort; nothing is up for another vote. We are not re-deciding whether a thing gets a home; it already has one. We are also not adding a label or a colored outline yet, and we are not mapping how a whole room fits together. Those are the next two chapters, each a tool in its own right. Today has one narrow job: take the homes you built and put each one where the reaching is cheapest for the things you reach for most.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: PRIME SPACE IS EXPENSIVE

Here is the idea the rest of this rests on, and it is worth slowing down for, because most of us have never thought of our own shelves this way.

Not all storage is equal. In any room, a small amount of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you, at hand height, no bending and no stretching. And a great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf, down at the back of the low cupboard, behind two other things. The pleasant part is small. That is the fact that changes everything. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly.

Think of it as real estate. The spot at the front of the shelf, at the height of your hand, is the corner shop on the busy street. It is expensive, and it should be, because everything placed there gets used without effort. The high shelf and the deep back of the cabinet are the storage unit across town: cheap, roomy, and a genuine bother to visit. Neither is good or bad on its own. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each, and it is a mistake almost every home makes, usually by accident, because things get stored where they first happened to land.

You can feel this arithmetic in a single cabinet. Picture the mug you drink from every morning parked at the back of a high shelf, and a novelty teapot you have poured from exactly once sitting front and center at eye level. Nothing about that is dramatic, and yet you pay for it every day: a small stretch, a shuffle of the teapot, a reach into the dark at the back, all for the one cup you always want. Swap the two and the cabinet changes character overnight, not because it holds any less but because the tenant in the expensive spot finally matches the traffic through it.

So here is the principle, and I want you to read it as a budget, not a rule of neatness. Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off. You have a fixed amount of the good, easy reach, and far more of the awkward kind. Frequency tells you how to allocate it.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: THE PRIME ZONE

Let me give the good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it.

There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it rather than the back. Reach into that band and you take a thing without bending, stretching, climbing, or moving anything aside. Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it.

Two things make the prime zone easy to misjudge, so hold on to both. First, it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from some tidy category on a chart. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker; the prime zone for your reading glasses is at the chair where you read. Second, it is small on purpose. If you try to make everything prime, nothing is, because the band has room for only so much, and stuffing it full just buries the everyday things behind the rare ones again. Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work.

So what earns a place in that scarce band? Not size, not category, not how nice a thing looks lined up with its cousins. Only one thing earns it, and it is the entire rule of the chapter: the best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.

That is worth committing to memory, because it quietly settles a hundred small arguments about where things go. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness or which shelf they came in on. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins. This is the old Straighten idea, plain and home-sized: store a thing by how often you use it, and store it where you use it.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: MATCH THE REACH TO THE USE

Once you can see the prime zone, placing things becomes a matter of sorting, and the sort is refreshingly rough. You do not need a spreadsheet. You need three loose piles, and most things fall into one at a glance.

The first pile is the everyday things, the ones your hand goes to most, the coffee and the pans you cook with and the coat you actually wear. These go in the prime zone, front and center, no bending and no stretching and nothing to move aside. The second pile is the regular but not constant things, reached for across a week rather than across a day. The good scissors, the mixing bowl, the second jacket. These go near and still handy, a shelf up or over, one small reach away. The third pile is the rare and the seasonal, the things you touch a few times a year, the roasting pan, the holiday box, the good umbrella in a dry month. These can go a step further off with no loss at all.

That is the whole method, and it fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off. Nothing here asks you to buy anything or measure anything. It asks you to notice how often your hand actually moves, and to let that decide the address.

And do not agonize over the borderline cases, either. If a thing sits somewhere between weekly and rare, put it near and watch what happens. A busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two, and a rare thing hogging a prime spot just sits there looking increasingly silly. The room tells you where you guessed wrong, and sliding a thing one shelf over to fix it costs nothing. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FIVE: AUDIT YOUR PRIME SPACE

Before you place anything, it helps to look at what is already sitting in your best spots, because in most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it. The waffle iron at the front of the cabinet. The wedding china at eye level. The gadget you bought with real hope and have opened twice. These moved into the good real estate early, held their ground out of habit, and have been charging you a small toll ever since, because every time you reach past them for the thing you actually want, you pay.

So do a quick, honest look around one cabinet or drawer, and ask the plainest possible question of its best spots. Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.

And say that without any blame in it, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot the day it arrived and simply never moved, the way water sits wherever the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is just information, and useful information, because it tells you exactly which expensive spot is about to come free.

The other half of the audit is a small correction to how we usually think about where things go. We tend to store by where a thing seems to belong, its proper category, its natural shelf with its own kind. But belonging on a list is not the same as being useful to your hand. So store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list. The point of use beats the tidy category every time, because you live at the point of use and only visit the category. Coffee lives by the coffee maker, not across the kitchen with the other pantry goods.

## PLACING THE KEEPERS BY FREQUENCY

Let us put it to work, because it is easier to trust once you have watched the row rearrange itself. Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks and the mail sits in its tray. The homes are good; now we place them by how often the hand goes to each.

Start with the keys, because nothing here is touched more. They go out and come back every single time you leave the house, many times a day, so they take THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height, the very first thing your hand meets on the way in. The keys are the corner shop on the busy street, and they have plainly earned it.

The leash goes out just as often, out and back with the dog morning and evening, so it stays right up front beside the keys, prime and quick. The reusable bag comes next, but its rhythm is different: you take it a few times a week, not a few times a day. So it moves near rather than prime, a hook just to the side, still an easy grab on your way out. The umbrella is the one that has to move the furthest. It works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the many dry ones, so it steps a little off the main run, into a stand in the corner or onto a lower hook. And last is tomorrow's mail, brief but constant, in your hand for two seconds on the way out. It wants to sit right in the exit path, so it rides out with you instead of sinking into a pile.

Now, the entryway is a small area full of mostly frequent things, so the tiering here is gentle; the differences are inches. The principle earns its keep in fuller rooms, so scale it up for a moment. Take the kitchen cabinet: the mugs and plates you use every morning come to the front at eye-to-waist height, right in the prime zone, and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top, where the rare reach costs almost nothing. Or the coat closet: the jackets you actually wear this week hang at the front, and the out-of-season coats get slid to the ends. Same principle, more room for it to breathe, and the same quiet payoff.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART SIX: THE NEEDLE STEPS AGAIN

Now go and look at the friction meter, because it moves once more, and again nothing had to leave the house for it to move.

Last chapter, giving the keepers homes stepped the needle off its long-held ground and one notch into Straighten, past the Sort-complete milestone. Look now and it steps again, a little further toward the calm side. Yet no thing was added and no thing was carried out. The room holds precisely what it held this morning. All that changed is that the homes slid to match how often each thing is used, and somehow the room got easier.

It got easier because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most. Before, the keys and the umbrella cost you the same small effort, and the keys made you pay it a dozen times a day. Now the keys sit exactly where your hand already goes, so that toll drops to nearly nothing, twelve times over, while the umbrella pays its slightly higher toll only on the rare day it rains. Multiply that across a kitchen, a closet, a desk, and the whole room quietly speeds up. This is the second step of Straighten.

But be just as honest about what the meter still shows, because it is not pretending to be finished. Chapter 12's position is now the faint dashed ghost just behind the live needle, and the Sort-complete milestone sits further back still. The goal, the crosshair out on the calm side, is still ahead, and it is still a fair way off. We placed the homes well today, but we have not yet made any of them obvious at a glance, and we have not mapped how a whole room's homes work together. Those are the next two chapters. And past Straighten, four more S's are waiting.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So if you take one thing from this episode, take this. Match the reach to the use. The things your hand wants a dozen times a day should be the easiest to reach; the things you want once a year can afford to be a stretch. Spend your best space on your busiest things, and the room quietly speeds up.

And here is your assignment, and it is small on purpose. Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day. Find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster. And if you want to make it a family thing, here is the challenge. Everyone names the one thing they use every single day that lives in an annoying spot, and together move it to the easiest reach in that space. The most-used things should be the least trouble to get.

## OUTRO AND SOFT CTA

Look at the door one last time. The keys and the leash sit right at hand, first thing in and first thing out. The bag hangs a step to the side. The umbrella waits in its corner for a rainy day. The mail rides the exit path. Nothing left, nothing arrived, and yet the whole entryway is quicker to use than it was an hour ago, because your busiest things now hold your best spots and your rarest things stepped politely aside.

You did not add storage or buy a system. You looked at how often your hand actually moves and let that spend your scarce, valuable reach on your behalf. A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule, quietly, in seconds you never notice losing anymore.
