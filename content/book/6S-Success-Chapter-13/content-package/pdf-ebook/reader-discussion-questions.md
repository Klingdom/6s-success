# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Store by Frequency of Use

*Chapter 13, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter draws a line between two acts: giving a thing a home, and giving it the right home. Chapter 12 did the first; Chapter 13 does the second. Where in your own home have you finished assigning homes but never placed them by how often you reach for each thing, so an everyday thing sits in an awkward spot while a rare thing holds an easy one?

2. "But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place." Think of a spot in your home that passes every test for a good home, close and open and obvious, and yet still feels a little wrong. Is the thing living there better than the spot deserves, or worse than the spot needs?

3. The chapter's reframe is that "The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly." Before reading this, had you ever thought of your own shelves as scarce, valuable real estate? Which of your best spots is currently spent badly?

4. Read as a budget rather than a rule of neatness: "Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off." You have a fixed amount of good, easy reach. If you spent it deliberately on your busiest things, which everyday thing would you promote first, and what would you send a step further off to make room?

5. The prime zone is measured from where you stand when you use a thing, not from a tidy category on a chart. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker; for your reading glasses, at the chair where you read. Which of your daily things lives across the room from where you actually use it, and what would change if it moved to your hand?

6. "Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work." Have you ever tried to make everything easy to reach and found that nothing was, because the good spots got stuffed full? What does it take to let the prime zone stay small on purpose?

7. The one rule is: "The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most." Pick a shelf or drawer where two things compete for the same good spot. Set aside their size, their tidiness, and which set they came in, and ask only which one your hand goes to more often. Does the current arrangement match the answer?

8. The method is a rough sort into three tiers: "the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off." Take one space and name a daily, a weekly, and a rare thing in it. Are all three placed to match how often your hand goes to them?

9. "Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs." How does treating placement as a first draft, rather than a decision you have to get right the first time, change how much you agonize over where things go?

10. The audit is meant to lift blame, not assign it: "Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn." What is the waffle iron in your home, the rarely used thing hogging a prime spot, and how does it feel to read its placement as information rather than a verdict on you?

11. "Store it where you use it, not where it \"belongs.\" The prime spot is measured from your hand at the moment you need the thing, not from a tidy category on a list." Where have you stored something by its proper category, with its own kind, even though you never actually use it there? What would happen if you let the point of use win instead?

12. "The point of use beats the tidy category every time, because you live at the point of use and only visit the category." Which habit is stronger in your home, storing by where a thing belongs on a list or storing by where your hand needs it, and where would swapping the two make the biggest difference?

13. Nothing left the house in this chapter, yet the friction meter still stepped toward calm, because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most. Why might a room feel quicker to use even when it holds exactly what it held this morning, and where in your home could you feel that shift with nothing more than a swap?

14. "A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule, quietly, in seconds you never notice losing anymore." Think of one everyday reach you make without thinking. If you placed it at its easiest spot, how many times a day would that small saving repeat, and what would it add up to across a week?
