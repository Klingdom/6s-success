# Newsletter Edition: Store by Frequency of Use

**Subject line (placeholder):** The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most

**Preview text (placeholder):** Nothing leaves today. The homes just move to match how often you reach for them.

---

Hi friend,

Stand at the door one more time. Every keeper from last issue has a home now: the keys hang on a hook, the leash hangs beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook over, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a small tray below. Nothing is loose. Every thing has a place. And every place is treated exactly the same.

That last part is the whole of this issue. Look at what your hand actually does here. You grab the keys a dozen times a day, every coming and going. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, and only when the sky turns. Yet both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, in the same good light, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not.

Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds. But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. The keys and the umbrella are paying the same rent for very different work. This issue fixes that, and it does so quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

Here is the idea the rest of it rests on, because most of us have never thought of our own shelves this way. Not all storage is equal. In any room, a small amount of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you, at hand height, no bending and no stretching. A great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf, down at the back of the low cupboard, behind two other things. The pleasant part is small. That is the fact that changes everything: the best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable.

**Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off.**

Read that as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of the good, easy reach, and far more of the awkward kind. Frequency tells you how to allocate it.

Let me give that good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it. There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it rather than the back. Reach into that band and you take a thing without bending, stretching, climbing, or moving anything aside. Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it. Two things make it easy to misjudge, so hold on to both: it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from a tidy category on a chart, and it is small on purpose, because if you try to make everything prime, nothing is.

So what earns a place in that scarce band? Not size, not category, not how nice a thing looks lined up with its cousins. Only one thing earns it.

**The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.**

That is the entire rule, and it quietly settles a hundred small arguments about where things go. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins.

Placing things then becomes a rough sort into three loose piles. The everyday things, the ones your hand goes to most, go in the prime zone, front and center. The regular but not constant things, reached for across a week rather than a day, go near and still handy, one small reach away. The rare and the seasonal, the things you touch a few times a year, can go a step further off with no loss at all: up high, down low, at the back, in another room. The whole method fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be.

Before you place anything, though, it helps to look at what is already sitting in your best spots, because in most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it. The waffle iron at the front of the cabinet. The wedding china at eye level. The gadget you opened twice.

**Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.**

Say that without any blame in it, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot the day it arrived and simply never moved, the way water sits wherever the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is just information, and useful information, because it tells you exactly which expensive spot is about to come free.

Now back to the door. The keys go out and come back every single time you leave the house, so they take THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height, the very first thing your hand meets. The leash goes out just as often, so it stays right up front beside them. The reusable bag comes next, but you take it a few times a week, not a few times a day, so it moves near rather than prime, a hook just to the side. The umbrella has to move the furthest: it works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the many dry ones, so it steps a little off the main run, into a stand in the corner. And tomorrow's mail rides the exit path, in your hand for two seconds on the way out. The entryway is small and mostly frequent, so the tiering here is gentle. The principle earns its keep in fuller rooms: the kitchen cabinet, where the daily mugs come to the front and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top; the coat closet, where this week's jackets hang at the front and the out-of-season coats slide to the ends.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it moves once more, and again nothing had to leave the house for it to move. Last issue, giving the keepers homes stepped the needle one notch into Straighten, past the Sort-complete milestone. Look now and it steps again, a little further toward the calm side. Yet no thing was added and no thing was carried out. The room holds precisely what it held this morning. It got easier because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most: the keys now sit exactly where your hand already goes, so that toll drops to nearly nothing, twelve times over. This is the second step of Straighten. Be just as honest about what the meter still shows, though. Chapter 12's position is now the faint dashed ghost just behind the live needle, and the goal crosshair out on the calm side is still a fair way off, because two more Straighten chapters and four more S's remain.

**A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule, quietly, in seconds you never notice losing anymore.**

**To try this week:** Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day. Find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster.
