## Chapter Opening

Stand at the door one more time. Every keeper from the last chapter has a home now. The keys hang on a hook. The leash hangs beside them, the reusable bag on the next hook over, the umbrella on the one after that, and tomorrow's mail waits in a small tray below. Nothing is loose. Every thing has a place. And every place is treated exactly the same.

That last part is the whole of this chapter. Look at what your hand actually does here. You grab the keys a dozen times a day, every coming and going. You reach for the umbrella maybe twice a month, and only when the sky turns. Yet both hang at the same height, at the same easy arm's length, in the same good light, as though you needed them the same amount. You do not. One of them is doing a dozen jobs a day from a first-rate spot; the other is sitting in that same first-rate spot to do almost nothing.

Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, and it holds. But a home can be well built and still be in the wrong place. The keys and the umbrella are paying the same rent for very different work. This chapter fixes that, and it does so quietly, without adding a single thing or carrying anything out. All we are going to do is match each home to how often your hand goes to it.

---

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- See why a good home in the wrong place still slows a room down
- Recognize your prime zone, the small band of genuinely easy-to-reach space
- Sort each keeper into a rough tier by how often you use it, and place it to match
- Audit the best spots you already have and free the ones being wasted
- Put the busiest things within quick reach and send the rare ones a step further off

## Not Every Home Is in the Best Spot

The last chapter did one plain and load-bearing thing: it gave every keeper a single clear home, a spot where the thing lives and returns to in one easy motion. That was real progress, and none of it comes undone here. The keys still have their hook. The leash still has its. We are not going to reopen any of those decisions.

But giving a thing a home and giving it the right home are two separate acts, just as deciding what to keep and deciding where it lives were two separate acts a chapter ago. A home can pass every test we set for it, close, open, obvious, and still sit in a spot that is better than the thing deserves or worse than the thing needs. The umbrella by the door has a fine home. It just happens to be occupying a reach that the keys would put to far better use.

So the work turns again, from assigning homes to placing them well. Think of it as the difference between owning a set of good tools and hanging them in the right order on the wall. Chapter 12 got each keeper off the floor and onto its hook. This chapter decides which hook, by a single question we have not asked yet: how often does your hand actually go to this thing? The answer sorts the whole room, because the things you reach for most and the things you reach for rarely have no business sharing the same quality of spot.

Let me fence this off before we start, so we stay on the one task. We are not reopening Sort; nothing is up for another vote. We are not re-deciding whether a thing gets a home; it already has one. We are also not adding a label or a colored outline yet, and we are not mapping how a whole room fits together. Those are the next two chapters, each a tool in its own right. Today has one narrow job: take the homes you built and put each one where the reaching is cheapest for the things you reach for most.

## Prime Space Is Expensive

Here is the idea the rest of the chapter rests on, and it is worth slowing down for, because most of us have never thought of our own shelves this way.

Not all storage is equal. In any room, a small amount of it is genuinely pleasant to use, right in front of you, at hand height, no bending and no stretching, and a great deal of it is a nuisance, up on the high shelf, down at the back of the low cupboard, behind two other things. The pleasant part is small. That is the fact that changes everything. The best storage in your home is scarce, and like anything scarce, it is valuable, and like anything valuable, it can be spent well or spent badly.

Think of it as real estate. The spot at the front of the shelf, at the height of your hand, is the corner shop on the busy street. It is expensive, and it should be, because everything placed there gets used without effort. The high shelf and the deep back of the cabinet are the storage unit across town: cheap, roomy, and a genuine bother to visit. Neither is good or bad on its own. The only mistake is putting the wrong tenant in each, and it is a mistake almost every home makes, usually by accident, because things get stored where they first happened to land rather than where their frequency of use would put them.

You can feel this arithmetic in a single cabinet. Picture the mug you drink from every morning parked at the back of a high shelf, and a novelty teapot you have poured from exactly once sitting front and center at eye level. Nothing about that is dramatic, and yet you pay for it every day: a small stretch, a shuffle of the teapot, a reach into the dark at the back, all for the one cup you always want, while the teapot lounges in the good seat doing precisely nothing. Swap the two and the cabinet changes character overnight, not because it holds any less but because the tenant in the expensive spot finally matches the traffic through it.

> Prime space is expensive. Spend it on what you use every day, and send the once-a-year things a step further off.

Read that as a budget, not a rule of neatness. You have a fixed amount of the good, easy reach, and far more of the awkward kind. Frequency tells you how to allocate it. The things your hand wants many times a day belong in the expensive spots, earning their keep every time you reach. The things you want twice a year can sit in the cheap seats, because the cost of the occasional stretch is tiny, paid once in a while, instead of a small toll charged every single day.

## The Prime Zone

Let me give the good, easy reach a name, because it is easier to protect something once you can point at it.

There is a small band of space in front of you that is genuinely effortless to use. Roughly, it runs from your hips to your shoulders, it lives within an arm's length, and it sits at the front of whatever holds it rather than the back. Reach into that band and you take a thing without bending, stretching, climbing, or moving anything aside. Call it the prime zone. It is the most valuable storage you own, and there is never very much of it.

Two things make the prime zone easy to misjudge, so hold on to both. First, it is measured from where you stand when you use the thing, not from some tidy category on a chart. The prime zone for your coffee is at the coffee maker; the prime zone for your reading glasses is at the chair where you read. Second, it is small on purpose. If you try to make everything prime, nothing is, because the band has room for only so much, and stuffing it full just buries the everyday things behind the rare ones again. Scarcity is the whole point; it forces a choice, and the choice is what makes the room work.

So what earns a place in that scarce band? Not size, not category, not how nice a thing looks lined up with its cousins. Only one thing earns it.

> The best spot goes to the thing you reach for most.

That is the entire rule, and it is worth committing to memory, because it quietly settles a hundred small arguments about where things go. When two items want the same good spot, you do not weigh their size or their tidiness or which shelf they came in on. You ask which one your hand goes to more often, and that one wins. This is the old Straighten idea, plain and home-sized: store a thing by how often you use it, and store it where you use it. The needle we care about is your own reach, measured across an ordinary week.

## Match the Reach to the Use

Once you can see the prime zone, placing things becomes a matter of sorting, and the sort is refreshingly rough. You do not need a spreadsheet or a label for every hour of the week. You need three loose piles, and most things fall into one at a glance.

The first pile is the everyday things, the ones your hand goes to most, the coffee and the pans you cook with and the coat you actually wear. These go in the prime zone, front and center, no bending and no stretching and nothing to move aside. They earn the expensive spot because they collect on it many times over, every day.

The second pile is the regular but not constant things, reached for across a week rather than across a day. The good scissors, the mixing bowl, the second jacket. These go near and still handy, a shelf up or over, one small reach away. They do not need the very best spot, but they should never be a chore, because you come back to them often enough that a chore would add up.

The third pile is the rare and the seasonal, the things you touch a few times a year, the roasting pan, the holiday box, the spare duvet, the good umbrella in a dry month. These can go a step further off with no loss at all: up high, down low, at the back, in another room, in the storage bin. You will pay the cost of reaching them only once in a long while, so the cost barely registers.

That is the whole method, and it fits in a line: the more often you use a thing, the closer and easier its home should be. Daily to the prime zone, weekly near, rare a step further off. Nothing here asks you to buy anything or measure anything. It asks you to notice how often your hand actually moves, and to let that, rather than habit or category, decide the address.

Do not agonize over the borderline cases, either. If a thing sits somewhere between weekly and rare, put it near and watch what happens. The items you place wrong tend to announce themselves quickly: a busy thing stuck in a far spot will nag at you within a day or two, and a rare thing hogging a prime spot just sits there looking increasingly silly. The room tells you where you guessed wrong, and sliding a thing one shelf over to fix it costs nothing. Frequency placement is not a test you can fail; it is a first draft that your own hand keeps editing, week after week, until each thing lands where it belongs.

> **Common Mistake**
> Storing by category instead of by use. Putting all the baking gear together on one high shelf looks tidy, but if you reach for the measuring cup every day and the cake tin once a year, they should not share a hard-to-reach spot. Sort the shelf by frequency, not by kind.

> **Storing by frequency of use**
> Storing by frequency of use means placing each home by how often you reach for it: daily things in the prime, easy-reach space, weekly things near, and rare or seasonal things a step further off. The more you use it, the closer and easier it goes.

## Audit Your Prime Space

Before you place anything, it helps to look at what is already sitting in your best spots, because in most homes the prime zone is quietly occupied by things that never earned it. The waffle iron at the front of the cabinet. The wedding china at eye level. The gadget you bought with real hope and have opened twice. These moved into the good real estate early, held their ground out of habit, and have been charging you a small toll ever since, because every time you reach past them for the thing you actually want, you pay.

So do a quick, honest look around one cabinet or drawer and ask the plainest possible question of its best spots.

> Look at what is sitting in your best, easiest space right now. If it is something you touch twice a year, it is paying premium rent on a spot it did not earn.

Say that without any blame in it, because none is owed. Nobody placed the waffle iron there as a character flaw. It landed in the good spot the day it arrived and simply never moved, the way water sits wherever the ground is lowest. Finding it there is not a verdict on you; it is just information, and useful information, because it tells you exactly which expensive spot is about to come free.

The other half of the audit is a small correction to how we usually think about where things go. We tend to store by where a thing seems to belong, its proper category, its natural shelf with its own kind. But belonging on a list is not the same as being useful to your hand.

> Store it where you use it, not where it "belongs." The prime spot is measured from your hand at the moment you need the thing, not from a tidy category on a list.

When those two disagree, let the hand win. The point of use beats the tidy category every time, because you live at the point of use and only visit the category.

> **6S Tip**
> Store it where you use it, not where it belongs on a list. Coffee lives by the coffee maker, not across the kitchen with the other pantry goods, because the prime spot is measured from your hand, not from a category.

## Placing the Keepers by Frequency

Back to the door, where four keepers hang on four identical hooks and the mail sits in its tray. The homes are good; now we place them by how often the hand goes to each, and you will see the row rearrange itself.

Start with the keys, because nothing here is touched more. They go out and come back every single time you leave the house, many times a day, so they take THE prime spot, the hook or tray closest to the door at hand height, the very first thing your hand meets on the way in. The keys are the corner shop on the busy street, and they have plainly earned it.

The leash goes out just as often, out and back with the dog morning and evening, so it stays right up front beside the keys, prime and quick, no drawer and no fishing. Two everyday things, two of the best spots. The reusable bag comes next, but its rhythm is different: you take it a few times a week, not a few times a day. So it moves near rather than prime, a hook just to the side, still an easy grab on your way out but no longer holding a front-row seat it does not fill. The umbrella is the one that has to move the furthest. It works hard on a rainy day and does nothing on the many dry ones, so it steps a little off the main run, into a stand in the corner or onto a lower hook, close enough to grab when the sky turns and out of the way the rest of the time. Last is tomorrow's mail, which is brief but constant, in your hand for two seconds on the way out. It wants to sit right in the exit path, so it rides out with you instead of sinking into a pile.

Now, the entryway is a small area full of mostly frequent things, so the tiering here is gentle; the differences are inches. The principle earns its keep in fuller rooms, so scale it up for a moment. Take the kitchen cabinet: the mugs and plates you use every morning come to the front at eye-to-waist height, right in the prime zone, and the once-a-year roasting pan goes up top, where the rare reach costs almost nothing. Or the coat closet: the jackets you actually wear this week hang at the front where you can grab one without looking, and the out-of-season coats get slid to the ends, out of the everyday path until their season comes around. Same principle, more room for it to breathe, and the same quiet payoff: the things you reach for most became the easiest to reach.

> **Quick Win**
> Open one drawer or cabinet you use every day. Find the thing in the easiest-to-reach spot that you almost never use, and trade it with a daily thing that is currently awkward to get to. One swap, and the space is faster.

> **Family Challenge**
> Everyone names the one thing they use every single day that lives in an annoying spot, and together move it to the easiest reach in that space. The most-used things should be the least trouble to get.

## The Needle Steps Again

Go and look at the friction meter, because it moves once more, and again nothing had to leave the house for it to move.

Last chapter, giving the keepers homes stepped the needle off its long-held ground and one notch into Straighten, past the Sort-complete milestone. Look now and it steps again, a little further toward the calm side. Yet no thing was added and no thing was carried out. The room holds precisely what it held this morning. All that changed is that the homes slid to match how often each thing is used, and somehow the room got easier.

It got easier because reaching got cheaper for the things you reach for most. Before, the keys and the umbrella cost you the same small effort, and the keys made you pay it a dozen times a day. Now the keys sit exactly where your hand already goes, so that toll drops to nearly nothing, twelve times over, while the umbrella pays its slightly higher toll only on the rare day it rains. Multiply that across a kitchen, a closet, a desk, and the whole room quietly speeds up. That saved effort is real friction removed, so the needle honestly reflects it, and this is the second step of Straighten.

Be just as honest about what the meter still shows, though, because it is not pretending to be finished. Chapter 12's position is now the faint dashed ghost just behind the live needle, and the Sort-complete milestone sits further back still, both of them ground already covered. The goal, the crosshair out on the calm side, is still ahead, and it is still a fair way off. ## A Room That Speeds Up

Look at the door one last time. The keys and the leash sit right at hand, first thing in and first thing out. The bag hangs a step to the side. The umbrella waits in its corner for a rainy day. The mail rides the exit path. Nothing left, nothing arrived, and yet the whole entryway is quicker to use than it was an hour ago, because your busiest things now hold your best spots and your rarest things stepped politely aside.

That is the reward of the second S's second step, and it compounds. You did not add storage or buy a system. You looked at how often your hand actually moves and let that spend your scarce, valuable reach on your behalf. Every time you grab the keys without thinking, you collect a little of what you just saved, and you collect it again tomorrow, and the day after, for as long as the keys keep their spot. A room arranged by frequency of use pays you back on a schedule, quietly, in seconds you never notice losing anymore.

The homes are assigned and now well placed, and that is the whole of what this chapter asked. But look once more and the next gap shows itself. A good spot is obvious to you, the person who set it up, because you carry the whole arrangement in your head. It is not obvious to anyone else, or to you on a distracted Tuesday, which is why things still drift back and family members still ask where a thing goes. The placement is right; it is just silent. Making each home announce itself, so anyone can find and return a thing without asking, is where we go next.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> Match the reach to the use. The things your hand wants a dozen times a day should be the easiest to reach; the things you want once a year can afford to be a stretch. Spend your best space on your busiest things, and the room quietly speeds up.
