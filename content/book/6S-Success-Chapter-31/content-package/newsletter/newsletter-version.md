**We begin at the door, and not by accident.**

> The entryway is the room you spend the least time in and notice the most, because you cross it every single time you come or go. A calm doorway is the first thing that greets you home and the last thing that speeds you out, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own.

It is also the dirtiest room in the house for its size, because it is where the outside comes in. That is exactly why the clean-and-shine step matters most here.

**Five zones, not one room.** The reason a whole entryway feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not.

> You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.

> The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend.

Here is what you are working toward across all five.

> A finished entryway is one where everyone can land and launch in seconds: keys and phone drop into one tray, today's coat and shoes have an easy home, wet things stay on the mat, and the paper that comes through the door leaves again with a verdict. Nothing lives on the floor inside the door's swing, and two hooks always stand empty for whoever arrives next.

## The Shine method: the nine rules that carry the whole of Part Nine

Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room in this book. Learn it once and you will use it in all nineteen chapters that follow.

> Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way.

Here are the nine rules that method rests on. Every step-by-step procedure in the chapter is just these rules applied to a particular zone.

- work top to bottom
- work dry before wet
- work back to front, clean to dirty
- mist the cloth, not the surface
- use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry
- colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener
- match the cleaner to the surface
- give the cleaner a moment to work
- clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix

That last rule is the oldest idea in the book. Cleaning is how you inspect. When your hand passes over a frayed cord or a loose bracket, your eye catches it, you flag it, and you keep cleaning. **Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it.**

## A worked zone: the landing spot, start to finish

To make it real, here is one zone done the whole way: the tray, the console top, the mirror above it, the wall and switch plate by the door, the charging dock, and the floor beneath. Keys shed grit and coins leave a grey film, so the film under the tray tells you exactly how long it has been since anyone did this properly.

**Gather these inputs into your caddy first, so you never walk off mid-clean:**

- Streak-free glass and mirror cleaner
- Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
- Floor-material-compatible cleaner, matched to your floor
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set: one glass cloth, one surface cloth, one dry buffing cloth, one floor cloth
- A small soft detail brush
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool fitted
- Your portable caddy to carry it all

**Then work the steps in this order:**

1. Clear and unplug. Take everything off, lift the tray right out, and unplug the charging dock at the wall before any damp cloth comes near it.
2. Dust high and dry, first. Run the dry buffing cloth along the top of the mirror frame and the console's back edge. Dry first, always.
3. Do the mirror. Mist the glass cleaner onto the cloth, never onto the glass, so it cannot run behind the frame. Wipe one way, then buff dry in the crossing direction.
4. Wipe the wall and switch plate. With the surface cloth barely damp, lift the finger marks that ring the switch, and dry behind you.
5. Clean the console top. Mist onto the cloth, wipe from the back edge toward the front, give a sticky ring a few seconds to soften, then buff dry.
6. Wash the tray. Clean off the ring of grey film where it stood, then wash the tray inside and out, working the detail brush into the corners where key grit packs in. Dry it fully before the keys go back.
7. Clean the dock and cable, dry only. With the dock still unplugged, wipe it with a dry or barely damp cloth and run the dry brush through the port. Coil the cable loosely.
8. Do the floor, last. Vacuum the grit from under the console with the crevice tool, then mop the sealed floor and let it dry before you set anything back.

And while your hands are there, your eyes inspect: a brown water ring under the hooks, a cracked or frayed charging cable, a power strip that feels warm, a console leg that rocks, green crust on the dock's contacts. Flag each one and keep cleaning. That is one zone, genuinely clean, in about twenty minutes. The other four run the same way, each with its own inputs and steps.

**To try this week:** Pick one zone, and clean it start to finish, to the method. Gather the inputs into your caddy, clear the surface, then work top to bottom, dry before wet, clean cloth then dry cloth, and finish with the floor. The landing spot takes about twenty minutes and is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot. You will feel the difference the next time you walk in. Then, if you want the whole room, do a zone a session across a week, and by the weekend the door is done.

There are three genuinely hard calls the chapter names too, the paper that has to leave with a verdict, the coat you feel guilty about, and the mat that wears out while looking perfectly fine:

> A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen.

When the five zones are done, close the room the way every Part 9 chapter closes.

> This is the entryway the whole loop was for: the same doorway you started with, cleared, calmed, and holding only what the door actually needs. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, because within a month you will not believe the room was ever the other one, and take the after photo as the standard, because it is the picture the door has to match from now on.

**The door is done, and genuinely clean.**

So hold on to the one idea this chapter comes to rest on.

> A zone is not clean because it looks clean. It is clean because you gathered the right inputs, took it apart, and cleaned every surface to a method, top to bottom, dry before wet, one cloth to clean and one to dry, inspecting as you went. Do that to one zone this weekend, and let the door start working for you every time you cross it.

> **6S Tip:** Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead.
