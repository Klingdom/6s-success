Welcome to the part of the book where the teaching stops and the doing begins. Part Nine points the finished 6S loop at one real room at a time, and it starts at the door.

## 2. The nine rules Shine rests on

Shine is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room.

The nine rules:

- work top to bottom
- work dry before wet
- work back to front, clean to dirty
- mist the cloth, not the surface
- use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry
- colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener
- match the cleaner to the surface
- give the cleaner a moment to work
- clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix

Learn it once, and every zone procedure is just these rules applied to a particular surface.

## 3. Five small zones, not one big job

The reason a whole entryway feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not.

"The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend."

You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. Each zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.

## 4. Exactly how to clean the landing spot

The landing spot is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot, and it takes about twenty minutes.

Gather into your caddy first: streak-free glass cleaner, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a floor cleaner matched to your floor, a colour-coded microfiber set, a soft detail brush, a compact vacuum with the crevice tool.

Then work the order:

- Clear the console and unplug the dock before any damp cloth comes near it.
- Dust high and dry, first.
- Do the mirror: mist the cloth, wipe one way, buff dry across it.
- Wipe the wall and switch plate, then the console top, back to front.
- Wash the tray, clean the dock dry only.
- Vacuum and mop the floor, last.

## 5. The boot tray is the whole point

The boot tray takes longer than you expect, and that is the point. The salt and grit in it is exactly what scratches every floor in the house.

For the shoe zone, gather a floor cleaner, a multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded cloth set, a stiff dry brush, a compact vacuum, and access to an outside tap. The steps that matter most:

- Outside, knock the mud off the soles with the stiff brush.
- Wipe the shoes you keep and the rack shelves, front to back.
- Take the tray out, hose it, scrub the salt film out of the corners, and dry it fully before it comes back.
- Vacuum under the rack, then mop the clear strip between door and rack, last.

## 6. Clean to inspect, the safety pass fixes

Here is the oldest idea in the book, and it is why cleaning to a method pays twice.

Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. While you clean you inspect: a frayed charging cord, a loose hook bracket, a soft patch of rot at the base of the door, green crust of corrosion on a dock's contacts. You do not stop to fix it. You note it, and keep cleaning.

"Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it."

Clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix. The clean leaves the zone genuinely clean and hands the safety pass a ready-made list. You find the hidden hazard exactly because your hand was already there.

## 7. One colour per cloth, no fabric softener

One small habit separates real cleaning from moving dirt around: colour-code your cloths.

"Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead."

Use one grey cloth for everything and you carry salt off the boot tray straight onto the mirror. Three colours, kept apart and washed by colour, and every surface gets a clean cloth.

## 8. Coats are counted by weather, not by number

The coat you feel guilty about is one of the entryway's hardest calls. Here is the rule that settles it.

"Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm."

Count by weather, and the rail empties itself. Two hooks always stand empty for whoever arrives next.

## 9. Test the mat, do not look at it

The mat is most of the entryway job, because it is the one thing standing between the outside and every other floor in the house. And it wears out while looking perfectly fine.

"A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen."

Walk in on a dry, gritty day, wipe your feet, then lift the mat. If the grit is sitting on top and walking off, replace it this month.
