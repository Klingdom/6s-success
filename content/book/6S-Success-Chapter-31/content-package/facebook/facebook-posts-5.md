## Post 1 · A tidy room and a clean room are not the same thing

Most of us were never taught to clean, only to tidy. So a room can look sorted and still not be clean, and the difference is method: the order you work in, the cloth you use, and whether your eye is inspecting while your hand wipes.

"Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way."

Top to bottom so drips fall on what you have not cleaned yet. Dry before wet so you dust the grit off instead of dragging it into mud. Mist the cloth so nothing runs behind the frame. ## Post 2 · The entryway is five small zones, not one big job

The reason the whole entryway feels like too much is that you are looking at it as one job. It is not.

"You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon."

So here is the map for the door:

"The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend."

Pick one. The landing spot takes about twenty minutes and is the single highest-leverage square foot in the room. Read the free chapter for the exact steps.

## Post 3 · One small habit that makes cleaning actually work

The cheapest upgrade to your cleaning is not a product, it is how you handle your cloths.

"Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead."

That is the whole trick. One colour never touches the floor, another never leaves it, and the boot tray grit never rides up onto the console. And use two cloths per surface, one to clean and one to dry, because a wet floor at a doorway is a fall waiting to happen. ## Post 4 · The coat you feel guilty about

Every entryway has more coats than the family has weather. The fix is a rule, not a mood.

"Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm."

That last line is the one that frees people. The money is gone either way, so the only question left is whether the coat is doing any good on your hook or would do more good on someone's back. Read the free chapter.

## Post 5 · Test the mat, do not just look at it

Here is the one that quietly costs you money. Your doormat wears out long before it looks worn out.

"A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen."

Walk in on a dry, gritty day, wipe your feet the way you actually do, then lift the mat and look at the fibres and the floor beneath. If the grit is sitting on top and walking straight off, replace it this month.
