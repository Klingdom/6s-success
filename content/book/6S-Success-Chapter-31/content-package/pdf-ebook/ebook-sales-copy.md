## Not just what to do. Exactly how, one zone at a time.

Welcome to the part of the book where the teaching stops and the doing begins. Behind you are all six S's and the loop that keeps them turning. You do not need another lesson. What you need now is somewhere to point what you have learned, and a set of instructions precise enough to follow with your sleeves rolled up. So the rest of the book is a set of playbooks, one room at a time, and the heart of each one is the same promise: for every small zone of the room, you get the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, done the way a professional would do it rather than the way most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better. The method was the hard part, and you have it. This is where a real room becomes genuinely, inspectably clean.

And we begin at the door, and not by accident.

> The entryway is the room you spend the least time in and notice the most, because you cross it every single time you come or go. A calm doorway is the first thing that greets you home and the last thing that speeds you out, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own.

## Stop seeing the room. Start seeing the zones.

The reason a whole entryway feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not. Like every room in this part of the book, it is a small collection of separate zones that share a name and a bit of floor, and the trick to all of Part Nine is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones.

> You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.

> The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend.

You can run them in any order, but within each one the loop runs in the same fixed sequence, and the deep, detailed part of it, the part this chapter is really about, is Shine. Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain get a brisk pass, because you already own them. Then the chapter slows right down.

## Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean.

It is also the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room in the book. Learn it once and it carries through all twenty room chapters.

> Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way.

The method rests on nine plain rules, and every zone procedure in the chapter is just these rules applied to a particular surface: work top to bottom, so dust and drips fall onto what you have not cleaned yet; work dry before wet, so a damp cloth over dry grit does not make mud and scratch the finish; work back to front and clean to dirty; mist the cloth, not the surface, so nothing runs down behind a frame; use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry, because a wet floor at a doorway is a fall waiting to happen; colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener, which clogs the fibres and kills their grab; match the cleaner to the surface; give the cleaner a moment to work rather than scrubbing it dry; and clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix, because your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. Shine finds the frayed cord or the loose bracket; the safety pass fixes it.

## Zone by zone: exactly how to clean it.

Here is the core of the chapter. Each of the five zones gets the same three things: the exact inputs to gather into your caddy before you start, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there.

For the **landing spot**, you gather a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a floor-material-compatible cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber cloth set, a small soft detail brush, a compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy; then you clear and unplug, dust high and dry, do the mirror, wipe the wall and switch plate, clean the console top, wash the tray, clean the dock and cable dry only, and do the floor last, flagging a brown water ring under the hooks, a frayed charging cable, or a warm power strip as you go. The **coats** zone works from the shelf down: brush the coats, wipe the rail and hooks, empty and scrub the umbrella stand, and flag a hook pulling from the wall or a damp bloom behind the soaked coats. The **shoes** zone gives the boot tray the time it deserves, because the salt and grit in it is exactly what scratches every floor in the house, and finishes with the clear strip of floor between the door and the rack. The **bench or console** takes the drawer fully out to tip the sand from its corners, wipe the runners, and vacuum the cavity behind it. And the **door, mat, and immediate floor** treats the mat as most of the job, because it is the one thing standing between the outside and every other floor in the house: you vacuum both faces, beat it outdoors, and return it only once it is fully dry.

> A finished entryway is one where everyone can land and launch in seconds: keys and phone drop into one tray, today's coat and shoes have an easy home, wet things stay on the mat, and the paper that comes through the door leaves again with a verdict. Nothing lives on the floor inside the door's swing, and two hooks always stand empty for whoever arrives next.

## The three calls you have been avoiding for months.

Cleaning to a method will make the room shine, but three genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and a playbook earns its keep by naming them. The first is the paper on the landing spot, which gets a verdict with only three answers, act, file, or recycle, on a fourteen-day rule: a sheet that passes fourteen days has stopped being paperwork and become a decision. The second is the coat you feel guilty about.

> Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm.

The third is the mat, which quietly wears out while looking perfectly fine.

> A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen.

## Do not wipe until it looks better. Clean to a method.

One caution, so a surface ends up genuinely clean and not just tidied.

> Wiping until it looks better instead of cleaning to a method. Skipping the dry-dust-first step so you smear grit into mud, spraying cleaner straight onto a mirror so it runs behind the frame, leaving a wet floor to streak and slip, using one grey cloth for everything so you move dirt from the boot tray to the console. The method exists because it is faster and it actually works. Follow the order.

Gather the inputs first, work the order, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and keep a dedicated cloth colour for each job so you never carry grit from a dirty surface onto a clean one. The method is not extra effort; it is less, because it works the first time.
