## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
The teaching is done; now the doing begins, and the doing is the point. You own the whole six-S method and the loop that renews it, so a playbook does not re-teach Sort or Shine; it takes one real room, breaks it into the handful of small zones it is really made of, and, for every zone, gives the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, done the way a professional would rather than the way most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better. That distinction is the whole game, because a tidy room and a genuinely clean room are not the same thing, and the difference is method. It begins at the door because the entryway is the room you spend the least time in and notice the most: you cross it every single time you come or go, so a calm doorway is the first thing that greets you home and the last thing that speeds you out, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own; it is also the dirtiest room in the house for its size, because it is where the outside comes in, grit, road salt, rain, and everything that rides in on shoes and sleeves, which is exactly why the clean-and-shine step matters most here. The freeing idea is that a room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, so you do not 6S a whole entryway, you 6S one of its five small zones, then the next: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. The quick passes get a brisk treatment (Sort each zone first because you cannot clean a surface that is covered; Straighten survivors into a home chosen by use; Safety, Standardize, and Sustain, each short, after the shine), and then the chapter slows right down for Shine and teaches the universal nine-rule Shine method that governs every zone and every room: work top to bottom; work dry before wet; work back to front, clean to dirty; mist the cloth, not the surface; use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry; colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener; match the cleaner to the surface; give the cleaner a moment to work; and clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix, because Shine finds the hazard and the safety pass fixes it. Then, as the heart of the chapter, "Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It" gives every zone the same three things: the exact INPUTS to gather into the caddy, the NUMBERED steps in the true order of work (clear and unplug, dust high and dry, glass, surfaces, details, floor last), and an inspect-and-flag list. It lands the definition of a finished entryway, one where everyone can land and launch in seconds; it names the room's three genuinely hard calls, the paper with no verdict (act, file, or recycle; a sheet past fourteen days is a decision, not paperwork), the guilty coat (count by weather, not by number, and the expensive never-worn coat is not exempt because the money is already spent), and the tired mat (a mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it rather than look at it); it runs the door's real hazards through the five safety questions and acts there on everything the clean flagged; and it closes with the Part 9 signature that replaces the friction meter, the before-and-after room pair and the two photos to take, the before you keep because within a month you will not believe the room was ever the other one, and the after you tape inside the coat cupboard as the standard the door has to match from now on. Then a light hand-off to the kitchen. It does NOT re-teach the six S's; it points the loop the reader already owns at their first real room, and shows them precisely how to clean it.

## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule (hero): **You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.**
- The five zones (frozen, structural): **The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend.**
- The Shine method (frozen, pull-quote, the heart of the rewrite): **Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way.**
- The nine Shine rules (frozen list, govern every zone procedure): **work top to bottom · work dry before wet · work back to front, clean to dirty · mist the cloth, not the surface · use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry · colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener · match the cleaner to the surface · give the cleaner a moment to work · clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix.** (Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it.)
- The definition box (frozen): **A finished entryway is one where everyone can land and launch in seconds: keys and phone drop into one tray, today's coat and shoes have an easy home, wet things stay on the mat, and the paper that comes through the door leaves again with a verdict. Nothing lives on the floor inside the door's swing, and two hooks always stand empty for whoever arrives next.**
- The first-and-last reframe (frozen, pull-quote): **The entryway is the room you spend the least time in and notice the most, because you cross it every single time you come or go. A calm doorway is the first thing that greets you home and the last thing that speeds you out, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own.**
- The count-by-weather reframe (frozen, pull-quote): **Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm.**
- The worn-mat reframe (frozen, pull-quote): **A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen.**
- The before-and-after caption (frozen, governs the signature figure): **This is the entryway the whole loop was for: the same doorway you started with, cleared, calmed, and holding only what the door actually needs. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, because within a month you will not believe the room was ever the other one, and take the after photo as the standard, because it is the picture the door has to match from now on.**
- One Idea to Keep (frozen close, UPDATED for the rewrite): **A zone is not clean because it looks clean. It is clean because you gathered the right inputs, took it apart, and cleaned every surface to a method, top to bottom, dry before wet, one cloth to clean and one to dry, inspecting as you went. Do that to one zone this weekend, and let the door start working for you every time you cross it.**
- Callout (Quick Win): **Do one zone, start to finish, to the method: gather the inputs into your caddy, clear the surface, then work top to bottom, dry before wet, clean cloth then dry cloth, and finish with the floor. The landing spot takes about twenty minutes and is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot. You will feel the difference the next time you walk in.**
- Callout (6S Tip): **Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead.**
- Callout (Family Challenge): **Everyone claims their own named shoe shelf and their own hook, with the lowest hook set at the shortest person's height, and two hooks left empty for whoever visits. A third pair or a stray coat then has visibly nowhere to go, and nobody has to nag, because the empty hook does the asking.**
- Callout (Common Mistake): **Wiping until it looks better instead of cleaning to a method. Skipping the dry-dust-first step so you smear grit into mud, spraying cleaner straight onto a mirror so it runs behind the frame, leaving a wet floor to streak and slip, using one grey cloth for everything so you move dirt from the boot tray to the console. The method exists because it is faster and it actually works. Follow the order.**
