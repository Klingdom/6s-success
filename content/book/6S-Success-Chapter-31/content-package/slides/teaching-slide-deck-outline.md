### Slide 2 · The teaching stops, the doing begins

- Look at what you are holding at the start of Part Nine. Behind you are all six S's and the loop that keeps them turning. You do not need another lesson. What you need now is somewhere to point what you have learned, and a set of instructions precise enough to follow with your sleeves rolled up. So the rest of this book is a set of playbooks, one room at a time, and the heart of each one is the same promise: for every small zone of the room, you get the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, done the way a professional would do it rather than the way most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better.
- That distinction is the whole game. A tidy room and a genuinely clean room are not the same thing, and the difference is method: the order you work in, the cloth you use, the cleaner you match to the surface, and the fact that while your hand is on every surface, your eye is inspecting it. We begin at the door, and not by accident. The entryway is the room you cross most and notice most, and it is also the dirtiest room in the house for its size, because it is where the outside comes in: grit, road salt, rain, and everything that rides in on shoes and sleeves. That is exactly why the clean-and-shine step matters most here, and why this chapter spends most of its length on precisely how to do it.

### Slide 4 · Five zones, not one room

- The reason a whole entryway feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not. Like every room in this part of the book, it is a small collection of separate zones that share a name and a bit of floor, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones. You can run the zones in any order, but within each one the loop runs in the same fixed sequence, and the deep, detailed part of it, the part this chapter is really about, is Shine.
- Here is what you are working toward across all five, the finished state every zone is aiming at. A finished entryway is not one that merely looks neat for an afternoon; it is one where the room does its job for the people who cross it, every day, on its own.

### Slide 5 · The quick passes, then slow down for Shine

- Before the deep clean, four of the other five passes are quick, so we move through them briskly and then slow right down for Shine, which is where the real work is. Sort each zone first, because you cannot clean a surface that is covered: empty it, and let nothing back without a verdict, keys and today's coat and this week's shoes stay, the dead batteries and odd gloves and split-soled trainers go to the out box. Straighten what survives into a home chosen by use: the tray where your hand opens, hooks for daily coats, the lowest hook at the shortest person's height, boots in the tray, two mats and no more. Those two passes clear the ground so the clean can happen.
- Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short. Safety is where you act on everything your cleaning turned up, anchoring the tippy console, taking the overloaded power strip out of the umbrella's drip line, replacing the frayed cable you flagged. Standardize locks the result: photograph the finished landing spot and tape it inside the coat cupboard, name each person's shoe shelf, tape the outline where the mat sits. And Sustain hangs the upkeep on things that already happen: coat off, hands full, everything into the tray; dishwasher on, console back to three; bin night, the boot tray goes out. That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

### Slide 6 · How to Shine: the method, and the nine rules

- Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room in this book. Learn it once here and you will use it in all nineteen chapters that follow. Every step-by-step procedure in this chapter is just these nine rules applied to a particular zone.
- Work top to bottom, so the dust and drips you release fall onto what you have not cleaned yet, never onto what you have. Work dry before wet, because a damp cloth dragged over dry grit makes mud and scratches the finish. Work back to front, clean to dirty, so you never drag grime across ground you have already cleaned. Mist the cloth, not the surface, so nothing runs down behind a frame and no overspray lands where it should not. Use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry, because a surface left wet air-dries to streaks and a wet floor at a doorway is a fall waiting to happen. Colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener, which clogs the fibres and kills their grab. Match the cleaner to the surface, never a harsh spray on a surface it can strip or etch. Give the cleaner a moment to work, so the chemistry lifts the soil and you do not grind at the finish. And clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix.
- The Shine method, frozen: "Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way." The book line, verbatim: "Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method."

### Slide 7 · The Shine method and the entryway kit

So gather the kit into your caddy before you start, so you are not walking off mid-clean to fetch a cloth. The method is an order of work, top to bottom and dry before wet; the kit is a short, honest list of product types, and nothing the door does not need. Learn both here once and they carry through all twenty room chapters. Types, not brands. Buy for the job.
- The kit, by type. Cleaners: a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for everyday sealed surfaces, a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner for glass, and a floor cleaner matched to your actual floor material. Cloths: a colour-coded microfiber set, one colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray. Tools: a compact vacuum with crevice and soft-brush attachments, a small soft detail brush, a stiff dry brush for the boot tray, and a portable caddy to carry it all.
- 6S Tip, verbatim: "Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead."

### Slide 8 · Zone by zone: the landing spot, and the coats

- Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Gather the inputs into your caddy before you start.
- Zone 1, the landing spot: the tray, the console top, the mirror or art above it, the wall and switch plate, the charging dock, and the floor beneath. Inputs: streak-free glass and mirror cleaner, neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, floor-material-compatible cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber set (glass, surface, dry buffing, floor), a small soft detail brush, a compact vacuum with the crevice tool, and the caddy. Steps, in order: clear and unplug; dust high and dry, first; do the mirror or glass (mist the cloth, wipe one way, buff dry in the crossing direction); wipe the wall and switch plate; clean the console top back to front; wash the tray, working the detail brush into the corners; clean the dock and cable, dry only; then do the floor, last, vacuum then mop. It is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot, about twenty minutes.
- Zone 2, the coats and outerwear: the shelf, the hat and glove bin, the rail and hooks, the coats' shoulders, the wall behind, the umbrella stand, and the floor. Inputs: multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set (surface, dry, floor), a vacuum with the soft upholstery brush and crevice tool, a small detail brush for the umbrella stand, and the caddy. Steps, working from the shelf down: take the bin down and empty it; dust the shelf then wipe it; clean the bin inside and out; brush the coats' shoulders and collars downward; wipe the rail and hooks and dry them so nothing rusts; wipe the wall behind the hooks; empty and scrub the umbrella stand and stand it up to dry; then the floor, last.

### Slide 9 · Zone by zone: the shoes, the bench, and the door

- Zone 3, the shoes and boots: the rack shelves, the footwear, the boot tray, the wall and skirting, the floor under the rack, and the clear strip inside the door. Inputs: floor-material-compatible cleaner, multi-surface cleaner, a colour-coded set (surface, floor), a stiff dry brush for caked mud and the boot tray, a vacuum with the crevice tool, access to an outside tap or hose, and the caddy. Steps: clear the rack and knock off the mud outside; wipe the shoes you keep; wipe the rack shelves front to back; take the boot tray outside and wash it, scrubbing the salt film and grit from the corners; wipe the wall and skirting; vacuum under the rack; then do the clear floor strip, last. The boot tray takes longer than you expect, and it is the whole point: its salt and grit is exactly what scratches every floor in the house.
- Zone 4, the bench or console: the wall and back edge, the top, the seat, the drawer taken fully out, the runners and cavity, the fronts and legs, and the floor. ### Slide 10 · Clean to inspect, and flag; the safety pass fixes

- Cleaning is how you inspect. This is the oldest idea in the book, from the very first Shine chapter: your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the thing you never normally see, a frayed cord, a loose bracket, a soft patch of rot, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Each zone's inspect-and-flag list is exactly the set of things your hands are already near: the brown water ring under the hooks, the cracked cable or warm plug, the console that rocks, the hook pulling from the wall, the split boot tray, the curled mat, the dropped hinge, the soft threshold where rain keeps sitting.
- Then, after the shine, run the five safety questions once directly, so nothing is missed, and act on everything the cleaning flagged. Water and electricity: keep the charging dock and any power strip out of the drip line below the wet umbrellas and sleeves. Burn or fire: one dock, one outlet, nothing chained. Poison, choke, or strangle: long straps off the low hooks at a child's neck height, loose button batteries and coins into a lidded tin. Fall, cut, or crush: anchor the tippy console or the top-heavy rack, heavy things low. And the plain fall: keep the stride of floor inside the door clear, and the mat flat and gripped.
- The book line, verbatim: "Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it." Common Mistake, verbatim: "Wiping until it looks better instead of cleaning to a method. Skipping the dry-dust-first step so you smear grit into mud, spraying cleaner straight onto a mirror so it runs behind the frame, leaving a wet floor to streak and slip, using one grey cloth for everything so you move dirt from the boot tray to the console. The method exists because it is faster and it actually works. Follow the order."

### Slide 11 · The three calls that matter here

- Cleaning to a method will make the room shine, but three genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and a playbook earns its keep by naming them. The first is the paper on the landing spot: nothing returns to the surface without a verdict, and there are only three, act, file, or recycle. A sheet that has sat more than fourteen days has stopped being paperwork and become a decision, so do it tonight or recycle it. Both are answers; leaving it on the surface is not.
- The second is the coat you feel guilty about, and it is settled by weather, not by number. The third is the mat, which wears out while looking perfectly fine, so you test it instead of looking at it: walk in on a dry, gritty day, wipe your feet the way you actually do, then lift the mat and look at both the floor beneath and the fibres. If the grit is sitting on top and walking straight off, replace it this month.
- The count-by-weather reframe, frozen: "Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm." The worn-mat reframe, frozen: "A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen."
