**The recurring motif.** The hero of the chapter is HOW to clean a room to a professional standard, one small zone at a time, and the single idea that opens the applied half of the book: you do not 6S a whole room, you 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done, because a room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon. But the deeper hero, the thing this chapter spends most of its length on, is the universal Shine method: nine rules that turn wiping-until-it-looks-better into cleaning-to-a-method, learned once and applied five times, zone by zone, each with its exact inputs and its ordered, numbered steps. That distinction, a tidy room versus a genuinely clean one, is the whole game. One image carries the whole set: the entryway drawn in two states side by side, on the left "before", coats sliding off overloaded hooks, shoes across the swing of the door, a console breeding paper and chargers, a mat curling at the toe, the whole side drawn in a muted graying tone, and on the right "after", the same doorway calm in green, one coat per person with two hooks visibly empty, shoes paired on the rack with a clear stride of floor, a single tray and one upright folder, a mat dead flat inside a taped outline, every surface visibly clean, so the promise of the whole part is clear in a single picture, the same room, two completely different mornings, transformed not by buying anything new but by pointing the loop at it and cleaning each zone to a method. The arc of the chapter is the move from seeing a room as one overwhelming job to seeing it as five small finishable zones, through the case for beginning at the door, to the freeing idea itself (five zones, not one room) with the rule and the definition of a finished entryway, to the four quick passes and then the deep slow-down for Shine, to the nine-rule method taught once (the memorable card), to the method-and-kit figure, to the zone-by-zone procedures walked one at a time with their inputs and ordered steps, to clean-to-inspect-and-flag and the safety pass that fixes, to the three genuinely hard calls (the paper with no verdict, the guilty coat counted by weather, the tired mat you test rather than look at), and finally to the Part 9 signature that closes every room, the before-and-after pair and the two photos to take. **Two fences to hold visually.** First, Chapter 31 OPENS Part Nine and owns the room-playbook FORMAT: the room decomposed into its micro-zones and each cleaned to a method with the zone's own specific inputs and steps. Its whole work is to PROVE the format on the first real room, the entryway, and its centre of gravity is the HOW: the nine-rule Shine method and the five per-zone clean-and-shine procedures. ## Beat 9 · Clean to inspect, and flag; the safety pass fixes

The idea that runs under every zone procedure, given its own beat: cleaning is how you inspect, so while your hand is on every surface your eye is inspecting it, and when you find the thing you never normally see you flag it and keep cleaning.

- Composition: a hazard-map image on panel #FBF7EF, the finished entryway drawn calm with a handful of slate #3C5A6B flag marks pinned to the exact spots the cleaning turns them up: a flag on the cable under the console, on the loose hook, on the boot tray split, on the curled mat, on the soft threshold. A short arrow leads each flag from Shine (green #6E8B5B) to Safety (slate #3C5A6B), "found here, fixed here".
- Palette: paper and panel ground, green #6E8B5B on the Shine origin of each flag, slate #3C5A6B on the flags, the arrows, and the Safety destination. Warm; the register is the practised eye, not alarm.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix; the safety pass fixes them."
- Point: this is the oldest idea in the book, from the very first Shine chapter. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the frayed cord, the loose bracket, the soft patch of rot, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Then, after the shine, run the five safety questions once directly, and act on everything the cleaning flagged. Water and electricity: the charging dock out of the umbrella's drip line. Burn or fire: one dock, one outlet, nothing chained. Poison, choke, or strangle: straps high, loose button batteries into a lidded tin. Fall, cut, or crush: anchor the tippy console or top-heavy rack. And the plain fall: the stride of floor inside the door clear, and the mat flat and gripped.
- The book line runs in Newsreader italic: "Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it." The Common Mistake sits in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Wiping until it looks better instead of cleaning to a method. Skipping the dry-dust-first step so you smear grit into mud, spraying cleaner straight onto a mirror so it runs behind the frame, leaving a wet floor to streak and slip, using one grey cloth for everything so you move dirt from the boot tray to the console. The method exists because it is faster and it actually works. Follow the order."

## Beat 10 · The three calls that matter here

The judgement the method cannot make for you, given the three-decisions treatment: cleaning to a method makes the room shine, but three genuinely hard calls decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and a playbook earns its keep by naming them.

- Composition: three small decision vignettes on panel #FBF7EF, drawn plainly, side by side. One, the paper: a single sheet with three doors marked "act, file, recycle" and a small "fourteen days" mark. Two, the coat: a rail counted by weather icons (rain, cold, wind) rather than by number, one per kind. Three, the mat: a hand lifting the mat to reveal the floor and the flattened fibres beneath, the grit riding straight over.
- Palette: paper and panel ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, slate #3C5A6B on the labels, honey #DDA63A on the Family Challenge callout. Drawn plainly, without scolding; three honest calls, named for the reader.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "three hard calls the playbook names for you."
- Point: the first is the paper on the landing spot, and every sheet gets a verdict, act, file, or recycle; a sheet past fourteen days has become a decision, so do it tonight or recycle it, because both are answers and leaving it on the surface is not. The second is the coat you feel guilty about, settled by weather, not by number.
