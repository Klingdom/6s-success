### Slide 1 (hook)

**Welcome to the part of the book where the teaching stops and the doing begins. All six S's are behind you. Now watch the loop clean one real room, one small zone at a time, to a method.**

This is the entryway, the smallest room in the house and the one that sets the tone for every other. It is also the dirtiest room in the house for its size, because it is where the outside comes in: grit, road salt, rain, and everything that rides in on shoes and sleeves. So this playbook does not stop at telling you what to do. For every small zone of the room it gives you the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it to a professional standard, so the door does not just get tidy, it gets genuinely, inspectably clean.

We begin at the door, and not by accident. It is the room you cross most and notice most, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own. Swipe, and watch it change.

### Slide 2

**A tidy room and a genuinely clean room are not the same thing. The difference is method. That distinction is the whole game.**

Most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better. A professional works to a method: the order you work in, the cloth you use, the cleaner you match to the surface, and the fact that while your hand is on every surface, your eye is inspecting it. Do it that way and the console top is not just clear, it is genuinely clean, the grey film of key grit gone from under the tray, the mirror buffed bright against the light.

That is what this chapter is really about, and why it spends most of its length on precisely how to clean. Not what to do, which you already know, but exactly how to do it, with your sleeves rolled up. We begin at the door.

The book line, verbatim: "That distinction is the whole game."

### Slide 3

**You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done.**

The reason a whole entryway feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not. It is a small collection of separate zones that share a name and a bit of floor, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones.

The rule, frozen, the hero of the chapter:

"You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon."

The five zones, frozen:

"The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend."

### Slide 4

**Four of the six passes are quick. Then you slow right down for Shine, which is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean.**

Sort each zone first, because you cannot clean a surface that is covered: empty it, and let nothing back without a verdict. Straighten what survives into a home chosen by use: the tray where your hand opens, hooks for daily coats, the lowest hook at the shortest person's height. Those two clear the ground so the clean can happen. Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short: act on what the cleaning turned up, photograph and name and tape the result, then hang the upkeep on things that already happen.

That is the frame. The heart of it is Shine, and Shine is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room in this book.

The book line, verbatim: "Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method."

### Slide 5

**Learn one method once and use it in every zone and every room: the nine rules of Shine. This is the slide to photograph.**

Every step-by-step procedure in this chapter is just these nine rules applied to a particular zone. Learn them here and you will use them in all nineteen chapters that follow.

The nine rules, frozen, in order:

- work top to bottom
- work dry before wet
- work back to front, clean to dirty
- mist the cloth, not the surface
- use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry
- colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener
- match the cleaner to the surface
- give the cleaner a moment to work
- clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix

The Shine method, frozen:

"Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way."

### Slide 6

**The method on the left, the kit on the right. Every zone is the same nine rules applied to a particular surface, with the same handful of inputs. Types, not brands. Buy for the job.**

Gather the kit into your caddy before you start, so you are not walking off mid-clean to fetch a cloth. Cleaners: a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for everyday sealed surfaces, a streak-free glass and mirror cleaner for glass, and a floor cleaner matched to your actual floor material. Cloths: a colour-coded microfiber set, one colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray. Tools: a compact vacuum with crevice and soft-brush attachments, a small soft detail brush, a stiff dry brush for the boot tray, and a portable caddy.

6S Tip, verbatim: "Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead."

### Slide 7

**Now the core: each zone gets the same three things. The inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect as you go.**

Zone 1, the landing spot, the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot, about twenty minutes. Inputs: glass and mirror cleaner, multi-surface cleaner, floor cleaner, a colour-coded cloth set, a soft detail brush, a vacuum with the crevice tool, the caddy. Steps, in order: clear and unplug; dust high and dry, first; do the mirror; wipe the wall and switch plate; clean the console top back to front; wash the tray; clean the dock and cable dry only; then the floor, last.

Zone 2, the coats. Steps, working from the shelf down: take the bin down and empty it; dust the shelf then wipe it; clean the bin inside and out; brush the coats' shoulders downward; wipe the rail and hooks and dry them; wipe the wall behind; empty and scrub the umbrella stand; then the floor, last.

Quick Win, verbatim: "Do one zone, start to finish, to the method: gather the inputs into your caddy, clear the surface, then work top to bottom, dry before wet, clean cloth then dry cloth, and finish with the floor. The landing spot takes about twenty minutes and is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot. You will feel the difference the next time you walk in."

### Slide 8

**The same method, three more zones. The boot tray takes longer than you expect, and it is the whole point. Honestly, the mat is most of the job.**

Zone 3, the shoes and boots: clear the rack and knock off the mud outside; wipe the shoes you keep; wipe the rack shelves front to back; take the boot tray outside and wash it, scrubbing the salt and grit from the corners; wipe the wall and skirting; vacuum under the rack; then the clear floor strip, last. The salt and grit in that tray is exactly what scratches every floor in the house.

Zone 4, the bench or console, top down and back to front: clear the top and take the drawer out; wipe the wall and back edge; clean the top; do the seat, vacuuming its seams for outdoor grit; clean the drawer; wipe the runners and vacuum the cavity; wipe the fronts and legs; then the floor, last.

Zone 5, the door, mat, and immediate floor: dust the top rail; wipe the door face top to bottom; wipe the frame; clean the handle, letterbox, and glass; clean the bottom edge and threshold; vacuum, beat, and wash the inside mat; shake out the outside mat; then the floor inside the swing, last.

### Slide 9

**While your hand is on every surface, your eye is inspecting it. When you find the frayed cord or the loose bracket, you flag it and keep cleaning. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it.**

Cleaning is how you inspect, the oldest idea in the book. Each zone's inspect-and-flag list is exactly the set of things your hands are already near: the brown water ring under the hooks, the cracked cable or warm plug, the console that rocks, the hook pulling from the wall, the split boot tray, the curled mat, the dropped hinge, the soft threshold where rain keeps sitting. You note each one and keep going. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair.

Then run the five safety questions once, and act on what the cleaning flagged. Water and electricity: the charging dock out of the umbrella's drip line. Burn or fire: one dock, one outlet, nothing chained. Poison, choke, or strangle: straps high, loose button batteries into a lidded tin. Fall, cut, or crush: anchor the tippy console or top-heavy rack. And the plain fall: the stride of floor inside the door clear, and the mat flat and gripped.

The book line, verbatim: "Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it."

Skipping the dry-dust-first step so you smear grit into mud, spraying cleaner straight onto a mirror so it runs behind the frame, leaving a wet floor to streak and slip, using one grey cloth for everything so you move dirt from the boot tray to the console. ### Slide 10

**Cleaning to a method makes the room shine. Three genuinely hard calls decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and a playbook names them.**

The first is the paper on the landing spot: every sheet gets a verdict, and there are only three, act, file, or recycle. A sheet past fourteen days has become a decision, so do it tonight or recycle it. Both are answers; leaving it on the surface is not.

The count-by-weather reframe, frozen:

"Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm."

The worn-mat reframe, frozen:

"A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen."
