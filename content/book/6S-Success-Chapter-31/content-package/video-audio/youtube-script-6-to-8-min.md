## SECTION 1: FIVE ZONES, NOT ONE ROOM

[HOST]
Here is why the entryway feels like too much to face: you are looking at it as one big job. It is not.

[VISUAL: The whole entryway drawn as an overhead plan with its five zones marked and gently shaded, each in its own accent. Zone 1, the landing spot, a tray on a console. Zone 2, the coats, a rail with hooks. Zone 3, the shoes, a low rack and a boot tray. Zone 4, the bench or console, the seat and the drawer. Zone 5, the door, mat, and immediate floor, the door drawn open to show the swing. A foot line reads "one room, five small jobs".]

[HOST, voice over]
It is a small collection of separate zones that share a name and a bit of floor, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones. That turns an overwhelming room into a short list of finishable jobs. You never clear "the entryway", a thing so vague you could work at it for hours and not say you were done. You clear the shoe zone, which has an obvious edge and a clear finish.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next.]

[HOST, on camera]
Here is the rule that carries the whole thing. You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon. So here is the map. The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Sort each zone first, because you cannot clean a surface that is covered, straighten what survives into a home chosen by use, and then we slow right down for the part that matters most: Shine.

## SECTION 2: THE SHINE METHOD

[HOST]
Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. So here is one, and it is the same in every zone and every room.

[VISUAL: The method drawn as an order-of-work arrow down one wall of a room: dust dry and high at the top, then glass, then surfaces (mist the cloth, clean then dry), then the floor at the foot. Small icons for the two-cloth technique, one damp and one dry, and three colour-coded cloths for glass, surfaces, and floor. A foot line reads "top to bottom, dry before wet, mist the cloth, clean then dry".]

[HOST, voice over]
Here it is, frozen: Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. Nine rules hold it up. Work top to bottom, so dust falls onto what you have not cleaned yet. Dry before wet, because a damp cloth over dry grit makes mud. Back to front, clean to dirty. Mist the cloth, not the surface, so nothing runs behind a frame. Two cloths, one to clean and one to dry, because a wet floor at a doorway is a fall waiting to happen. Colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener, which clogs the fibres and kills their grab. Match the cleaner to the surface. Give it a moment to work. And clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Shine finds the hazard. The safety pass fixes it.]

[HOST, on camera]
That last rule is the oldest idea in the book. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the frayed cord, the loose bracket, the soft patch of rot, you note it and keep cleaning. You do not stop your clean to chase a repair. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Every zone procedure that follows is just these nine rules applied to a particular surface.

## SECTION 3: ZONE BY ZONE, EXACTLY HOW TO CLEAN IT

[HOST]
So let me show you the method cleaning a real zone, the landing spot, and then take you fast through the rest.

[VISUAL: The landing spot cleaned in numbered sequence, 1 to 8. A caddy set down first with its inputs as labelled icons: glass cleaner, multi-surface cleaner, floor cleaner, a colour-coded microfiber set, a soft detail brush, a vacuum with the crevice tool. Then the tray lifted out, the dock unplugged, the glass cloth misted not the mirror, the console top wiped back to front, the tray brushed in the corners, the dock wiped dry, the floor vacuumed and mopped last.]

[HOST, voice over]
Gather the inputs into a caddy first, so you never walk off mid-clean: a glass cleaner, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a floor cleaner matched to your floor, a colour-coded microfiber set, a soft detail brush, and a vacuum with the crevice tool. Then, in order. One, clear and unplug, and unplug the charging dock before any damp cloth comes near it. Two, dust high and dry first. Three, the mirror: mist the glass cloth, never the glass, wipe one way, buff dry in the crossing direction. Four, the wall and switch plate. Five, the console top, back edge toward you, give a sticky ring a few seconds, then dry it. Six, wash the tray, detail brush into the corners. Seven, the dock and cable, dry only. Eight, the floor last: vacuum, then mop, and let it dry.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Gather the inputs. Clear. Dust dry. Glass. Surfaces. Details. Floor last.]

[HOST, on camera]
And the other four run the same way. The coats, from the shelf down, and dry the rail so nothing rusts. The shoes, where you take the salt-caked boot tray outside and hose it, scrub the salt from the corners, and dry it fully, because that grit is exactly what scratches every floor in the house. The bench, with the drawer pulled fully out and the seat vacuumed before you wipe it. And the door, where the mat is most of the job: vacuum both faces, beat it, wash it, and return it only bone dry, or it just holds water against the floor.

## SECTION 4: THE THREE CALLS, AND THE HAZARDS YOU FLAGGED

[HOST]
Cleaning to a method makes the room shine, but three hard calls decide whether it stays clean, and while you cleaned, you were flagging hazards to fix now.

[VISUAL: Three quick beats, the paper stack with its three-way verdict and a sheet passing fourteen days, the guilty coat on its two-week trial hook, the mat failing its test on a gritty day with grit riding straight over flattened fibres. Then the safety pass acting on what the clean flagged: the frayed cable replaced, the overloaded strip taken out of the drip line, the tippy console anchored.]

[HOST, voice over]
The paper gets a verdict, act, file, or recycle, and a sheet past fourteen days is a decision, so do it tonight or recycle it. The coat is counted by weather, not by number; the expensive one you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm. And the mat you test, you do not just look at it: a mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, and a worn one is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen. And every hazard your clean turned up, the frayed cable, the overloaded strip in the drip line, the rocking console, this is where the safety pass fixes it.

[ON SCREEN TEXT: Count coats by weather. Test the mat, do not look at it.]

[HOST, on camera]
Run the five safety questions once directly so nothing is missed. Water and electricity: keep the dock out of the drip line. Burn or fire: one dock, one outlet, nothing chained. Choke or strangle: straps up high, batteries in a lidded tin. Tip-over: anchor the narrow console or top-heavy rack. And the plain fall: keep the stride of floor clear and grip the mat flat. Shine found them; the safety pass fixes them.
