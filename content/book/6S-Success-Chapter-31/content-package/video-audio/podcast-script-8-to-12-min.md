## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: FIVE ZONES, NOT ONE ROOM

First, why the whole entryway feels like too much to face on a Saturday: you are looking at it as one big undifferentiated job. It is not. Like every room in this part of the book, it is a small collection of separate zones that share a name and a bit of floor, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones. Here is the rule, frozen. You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.

Applied to the door, here is the map, also frozen. The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend. You can run them in any order, but within each one the loop runs in the same fixed sequence, and the deep, detailed part of it, the part this episode is really about, is Shine.

Now, four of the other passes are quick, so let me move through them fast. Sort each zone first, because you cannot clean a surface that is covered: empty it, and let nothing back without a verdict. Straighten what survives into a home chosen by use: the tray where your hand opens, hooks for daily coats, the lowest hook at the shortest person's height, boots in the tray. And Safety, Standardize, and Sustain come after the shine, and each is short. That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: THE SHINE METHOD

Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room in the book. Learn it once and you use it everywhere. Here is the method, frozen. Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way.

Let me give you the nine rules that method rests on, because every zone procedure is just these applied to a particular surface. Work top to bottom: always clean the highest surface first and the floor last, so the dust and drips you release fall onto what you have not cleaned yet. Work dry before wet: dust or vacuum the loose grit off before you put anything wet on it, because a damp cloth dragged over dry grit makes mud and scratches the finish. Work back to front, clean to dirty: start at the back edge against the wall and pull toward yourself, least dirty surface to dirtiest, so you never drag grime across ground you have already cleaned. Mist the cloth, not the surface: spray the cleaner onto the cloth, never onto the wall or glass or electronics, so nothing runs down behind a frame. Use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry, because a surface left wet air-dries to streaks, and at a doorway a wet floor is a fall waiting to happen. Colour-code your cloths, and never wash them with fabric softener, which clogs the fibres and kills their grab, so keep one colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray. Match the cleaner to the surface: a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for everyday sealed surfaces, a streak-free glass cleaner for glass and mirror, a floor cleaner matched to your actual floor. Give the cleaner a moment to work: let it sit a few seconds on a sticky mark rather than scrubbing it dry. And the ninth, the oldest idea in the book: clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too, and when you find the frayed cord or the loose bracket or the soft patch of rot, you note it and keep cleaning. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair.

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: ZONE BY ZONE, EXACTLY HOW TO CLEAN IT

Now the centerpiece. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather into your caddy, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Let me walk the landing spot in full, then take you briskly through the other four.

Zone one, the landing spot: the tray, the console top, the mirror, the wall and switch plate, the charging dock, and the floor. Your inputs: a glass and mirror cleaner, a neutral pH multi-surface cleaner, a floor cleaner matched to your floor, a colour-coded microfiber set, a soft detail brush, a vacuum with the crevice tool, and your caddy. The steps, in order. One, clear and unplug: everything off, the tray lifted right out, and the charging dock unplugged before any damp cloth comes near it. Two, dust high and dry first, the mirror top and the console's back edge. Three, the mirror: mist the glass cloth, never the glass, wipe one way, then buff dry in the crossing direction so a missed streak shows against the light. Four, the wall and switch plate, barely damp, gently on painted plaster. Five, the console top, working from the back edge toward you, giving a sticky ring a few seconds before you dry it. Six, wash the tray, detail brush into the corners where key grit and coin dust pack in, and dry it fully before the keys go back. Seven, the dock and cable, dry only, still unplugged, a dry brush through the port to lift lint. Eight, the floor last: vacuum the grit with the crevice tool, then mop and let it dry.

Now the other four, same shape, and I will flag the one step in each that people miss. Zone two, the coats: work from the shelf down. Take the bin down and empty it, dust the shelf then wipe it, clean the bin and dry it fully before the wool goes back, brush the coat shoulders, wipe the rail and hooks, and here is the missed step, dry the rail so nothing rusts where the metal was wet. Empty and scrub the umbrella stand, and the floor last.

Zone three, the shoes, and the boot tray is the whole point, because the salt and grit in it is exactly what scratches every floor in the house. Add a stiff dry brush and access to an outside tap to your inputs. Knock the mud off outside, wipe the shoes you keep, wipe the shelves front to back and dry them, and then the big one: take the boot tray outside, hose it, scrub the salt film out of the corners, rinse it, and dry it fully before it comes back, because a wet tray just re-wets the floor. Wipe the skirting, vacuum the grit trap under the rack, and mop the clear floor strip last.

Zone four, the bench or console: work top down, back to front, and the key move is the drawer. Clear the top and pull the drawer right out of its runners. Wipe the wall and back edge, clean the top, then do the seat, vacuum its seams with the upholstery tool first because people sit on it in outdoor clothes. Clean the drawer, tipping the sand from the back corners and brushing them out, wipe the runners and vacuum the empty cavity, wipe the fronts and legs and the kick line, and the floor last.

Zone five, the door, mat, and immediate floor, and honestly the mat is most of the job, because it is the one thing standing between the outside and every other floor in the house. Dust the top rail and clear the corners, wipe the door face top to bottom, the frame and architrave, the handle and letterbox and any glass panel. Run a damp cloth along the bottom edge and threshold to lift the dark grit line the mat never reaches. Then the mat: vacuum the top face, flip it and vacuum the underside where grit sifts through, beat it outdoors, wash it if it is washable, and return it only once it is bone dry, or it just holds water against the floor. Shake out the outside mat, and mop the floor inside the swing last, with both mats up.

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: THE THREE CALLS, AND THE HAZARDS YOU FLAGGED

Cleaning to a method will make the room shine, but three genuinely hard calls decide whether it stays clean. The first is the paper on the landing spot: nothing returns without a verdict, and there are only three, act, file, or recycle. A sheet that has sat more than fourteen days has become a decision, so do it tonight or recycle it. The second is the coat you feel guilty about, and here is the reframe, frozen. Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm. And the third is the mat, frozen. A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen. So walk in on a gritty day, lift it, and if the grit is riding straight over it, replace it this month.

And remember, all through that clean you were flagging hazards, not fixing them. This is where the safety pass acts on them: the frayed charging cable, the overloaded strip in the umbrella's drip line, the tippy console, the hook pulling out of the wall, the rot flagged at the threshold. Run the five safety questions once directly so nothing is missed, and act here on everything the clean turned up. Shine found it; the safety pass fixes it.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So here is your one takeaway, frozen. A zone is not clean because it looks clean. It is clean because you gathered the right inputs, took it apart, and cleaned every surface to a method, top to bottom, dry before wet, one cloth to clean and one to dry, inspecting as you went. Do that to one zone this weekend, and let the door start working for you every time you cross it.

And here is your assignment, the smallest possible proof of the whole idea. Gather the inputs into a caddy and clean one zone, start to finish, to the method. Start with the landing spot: about twenty minutes, and the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot. Take the before photo first, from just inside the door, because you will want the proof. Then work top to bottom, dry before wet, clean cloth then dry cloth, and finish with the floor. You will feel the difference the next time you walk in.
