## NEW Part 9 format (this chapter sets the template for Ch 31 to 50)

Part 9 chapters are PLAYBOOKS, not method chapters. They do not re-teach the six S's; they apply the loop the reader now owns to one room, decomposed into that room's micro-zones. **THE CORE OF EVERY PART 9 CHAPTER IS THE DETAILED, STEP-BY-STEP CLEAN-AND-SHINE INSTRUCTION FOR EACH MICRO-ZONE, WITH THE EXACT INPUTS (Phil's directive, 2026-07-22): the instructions and inputs are the most important aspect of the room chapters.** Key format decisions established here:

- **Instruction-centric structure:** open the room → the room as a handful of zones → the OTHER passes (Sort/Straighten/Safety/Standardize/Sustain) in brief → **the universal Shine method (nine best-practice rules)** → **ZONE BY ZONE: EXACTLY HOW TO CLEAN IT** (the centerpiece: for each zone, an INPUTS list, NUMBERED step-by-step procedure, and an inspect-and-flag list) → the calls that matter → watch for (five safety questions) → before-and-after.
- **Each zone's clean/shine block has three parts:** (1) INPUTS, the exact cleaners, cloths, and tools to gather; (2) STEPS, numbered in the true order of work (clear, dry-dust high, glass, surfaces, details, floor last), world-class technique; (3) INSPECT AND FLAG, the notice-and-flag list. Sourced from the Micro Zone Manual's per-zone shine_detail (surfaces + methods + products_used + inspect_as_you_clean), ELEVATED into professional numbered procedures.
- **The universal Shine method** is stated once (nine rules: top to bottom; dry before wet; back to front / clean to dirty; mist the cloth not the surface; two cloths, clean then dry; colour-coded cloths, no fabric softener; match cleaner to surface; give dwell time; clean to inspect and FLAG not fix). It governs every zone procedure and carries across all 20 rooms. CRITICAL: Shine NOTICES and FLAGS hazards (frayed cords, loose brackets, rot, rust); FIXING belongs to the Safety pass (the Manual's enforced rule; Ch 16 clean-to-inspect).
- **NEW recurring signature replaces the friction meter.** The friction meter reached its goal at Chapter 30, so Part 9 retires it and closes each room chapter with a BEFORE-AND-AFTER room card plus the two photos to take (the before to keep, the after as the standard).
- **Product types are woven in** as the per-zone INPUTS and in the Shine-method-and-kit figure, drawn from the 7-family Master Product Library (types only, never brand names). The exhaustive product list is a separate appendix.
- **Cross-references** point back to the method chapters (esp. Ch 16 to 19 Shine); the room's own zone-level clean/shine detail is the new material.
- Standard book apparatus stays: drop-cap opener, What You Will Learn, four callouts, One Idea to Keep box, exactly 4 visual blocks, the full 56-file content package, honest review, Desktop copy. **Length is NOT capped at 3,600 to 3,900 words: room chapters run long (~5,000 to 6,500 prose words) because the per-zone instructions are the point. Completeness of the clean/shine instruction beats brevity.**

## The frozen lines (copy verbatim in manuscript and HTML)

- **The rule (frozen):** You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.

- **The five zones (frozen, structural list):** The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend.

- **Definition (frozen, defbox):** A finished entryway is one where everyone can land and launch in seconds: keys and phone drop into one tray, today's coat and shoes have an easy home, wet things stay on the mat, and the paper that comes through the door leaves again with a verdict. Nothing lives on the floor inside the door's swing, and two hooks always stand empty for whoever arrives next.

- **The Shine method (frozen, pull-quote):** Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way.

- **The nine Shine rules (frozen list, govern every zone procedure):** work top to bottom; work dry before wet; work back to front, clean to dirty; mist the cloth, not the surface; use two cloths, one to clean and one to dry; colour-code your cloths and never use fabric softener; match the cleaner to the surface; give the cleaner a moment to work; clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix (Shine finds hazards, the safety pass fixes them).

- **The first-and-last reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** The entryway is the room you spend the least time in and notice the most, because you cross it every single time you come or go. A calm doorway is the first thing that greets you home and the last thing that speeds you out, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own.

- **The count-by-weather reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm.

- **The worn-mat reframe (frozen, pull-quote):** A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen.

- **The before-and-after caption (frozen, governs the signature figure):** This is the entryway the whole loop was for: the same doorway you started with, cleared, calmed, and holding only what the door actually needs. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, because within a month you will not believe the room was ever the other one, and take the after photo as the standard, because it is the picture the door has to match from now on.

- **One Idea to Keep (frozen, close box):** A zone is not clean because it looks clean. It is clean because you gathered the right inputs, took it apart, and cleaned every surface to a method, top to bottom, dry before wet, one cloth to clean and one to dry, inspecting as you went. Do that to one zone this weekend, and let the door start working for you every time you cross it.

- **Outcome vocabulary (frozen):** one zone at a time / count by weather, not by number / land and launch in seconds. Part 9 applies the loop you built in Parts 1 to 8; it does not teach it again.

## The four callouts (frozen)

- **Quick Win:** Give the landing spot twenty minutes. Empty the tray and the paper stack onto the floor, let back only keys, sunglasses, one wallet and one phone per adult, and one upright folder of act-on paper, and wipe the bare surface. That one zone is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot.
- **Family Challenge:** Everyone claims their own named shoe shelf and their own hook, with the lowest hook set at the shortest person's height, and two hooks are left empty for whoever visits. A third pair or a stray coat then has visibly nowhere to go, and nobody has to nag, because the empty hook does the asking.
- **6S Tip:** Take the two photos. Shoot the before from just inside the door before you start, and keep it. When the room is done, photograph the cleared landing spot and tape that picture inside the coat cupboard at the height people glance while hanging a coat. The after photo is the standard, and the door then teaches itself.
- **Common Mistake:** Trying to do the whole entryway in one exhausting go, then stalling halfway with everything worse than when you started. The room is five zones, and one finished zone beats five half-done ones. The other classic error is letting the console drawer quietly become the second junk drawer: it serves the door and nothing else.

## The room: source facts (from the Micro Zone Manual, Entryway, 5 zones)

The entryway is five zones. Carry the specific nouns; never write a generic sentence that would fit any room.

1. **Landing spot (the tray and the paper).** Job: where pockets empty coming in and refill going out. Done: one tray for keys and sunglasses, one wallet and one phone per adult, a single upright folder of fewer than ten sheets, bare surface both sides. Signature call: the paper with no verdict (act, file, or recycle; a sheet older than fourteen days is a decision, not paperwork). Hazards: charging cable in the umbrella drip line (water and electricity); a dock, lamp and heater chained on one strip (burn or fire); a work bag on the floor (fall). Trigger: coat off, hands already full and stopped, everything goes in the tray.
2. **Coats and outerwear.** Job: hold the outerwear you will actually wear this week. Done: one coat per person on the rail, one bag, hats and gloves in one labeled bin above, umbrellas in the stand, at least two empty hooks. Signature call: the coat you feel guilty about (count by weather, not number; give the expensive-never-worn coat one two-week trial on the front hook). Hazards: heavy bins stored above head height (fall, cut, or crush); an overloaded rail sagging (fall); long scarves and straps at child neck height (poison, choke, or strangle); hooks in unanchored plasterboard. Trigger: first cold morning you go looking for gloves is the seasonal swap.
3. **Shoes and boots.** Job: hold footwear that comes off at the door and keep its grit off every other floor. Done: two pairs per person on the rack soles down, boots in the tray, one pair of slippers each, a clear stride-wide stretch of floor between door and rack. Signature call: the worn-out pair kept "for the garden" (the sole decides, not the memory; one dirty-job pair per person, stored where the dirty jobs happen). Hazards: wet soles on hard floor (fall); solvents (polish, waterproofing spray) at toddler height (poison, choke, or strangle); a top-heavy rack a child climbs (fall, cut, or crush). Trigger: shoes off and onto the rack before the coat comes off, while hands are free.
4. **Console or bench (the seat and the drawer).** Job: somewhere to sit to lace a boot and set a bag down for thirty seconds. Done: three items or fewer on top, an empty seat, a drawer where every divided section holds one named category. Signature call: what the drawer is for (it serves the door only; one test, would you need this while wearing your shoes). Hazards: a tippy narrow console a child climbs (fall, cut, or crush); loose button batteries, coins, magnets in the drawer at toddler height (poison, choke, or strangle). Trigger: clearing the dinner table, take the top back down to three on the way past.
5. **Door, mat, and immediate floor.** Job: catch grit, water and road salt at the threshold so none of it reaches the rest of the floors. Done: coarse mat outside, absorbent mat inside, both dead flat, nothing on the floor inside the door swing, a door that latches on the first push. Signature call: when the mat is finished (test it, do not look at it). Hazards: a curled or sliding mat (fall); a loose hinge or failing closer catching fingers (fall, cut, or crush). Trigger: last one home straightens the mat with a foot before turning the key.
