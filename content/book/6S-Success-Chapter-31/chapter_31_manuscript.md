## Chapter Opening

Welcome to the part of the book where the teaching stops and the doing begins. Behind you are all six S's and the loop that keeps them turning. You do not need another lesson. What you need now is somewhere to point what you have learned, and a set of instructions precise enough to follow with your sleeves rolled up.

So the rest of this book is a set of playbooks, one room at a time, and the heart of each one is the same promise: for every small zone of the room, you get the exact inputs to gather and the exact, ordered, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, done the way a professional would do it rather than the way most of us wipe at a surface until it looks a bit better. That distinction is the whole game. A tidy room and a genuinely clean room are not the same thing, and the difference is method: the order you work in, the cloth you use, the cleaner you match to the surface, and the fact that while your hand is on every surface, your eye is inspecting it.

We begin at the door, and not by accident.

> The entryway is the room you spend the least time in and notice the most, because you cross it every single time you come or go. A calm doorway is the first thing that greets you home and the last thing that speeds you out, which is why the smallest room in the house pays back a 6S loop faster than any other room you own.

It is also the dirtiest room in the house for its size, because it is where the outside comes in: grit, road salt, rain, and everything that rides in on shoes and sleeves. That is exactly why the clean-and-shine step matters most here, and why this chapter spends most of its length on precisely how to do it. By the end you will be able to take the entryway apart zone by zone and put it back genuinely clean, and you will know what you are holding in your hand at every step.

## Five Zones, Not One Room

The reason a whole entryway feels like too much to face is that you are looking at it as one big job. It is not. Like every room in this part of the book, it is a small collection of separate zones that share a name and a bit of floor, and the trick to all of Part 9 is to stop seeing the room and start seeing the zones.

> You do not 6S a whole room. You 6S one zone of it, then the next, until the room is done. A room is only ever a handful of small zones wearing a single name, and a zone is a job you can finish in an afternoon.

> The entryway is five small zones, not one big job: the landing spot where pockets empty, the coats, the shoes, the bench or console, and the door with its mat and the strip of floor inside its swing. Do them one at a time and the room takes a few short sessions, not a lost weekend.

## Which Door Is This

One thing to settle before you start, because it decides what belongs in here and what does not. The entryway is the room with the front door in it. That is the whole definition, and it carries further than it sounds.

The front door is the public threshold. It is where a guest arrives and waits to be let in, where the post lands, where a parcel gets left, and where you leave from when you are going somewhere on purpose. It is also, in most homes, the only threshold a visitor ever sees. That single fact shapes every call in this chapter, because the entryway is judged twice over: on how fast it launches you, and on how it looks to somebody standing in it.

Many homes have a second way in. A side door, a back door, a door in from the garage, a door onto a utility passage. That second threshold is the mudroom, and it has a chapter of its own later in this part of the book. If your home has one, the division of labour between them is simple, and settling it now will save you a great deal of arguing with your own hallway.

> The entryway is the room with the front door in it. The mudroom is the room with the other door in it. That one distinction settles almost every argument about which of them a thing belongs to.

The front door keeps the launch pad: keys, the bag you leave with, today's outgoing post, and the coat you are actually wearing this week. The other door takes the working end of family life: the wet boots, the dog, the seasonal gear, the cleaning kit, and everything that arrives muddy. Send the wet and the seasonal down there and this room stops fighting you, because most of what clogs a front hall was never front-hall work in the first place.

And if your home has only a front door, then this room is quietly doing both jobs at once. Read this chapter, then read the threshold system in the mudroom chapter, because a one-door home needs the grit stopped just as hard and has nowhere else to stop it.

## What This Room Alone Must Do

Every zone here except one is shared with some other threshold. Coats hang in more than one place, boots come off in more than one place, and a bench is a bench. The landing spot is the exception, and it is the reason this chapter exists.

No other room in this book handles the paper that arrives through a door with a decision attached to it. The post, the form, the slip that needs signing tonight, the card that needs a reply. That is the entryway's own work, nobody else does it, and it is the thing that goes wrong first. So if you only ever fix one zone in this room, fix the landing spot, because a launch pad that is buried in paper has stopped being a launch pad and become a filing problem standing between you and the door.

That list is the map for everything that follows. You can run the zones in any order, but within each one the loop runs in the same fixed sequence, and the deep, detailed part of it, the part this chapter is really about, is Shine. Here is what you are working toward across all five.

> A finished entryway is one where everyone can land and launch in seconds: keys and phone drop into one tray, today's coat and shoes have an easy home, wet things stay on the mat, and the paper that comes through the door leaves again with a verdict. Nothing lives on the floor inside the door's swing, and two hooks always stand empty for whoever arrives next.

## The Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

Before the deep clean, four of the other five passes are quick, so we will move through them briskly and then slow right down for Shine, which is where the real work is.

**Sort** each zone first, because you cannot clean a surface that is covered. Empty it, and let nothing back without a verdict: keys and today's coat and this week's shoes stay; the dead batteries, the odd gloves, the paper with no purpose, the split-soled trainers go to the out box. **Straighten** what survives into a home chosen by use: the tray where your hand opens, hooks for daily coats, the lowest hook at the shortest person's height, boots in the tray, two mats and no more. Those two passes clear the ground so the clean can happen.

**Safety, Standardize, and Sustain** come after the shine, and each is short. Safety is where you act on everything your cleaning turned up, anchoring the tippy console, taking the overloaded power strip out of the umbrella's drip line, replacing the frayed cable you flagged. Standardize locks the result: photograph the finished landing spot and tape it inside the coat cupboard, name each person's shoe shelf, tape the outline where the mat sits. And Sustain hangs the upkeep on things that already happen: coat off, hands full, everything into the tray; dishwasher on, console back to three; bin night, the boot tray goes out.

That is the frame. Now the heart of it.

## How to Shine the Entryway: The Method

Shine is where a room stops being tidy and becomes clean, and it is the pass most people do worst, because they were never taught a method. There is one, it is not complicated, and it is the same in every zone and every room in this book. Learn it once here and you will use it in all nineteen chapters that follow.

> Shine is cleaning to a method, not wiping until it looks better: work top to bottom and dry before wet, mist the cloth and not the surface, clean with one cloth and dry with a second, and let your eye inspect every surface your hand passes over. You leave the zone genuinely clean, and you find what needs fixing on the way.

Here are the nine rules that method rests on. Every step-by-step procedure below is just these rules applied to a particular zone.

- **Work top to bottom.** Always clean the highest surface first and the floor last, so the dust and drips you release fall onto what you have not cleaned yet, never onto what you have.
- **Work dry before wet.** Dust or vacuum the loose grit off a surface before you put anything wet on it. A damp cloth dragged over dry grit makes mud and scratches the finish.
- **Work back to front, clean to dirty.** Start at the back edge against the wall and pull toward yourself, and start with the least dirty surface and move to the dirtiest, so you never drag grime across ground you have already cleaned or walk over a wet floor.
- **Mist the cloth, not the surface.** Spray the cleaner onto the cloth, not onto the wall, glass, or electronics. You control how much you use, nothing runs down behind a frame, and no overspray lands where it should not.
- **Use two cloths: one to clean, one to dry.** A surface left wet air-dries to streaks, and at a doorway a wet floor is a fall waiting to happen. Clean with the damp cloth, follow immediately with a dry one.
- **Colour-code your cloths.** Keep a dedicated cloth colour for glass, another for general surfaces, another for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Wash them by colour, and never with fabric softener, which clogs the fibres and kills their grab.
- **Match the cleaner to the surface.** A neutral pH multi-surface cleaner for everyday sealed surfaces, a streak-free glass cleaner for glass and mirror, and a floor cleaner matched to your actual floor material. Never a harsh all-purpose spray on a surface it can strip or etch.
- **Give the cleaner a moment to work.** Let it sit a few seconds on a sticky mark rather than scrubbing it dry. The chemistry lifts the soil so you do not have to grind at the finish.
- **Clean to inspect, and flag, do not fix.** This is the oldest idea in the book, from the very first Shine chapter: cleaning is how you inspect. Your hand passes over every inch of a surface, so your eye should too. When you find the thing you never normally see, a frayed cord, a loose bracket, a soft patch of rot, you note it or flag it and keep cleaning. Shine finds it; the safety pass fixes it. Do not stop your clean to chase a repair.

## Zone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Here is the core of the chapter. Each zone gets the same three things: the inputs to gather, the numbered steps in the order you actually do them, and what to inspect and flag while your hands are already there. Gather the inputs into your caddy before you start, so you are not walking off mid-clean to fetch a cloth.

### Zone 1 · The Landing Spot

The tray, the console top, the mirror or art above it, the wall and switch plate beside the door, the charging dock, and the floor beneath. Keys shed grit and coins leave a grey film, so the film under the tray tells you exactly how long it has been since anyone did this properly.

**Inputs:**

- Streak-free glass and mirror cleaner
- Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
- Floor-material-compatible cleaner, matched to your floor
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (one glass cloth, one surface cloth, one dry buffing cloth, one floor cloth)
- A small soft detail brush
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool fitted
- Your portable caddy to carry it all

**Steps:**

1. Clear and unplug. Take everything off the console, lift the tray right out, remove the paper folder, and unplug the charging dock at the wall before any damp cloth comes near it. A clear surface is the only one you can truly clean.
2. Dust high and dry, first. Run the dry buffing cloth along the top edge of the mirror or picture frame and across the console's back edge where dust packs into the seam against the wall. Dry first, always: a wet cloth over dry dust just makes grime you then have to chase.
3. Do the mirror or glass. Mist the streak-free glass cleaner onto your glass cloth, never straight onto the glass, so it cannot run down behind the frame. Wipe the whole pane in one direction, then buff it dry with the dry cloth in the crossing direction, so any streak you missed shows up against the light.
4. Wipe the wall and switch plate. With the surface cloth barely damp with multi-surface cleaner, lift the finger marks that ring the light switch and the scuff where shoulders and bags brush the wall by the door. Go gently on painted plaster, and dry behind you.
5. Clean the console top. Mist multi-surface cleaner onto the surface cloth and wipe the whole top, working from the back edge against the wall toward the front. Give any sticky ring a few seconds to soften rather than scrubbing, then dry it with the buffing cloth.
6. Wash the tray. Wipe the ring of grey film off the spot where the tray stood, then wash the tray inside and out with the multi-surface cleaner, working the detail brush into the corners where key grit and coin dust pack in. Dry it fully before the keys go back.
7. Clean the dock and cable, dry only. With the dock still unplugged, wipe the body and cradle with a dry or barely damp cloth, never a wet one, and run the dry detail brush through the charging port and along the cable to lift lint. Coil the cable loosely before it goes back.
8. Do the floor, last. Vacuum the grit from under the console and around each leg with the crevice tool, reaching the corner behind the legs where a bag usually parks, then mop the sealed floor with the floor cleaner on the floor cloth and let it dry before you set anything back.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a brown water ring or spreading stain on the wall or top directly under the hooks, the sign wet sleeves have been dripping in one line; a charging cable with cracked, frayed, or hardened insulation, or a plug with a scorch mark or that sits warm in the socket; a power strip that feels warm or has yellowed around a socket; a console leg or wall bracket that rocks when you lean on the top; green or white crust on the dock's metal contacts, which is corrosion from damp. Flag each one and keep cleaning; the safety pass fixes them.

### Zone 2 · The Coats and Outerwear

The shelf above the rail, the hat and glove bin, the rail and hooks, the shoulders of the hanging coats, the wall behind the hooks, the umbrella stand, and the floor beneath. Work from the shelf down.

**Inputs:**

- Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth, a floor cloth)
- A compact vacuum with the soft upholstery brush and the crevice tool
- A small detail brush for the umbrella stand
- Your portable caddy

**Steps:**

1. Take the bin down and empty it. Bring the hat and glove bin off the shelf, empty it, and shake it out over the bin. Set the contents aside on a clean surface, not the floor.
2. Dust the shelf, then wipe it. Dust the shelf top with the dry cloth first, then wipe it with the surface cloth barely damp with multi-surface cleaner, reaching the back edge and the front lip where a grey line of dust settles unseen above eye level.
3. Clean the bin, inside and out. Wipe the inside and outside of the bin with the damp cloth and let it dry fully before the hats and gloves go back, because wool put away damp is what starts a musty smell.
4. Brush the coats. Brush each coat's shoulders and collar downward with the soft upholstery tool or a dry brush to lift settled dust and hair, starting with the pieces you actually wear this week.
5. Wipe the rail and hooks. Slide the coats along and wipe the rail its full length with the damp cloth, then wipe each hook and its base plate. Dry the rail so nothing rusts where the metal was wet.
6. Wipe the wall behind the hooks. Lift the dark scuff where shoulders and bags rub with a barely damp cloth, gently on painted plaster, and dry it after.
7. Empty and scrub the umbrella stand. Tip the stand out over a drain or outside, pour out the standing water and grit, scrub the inside with multi-surface cleaner and the detail brush, rinse it, and stand it upside down to dry before the umbrellas go back.
8. Do the floor, last. Vacuum under the hanging line and behind the umbrella stand with the crevice tool, catching the grit that drops off boots and hems, then mop the sealed floor with the floor cleaner.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a hook whose base plate is pulling away from the wall, a gap opening behind it or a screw head standing proud, before it lets go with a coat on it; a rail bracket with a screw backing out, or a rail bowing in the middle; a brown damp bloom or soft patch on the wall behind the soaked coats, where the plaster is taking on water; a musty mildew smell off a coat or out of the bin, and note which piece; rust eating the base of the umbrella stand, or a crack in it letting water onto the floor. Flag each and keep going.

### Zone 3 · The Shoes and Boots

The rack shelves, the footwear itself, the boot tray, the wall and skirting behind the rack, the floor under the rack, and the clear strip between the door and the rack. The boot tray takes longer than you expect, and it is the whole point: the salt and grit in it is exactly what scratches every floor in the house.

**Inputs:**

- Floor-material-compatible cleaner
- Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a floor cloth)
- A stiff dry brush for caked mud and the boot tray
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool
- Access to an outside tap or a hose for the boot tray
- Your portable caddy

**Steps:**

1. Clear the rack and knock off the mud. Take the footwear off, and outside, knock the soles together to shed grit and stones and brush caked mud off the welts with the stiff dry brush.
2. Wipe the shoes you keep. Wipe the uppers of the pairs that live on the rack, and give the slippers a shake and a wipe of the sole. Set them aside to go back at the end.
3. Wipe the rack shelves. Knock any loose dried mud off each shelf into the bin, then wipe each shelf with the surface cloth damp with multi-surface cleaner, front to back. Dry them so damp mud does not cake back on.
4. Take the boot tray outside and wash it. Carry it out, hose it down, then scrub the salt film and grit out of the corners with the stiff brush and multi-surface cleaner and rinse it clear. Dry it fully before it comes back in, because a wet tray just re-wets the floor.
5. Wipe the wall and skirting. Lift the mud spray and scuff off the skirting board and lower wall behind the rack with a barely damp cloth, and dry it after.
6. Vacuum under the rack. Pull the rack out or reach under it and vacuum the grit trap that builds in the dark underneath, then wipe the sealed floor there with the floor cleaner.
7. Do the clear floor strip, last. Vacuum the full stride-wide strip between the door and the rack to lift the walked-in grit, then mop it with the floor cleaner and let it dry before anyone crosses it wet.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a hairline crack or split in the boot tray letting water seep onto the floor, and note any dark stain formed underneath; a rack shelf that sags or a joint worked loose so the rack rocks; rust blooming on the metal frame where salt water pools; a dark damp stain or soft patch on the skirting behind the tray; the floor finish worn dull, grey, or rough under the tray where repeated salt has eaten the seal. Flag each.

### Zone 4 · The Bench or Console

The wall and back edge, the console top, the seat, the drawer taken fully out, the runners and cavity, the drawer fronts and legs, and the floor beneath. The seat collects the same grit as the floor, because people sit on it in outdoor clothes. Work top down, back to front.

**Inputs:**

- Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a dry cloth, a floor cloth)
- A compact vacuum with the upholstery tool and the crevice tool
- A small detail brush for the drawer corners
- Your portable caddy

**Steps:**

1. Clear the top and take the drawer out. Clear the top to three items or fewer, then pull the drawer right out of its runners and set it on a clear surface. Lift out any divider insert.
2. Wipe the wall and back edge. Clear the dust line where the top meets the wall with a barely damp cloth.
3. Clean the top. Dust or vacuum the top, then wipe the whole surface with the surface cloth damp with multi-surface cleaner, and dry it off.
4. Do the seat. Vacuum the seat and its seams with the upholstery tool to lift the outdoor grit, then wipe a hard seat with multi-surface cleaner, or spot-clean a padded one with a barely damp cloth. Let it dry before anyone sits.
5. Clean the drawer. Tip the sand and crumbs from the back corners over the bin, wipe the base and the sections with a damp cloth, and work the detail brush into the corners the cloth misses. Wipe the divider insert before it goes back.
6. Wipe the runners and vacuum the cavity. With the drawer out, wipe both runner tracks with a barely damp cloth and vacuum the empty cavity, lifting the grit that has dropped down behind the drawer.
7. Wipe the fronts and legs. Clean the drawer fronts and the console legs, getting the handles and the kick line at the bottom where shoes scuff.
8. Do the floor, last. Vacuum under and around the legs with the crevice tool, then mop the sealed floor with the floor cleaner.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a console that rocks or tips toward you when you lean on the top, the sign it is top-heavy and not yet held to the wall; a drawer runner screw backing out so the drawer drops or drags; a hairline split in the seat or a splinter lifting on a wooden bench, before someone sits on it; a wobble in a leg joint or a cracked or seized caster; a dark water ring or swelling on the top where a wet umbrella or bag keeps landing. Flag each.

### Zone 5 · The Door, Mat, and Immediate Floor

The door face and frame, the handle and letterbox and any glass panel, the bottom edge and threshold, the inside mat, the outside coarse mat, and the floor inside the swing. Honestly, the mat is most of the job, because it is the one thing standing between the outside and every other floor in the house.

**Inputs:**

- Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
- Streak-free glass and mirror cleaner, if the door has a glass panel
- Floor-material-compatible cleaner
- A colour-coded microfiber cloth set (a surface cloth, a glass cloth, a dry cloth, a floor cloth)
- A compact vacuum with the crevice tool
- Access to an outside space to beat and wash the mats
- Your portable caddy

**Steps:**

1. Dust the top rail and clear the corners. Dust the top rail of the door first, and clear any cobwebs from the top corners of the frame.
2. Wipe the door face, top to bottom. Wipe the whole inside face with the surface cloth damp with multi-surface cleaner, working down and giving the panels and the kick line at the base extra attention where shoes and bags mark it.
3. Wipe the frame and architrave. Clean the frame and architrave, working the top ledge where dust settles and the strike-side edge that hands push against.
4. Clean the handle, letterbox, and glass. Wipe the handle and the letterbox flap with multi-surface cleaner. For any glass panel, mist the glass cleaner onto the glass cloth, wipe in one direction, and buff dry.
5. Clean the bottom edge and threshold. Open the door and run a damp cloth along the very bottom edge of the door and into the threshold strip, lifting the dark grit line the mat never reaches. Dry the threshold after.
6. Vacuum, beat, and wash the inside mat. Vacuum the top face, flip it and vacuum the underside where grit sifts through, then take it outdoors, beat it against a wall or rail, and wash it if it is washable. Return it only once it is fully dry, or it just holds water against the floor.
7. Shake out the outside mat. Lift the coarse outside mat, shake or beat the grit and salt out of it, hose it if it is the rubber-backed sort, and stand it up to dry before it goes back flat.
8. Do the floor inside the swing, last. With both mats up, vacuum the whole patch inside the door swing, including where the mats sat, then mop the sealed floor with the floor cleaner and let it dry before anyone steps on it.

**Inspect and flag as you go:** a mat with a corner that has curled and stopped lying flat, or backing gone brittle and shedding crumbs, both signs the grip is failing; a hinge that has dropped so the door scrapes the frame, or a hinge screw standing loose; a swelling, dark line, or soft patch along the bottom of the door or the threshold where rain keeps sitting, the start of rot; a door that no longer latches on the first push, or a threshold seal squashed flat so draught comes under it; a water stain spreading on the floor just inside the threshold, where wet feet land before the mat. Flag each.

> **Quick Win**
> Do one zone, start to finish, to the method: gather the inputs into your caddy, clear the surface, then work top to bottom, dry before wet, clean cloth then dry cloth, and finish with the floor. The landing spot takes about twenty minutes and is the entryway's single highest-leverage square foot. You will feel the difference the next time you walk in.

> **6S Tip**
> Colour-code your cloths and never wash them with fabric softener. One colour for glass, one for surfaces, one for the floor and the boot tray, so you never carry grit or grime from a dirty surface onto a clean one. Softener coats the fibres and destroys the grab that makes microfiber work, so it wipes like a wet rag instead.

## The Calls That Matter Here

Cleaning to a method will make the room shine, but three genuinely hard decisions decide whether it stays clear enough to keep clean, and a playbook earns its keep by naming them.

The first is the paper on the landing spot. Nothing returns to the surface without a verdict, and there are only three: act, file, or recycle. A sheet that has sat more than fourteen days has stopped being paperwork and become a decision, so do it tonight or recycle it. Both are answers; leaving it on the surface is not.

The second is the coat you feel guilty about.

> Coats are counted by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat per kind of weather your climate genuinely produces, and a second in the same category has to beat the first on a specific, nameable day. The expensive coat you never wear is not exempt, because the money is already spent whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm.

The third is the mat, which wears out while looking perfectly fine.

> A mat looks perfectly fine long after it has stopped working, so you test it instead of looking at it. A finished mat sits with its fibres flattened one way and the grit rides straight over it onto the next pair of shoes. A worn mat is not saving you anything, it is quietly sanding the finish off every floor between the front door and the kitchen.

Walk in on a dry, gritty day, wipe your feet the way you actually do, then lift the mat and look at both the floor beneath it and the fibres. If the grit is sitting on top and walking straight off, replace it this month.

> **Family Challenge**
> Everyone claims their own named shoe shelf and their own hook, with the lowest hook set at the shortest person's height, and two hooks left empty for whoever visits. A third pair or a stray coat then has visibly nowhere to go, and nobody has to nag, because the empty hook does the asking.

## Watch for at the Door

Cleaning to inspect will surface most of the room's hazards for you, but run the five safety questions once directly, so nothing is missed. For **water and electricity**, keep the charging dock and any power strip out of the drip line below the wet umbrellas and sleeves. For **burn or fire**, never chain a dock, a lamp, and a plug-in heater to one strip: one dock, one outlet, nothing chained. For **poison, choke, or strangle**, keep long scarves and bag straps off the low hooks at a child's neck height, and loose button batteries, coins, and magnets out of a low drawer and into a lidded tin. For **fall, cut, or crush**, anchor a tippy console or a top-heavy shoe rack to the wall and keep the heavy things low. And for the plain **fall**, keep the stride of floor inside the door clear, and the mat flat and gripped. Everything your cleaning flagged, this is where you act on it.

> **Common Mistake**
> Wiping until it looks better instead of cleaning to a method. Skipping the dry-dust-first step so you smear grit into mud, spraying cleaner straight onto a mirror so it runs behind the frame, leaving a wet floor to streak and slip, using one grey cloth for everything so you move dirt from the boot tray to the console. The method exists because it is faster and it actually works. Follow the order.
