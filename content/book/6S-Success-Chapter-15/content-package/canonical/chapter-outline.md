# Chapter 15 Outline: The Room Map

A section-by-section map of the chapter as written in the final HTML and manuscript. This chapter is the fourth and final of Part Four (Straighten) and completes the second S. It turns from making each home explain itself to gathering the homes into zones by activity and drawing the whole arrangement into one shared picture. It does not re-choose, re-place, or re-mark homes (Chapters 12 to 14), does not clean or teach cleaning (Shine, Chapter 16), does not build routines, schedules, or standards for keeping order over time (the later Standardize S), does not address keeping it going over weeks or months (Sustain), and does not re-open Sort. It only gathers the finished homes into zones and makes the picture shared.

## 1. Chapter Opening: The view exists in exactly one place
Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home, every home sits where your hand reaches for it, and every one can explain itself now, so a friend or a guest can put a thing back without asking. Three chapters of patient work hold. And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots; a newcomer meets them the way you meet strangers at a party, one at a time. There is no single view of the space. The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer. That is the last gap in Straighten, and it is a quiet one: nothing is broken, but there is no way to see the arrangement as a whole and no way to lift that view out of your head into everyone else's. This chapter draws that view, gives it a name, and hands it around. Then the second S is done.

## 2. What You Will Learn (objectives)
- See why good, self-explaining homes can still add up to a scatter rather than a single view
- Understand what a room map is: one shared view of a space, organized into zones
- Group the homes into zones by activity, using the verb and not the noun
- Recognize the entryway you have been building as a single zone, the launch pad
- Draw a simple map of your home's zones and walk the household through it, so the map is shared

## 3. Good Homes, No Shared Picture
Gather up what the last three chapters built, because it is the ground this one stands on. Chapter 12 gave every keeper a single home; Chapter 13 placed each home by how often your hand goes there; Chapter 14 made each home speak. Three real gains, and none unwinds today. But notice the shape of all three: every one works on a home, one at a time, and that is exactly right, and it leaves a thing undone. A house is not experienced one hook at a time; you move through it in errands (leaving, making coffee, getting the kids out the door, paying a bill), and nothing so far has arranged the homes around the errands. They are arranged around themselves. So the arrangement still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects them still lives in your head. A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step. Then the fence: not re-choosing, re-placing, or re-marking homes; not cleaning; not building routines or schedules. Today has one job: gather the homes into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it.

## 4. What a Room Map Is
Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or painting; it shows you the shape of the place, so in one glance you hold the whole layout. A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home. The idea comes, like much of Straighten, from the workshop floor, where a good shop keeps an overall layout anyone can read, so a newcomer or a tired regular can see how the space flows without carrying it all in memory. Bring that home and shrink it, and you get a single view of your rooms that shows not just where each thing lives but how the space is organized.
- Illustration (the home as a handful of zones): a flattened floor plan with a few labeled activity zones blocked in (launch pad, coffee, homework, charging, mail-and-admin), calm and legible at a glance, tagged "Grouped by what you do, not by what things are."
- Definition box (frozen): A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once.
- Two words do the heavy lifting and are the rest of the chapter: shared, which comes soon, and zones, which comes next.

## 5. Zones: Group by What You Do
Here is the move that turns pieces into a picture, a small change in how you look, not a change in what sits on the shelf. Most homes, left to grow, get organized by category ("kitchen things" in the kitchen cupboard), and it quietly fails you every day, because you live by activity, not by category. Nobody thinks "time to interact with my kitchen things"; they think "I want coffee," and then need the mug, beans, grinder, and filters, which a category-minded house scatters across three cupboards and a shelf. Put the two ways side by side: by category the morning coffee lives in four places and making a cup is a small circuit of the kitchen; by activity all four sit at the machine and the circuit collapses to a reach, though nothing was bought and nothing thrown out. That one spot is a zone. Name it by the verb, not the noun: the coffee zone. Do that for the handful of things your household actually does, and the room becomes a short list of zones anyone can point to. The groups are already there; you just have not named them.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the rule): Group by what you do, not by what things are.
- Definition, woven in (frozen): A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room.
- Callout (6S Tip): Zone by the verb, not the noun. A coffee zone (mug, beans, grinder, filters, all at the machine) beats a "kitchen things" cupboard, because you live by what you are doing, not by what category a thing happens to fall in.

## 6. The Entryway Was a Zone All Along
Now the quiet reveal. That row of homes by the door, the keys, leash, bag, umbrella, outgoing mail, and shoes, was never six separate spots. It was one zone all along, the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens, at the threshold between inside and out. On the way out it hands you what you cannot leave without; on the way in it catches all of it back. We built the launch pad one keeper at a time across three chapters; naming it as one zone is the last stitch. And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them: the coffee zone at the machine, the homework corner at the end of the table, the charging station by the sofa, the mail-and-admin spot near the front hall. Five or six named zones describe almost any home; you need only the few your life actually runs on.
- Illustration (the launch pad zone): the entryway drawn as one coherent zone, all its keepers gathered inside a single labeled boundary "launch pad," with a note that this is what four chapters were quietly building.
- Callout (Quick Win): Pick one busy corner and name, in a single word, the activity that happens there. Gather everything that activity needs into that spot, and move out anything that belongs to a different activity. You just drew your first zone.

## 7. Draw It, and Share It
A zone you can see in your mind is only half the job, because the other word in the definition was shared. It takes two small acts: draw it, and walk everyone through it. Drawing it is almost absurdly simple: take a sheet of paper, sketch the rough shape of the home no straighter than a napkin diagram, and block in the zones. You are not drafting a blueprint; you are making the map in your head visible, so it can be looked at, pointed to, argued with, and improved. So do not draw it alone. Draw it with whoever lives there, or at least walk them through it, and let them push back (someone will tell you the charging station is in the wrong place, and they will be right). The sharing matters more than the drawing, so if you do only one thing, walk the household through it: stand in each zone, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told. It costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the shared-map completion): A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the reframe): The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run.
- Callout (Family Challenge): Walk the house together and give each area a one-word job: launch pad, homework, coffee, charging. Then sketch a simple map of where those zones sit. When everyone can point to the same picture, the map is shared.
- Callout (Common Mistake): Keeping the map in your own head and expecting the household to absorb it. If you never draw it and never walk everyone through it, you are still the map, and the room still stops working the day you are away.

## 9. Straighten, Complete
Stand at the door one last time and take in the four chapters together. You gave every keeper a home, placed each where your hand wants it, taught each to speak, and today gathered them into zones and drew them into one shared picture. Sort emptied the room of what did not belong; Straighten arranged what stayed until the space almost runs itself. The corner by the door that used to swallow keys and pile up mail is now a launch pad anyone can name, use, and reset without a word from you. You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters; you took a space that worked only because you were there and turned it into one that works for everyone, on its own logic, drawn where all can see it. But the room is cleared and arranged, not yet cared for: the surfaces still gather dust, and there is a kind of attention, cleaning done as noticing, that keeps a room healthy. That is the third S, Shine, and it opens the next part of the book. For now, the map is drawn, the zones are named, the picture is shared, and Straighten, the second S, is complete.
- Boxed recap (One Idea to Keep): Group the homes into a few zones by what you do, and draw the map once with everyone, so the arrangement stops living in your head and starts living in the whole household. A room whose logic is shared is a room that runs itself, which is exactly where Straighten set out to go.
