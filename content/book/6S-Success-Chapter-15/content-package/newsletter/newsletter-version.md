# Newsletter Edition: The Room Map

**Subject line (placeholder):** Group by what you do, not by what things are

**Preview text (placeholder):** The picture that ties your home together lives in one head. Let's get it on paper.

---

Hi friend,

Stand at the door one last time. Every keeper has a home now, every home sits where your hand reaches for it, and every one of them can explain itself, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. Three chapters of patient work are standing in that hallway, and all of it holds.

And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each of those is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. A newcomer meets them one at a time, in the moment they happen to need each one. The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer.

That is the last gap in Straighten, and it is a quiet one. Nothing here is broken. The homes are right, the placement is right, the cues are right. What is missing is a way to see the arrangement as a whole rather than as a heap of well-made parts, and a way to lift that view out of your head and into everyone else's. And let me say this plainly, because it matters: a map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach. It is where every well-run home sits right before this step. It is not a failing. It is simply the last thing that has not been made shareable, and making it shareable is a small, satisfying job.

So let me fence off what we are not doing, to stay on one task. We are not re-choosing homes, re-placing them, or re-marking them; those are done and they stay done. We are not cleaning anything, which is the next part of the book. We are not building routines or schedules to keep this up over the coming weeks, which is a later S entirely. Today has one job: gather the homes you already have into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it. Nothing moves house. We only step back far enough to see it all at once.

Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or every painting. It shows you the shape of the place, so in one glance you hold the whole layout and can find anything without being led by the hand. A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home.

**A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once.**

Two words in that definition do the heavy lifting. One is *zones*, which is how a scatter of separate homes becomes one readable thing. The other is *shared*, which is what finally lifts the map out of your head. Take them in turn.

Here is the move that turns pieces into a picture, and it is a small change in how you look, not a change in what sits on the shelf.

**Group by what you do, not by what things are.**

Most homes, left to grow on their own, get organized by category. All the "kitchen things" go in the kitchen cupboard. It feels tidy, and it quietly fails you every single day, because you do not live by category. You live by activity. Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, "time to interact with my kitchen things." They think, "I want coffee," and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items that a category-minded house happily scatters across three cupboards and a shelf. Group by activity and all four sit together at the machine, and the circuit collapses to a reach. Nothing was bought and nothing was thrown out; the same objects simply gathered around the thing you actually do with them.

That one spot is a zone. A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room. You name it by the verb, not the noun: not "the cupboard of cups and coffee-related objects" but simply the coffee zone. Name the activity, gather what it needs, and you have drawn a zone.

And now the quiet reveal, the one four chapters have been building toward. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. It was one zone all along. It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens. We built it one keeper at a time across three chapters. Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word. And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them: a coffee zone at the machine, a homework corner at the end of the table, a charging station by the sofa, a mail-and-admin spot near the front hall. Five or six named zones will describe almost any home.

Now the other word: shared. A zone you can see in your mind is a good start, and it is only half the job. This is where the room map stops being a private insight and becomes something the household actually holds, and it takes two small acts: draw it, and walk everyone through it. Drawing it is almost absurdly simple. Take a sheet of paper and sketch the rough shape of your home, no straighter than a napkin diagram, then block in the zones. You are not drafting a blueprint. You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes.

**A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it.**

So do not draw it alone. Draw it with whoever lives there, and let them push back. Someone will tell you the charging station is in the wrong place, and they will be right, and the map will get better. Then walk the household through it: stand in each zone, say its name out loud, let people put their hands on it. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told. And the questions that used to arrive at your elbow all day now have a place to point instead.

That shift is the real prize, and it is easy to underrate. For as long as the arrangement lived only in your head, you were on call to run it, and everyone else was a guest following rules they could feel but not see. A shared map ends that. It is the difference between a house one person maintains and a home a family keeps, and it costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table.

Then go and look at the friction meter, and take a moment with it, because this time it reaches something. Three chapters of Straighten stepped the needle toward calm. This chapter takes the last step of the four, and the needle does not just nudge; it arrives at a marked tick on the dial, the second milestone after Sort complete. Straighten is done. And, exactly as before, nothing left the house today to earn it. Not one object arrived and not one departed. The homes simply gathered into zones and the arrangement became shared, so the room's logic stopped being a private thing and became a public one.

And now the honest part, the part that makes the meter worth trusting. Reaching the Straighten mark is a genuine milestone, not the finish line. Two of the six S's are done: the room is cleared and it is fully arranged. Four remain, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain, and their job is to clean what you have, protect it, and hold it steady over time. The needle reached a real place today. It also has real distance left to travel, and both of those things are true at once.

It is worth pausing on that, quietly, before the book turns to what comes next. You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters. You took a space that worked only because you were there to work it, and you turned it into one that works for everyone, on its own logic, drawn where all can see it. That is a real thing to have made, and it deserves a moment's satisfaction.

**Group the homes into a few zones by what you do, and draw the map once with everyone, so the arrangement stops living in your head and starts living in the whole household. A room whose logic is shared is a room that runs itself, which is exactly where Straighten set out to go.**

**To try this week:** Pick one busy corner and name, in a single word, the activity that happens there. Gather everything that activity needs into that spot, and move out anything that belongs to a different activity. You just drew your first zone.
