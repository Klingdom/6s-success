## Post 1 · Good homes, no shared picture

Stand at your door and look at what you built. Every keeper has a home. Every home sits where your hand reaches for it. Each one can explain itself now, so a friend locking up can put a thing back without asking you a thing. Three chapters of patient work, and all of it holds.

And yet step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bag has its basket, the mail has its tray, and nothing says they belong to one another.

"So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head."

That is the last small gap in Straighten. Nothing here is broken. What is missing is a way to see it all at once, and a way to lift that view out of your head and hand it around.

## Post 2 · The whole move in one line

Most homes, left to grow on their own, get organized by category. All the kitchen things in one cupboard. All the office stuff in one drawer. It feels tidy, and it quietly fails you every day, because you do not live by category. You live by activity.

Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, time to interact with my kitchen things. They think, I want coffee, and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items a category-minded house happily scatters across three cupboards and a shelf.

Here is the whole move of this chapter, in one line.

"Group by what you do, not by what things are."

Gather what one activity needs into the place that activity happens, and the small hunt at the start of every errand is over.

## Post 3 · What a zone actually is

A room map does not list every plant or every painting, the way the little you-are-here diagram at a garden never does. It shows the shape of the place, so in one glance you hold the whole layout.

The pieces of that map are zones, and a zone is a simple thing.

"A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room."

You name it by the verb, not the noun. Not the cupboard of cups and coffee-related objects, but simply the coffee zone: the corner where coffee gets made and everything coffee needs is within arm's reach of the machine.

The best part is that the groups are already there. Watch your household for a day and the zones announce themselves by where people keep colliding: the spot everyone passes leaving, the coffee counter, the homework table, the tangle of charging cables.

## Post 4 · The entryway was a zone all along

Here is the quiet reveal four chapters have been building toward. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots.

"It was one zone all along."

It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens, at the threshold between inside and out. On the way out it hands you what you cannot leave without. On the way in it catches all of it back.

And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them. The coffee zone at the machine. The homework corner at the end of the table. The charging station by the sofa. Five or six named zones will describe almost any home.

## Post 5 · Draw it, and share it

A zone you can see in your mind is a good start, and it is only half the job, because a map only counts when more than one person holds it.

"A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it."

So take a sheet of paper, sketch the rough shape of your home no straighter than a napkin diagram, and block in the zones. Then walk the household through it. Stand in each zone, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told.

Nothing left the house today, yet the friction meter reaches the Straighten-complete mark, the second milestone on the dial. Two of the six S's are done, the room is cleared and fully arranged, and the goal is still ahead, because Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain remain.
