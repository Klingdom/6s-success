# Your Whole Home Runs on a Map Only You Can See. Here Is How to Hand It Around.

Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home. Every home sits where your hand reaches for it. Every one of them can explain itself now, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. Three chapters of patient work are standing in that hallway, and all of it holds.

And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each of those is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. A newcomer meets them the way you meet strangers at a party: one at a time, in the moment you happen to need each one. There is no single view of the space. The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer.

That is the last gap in Straighten, and it is a quiet one. Nothing here is broken. The homes are right, the placement is right, the cues are right. What is missing is a way to see the arrangement as a whole rather than as a heap of well-made parts, and a way to lift that view out of your head and into everyone else's.

Let me fence this off before we go further. We are not re-choosing homes, re-placing them, or re-marking them; those are done and they stay done. We are not cleaning anything, which is the next part of the book. We are not building routines or schedules to keep this up over the coming weeks, which is a later S entirely. Today has one job: gather the homes you already have into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it. Nothing moves house.

## What a room map is

Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or every painting. It shows you the shape of the place: this wing is the sculpture, that corner is the cafe, the gardens are out the back. In one glance you hold the whole layout. A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home.

> A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once.

Two words in that definition are doing the heavy lifting. One is shared, which we come to soon. The other is zones, which is how a scatter of separate homes becomes one readable thing in the first place.

## Zones: group by what you do

Here is the move that turns pieces into a picture, and it is a small change in how you look, not a change in what sits on the shelf.

> Group by what you do, not by what things are.

Most homes, left to grow on their own, get organized by category. All the "kitchen things" go in the kitchen cupboard. It feels tidy, and it quietly fails you every single day, because you do not live by category. You live by activity. Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, "time to interact with my kitchen things." They think, "I want coffee," and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items that a category-minded house happily scatters across three cupboards and a shelf.

Organized by activity, all four sit together at the machine, and the circuit collapses to a reach. Nothing was bought and nothing was thrown out; the same objects simply gathered around the thing you actually do with them. That one spot is a zone.

> A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room.

You name it by the verb, not the noun: not "the cupboard of cups and coffee-related objects" but simply the coffee zone. Name the activity, gather what it needs, and you have drawn a zone. The beauty of it is that the groups are already there; you just have not named them. Watch your household for a day and the zones announce themselves by where people keep colliding.

## The entryway was a zone all along

Now for the quiet reveal, the one four chapters have been building toward without quite saying so. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. It was one zone all along. It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens, at the threshold between inside and out.

We built the launch pad one keeper at a time across three chapters, giving each a home, placing it, and teaching it to speak. Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word.

And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them. The counter by the machine is the coffee zone. The end of the dining table is the homework corner. The drawer and the power strip by the sofa are the charging station. The shelf near the front hall is the mail-and-admin spot. Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home. You do not need dozens. You need the few your life actually runs on.

## Draw it, and share it

A zone you can see in your mind is a good start, and it is only half the job, because the other word in the definition was shared. It takes two small acts: draw it, and walk everyone through it.

Drawing it is almost absurdly simple. Take a sheet of paper and sketch the rough shape of your home, no straighter than a napkin diagram. Then block in the zones: a rectangle by the door labeled launch pad, a square at the counter labeled coffee, a patch at the table labeled homework. You are not drafting a blueprint. You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes.

> A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it.

So do not draw it alone. Draw it with whoever lives there, and let them push back. Someone will tell you the charging station is in the wrong place because nobody actually charges by the sofa, they charge by their beds, and they will be right, and the map will get better. The sharing matters more than the drawing, so if you do only one thing, do this: walk the household through it. Stand in each zone in turn, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it.

> The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run.

## Nothing moved house, and Straighten is complete

Go and look at the friction meter, because this time it reaches something. Three chapters of Straighten stepped the needle toward calm: a home for everything, then each home placed by reach, then each home able to speak. This chapter takes the last step of the four, and the needle arrives at a marked tick on the dial, the second milestone after Sort complete. And, exactly as before, nothing left the house today to earn it. Not one object arrived and not one departed. The homes simply gathered into zones and the arrangement became shared.

Be honest about where the needle sits, though. Reaching the Straighten mark is a genuine milestone, not the finish line. Two of the six S's are done: the room is cleared and it is fully arranged. Four remain, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain, and their job is to clean what you have, protect it, and hold it steady over time. The needle reached a real place today. It also has real distance left to travel, and both of those things are true at once.

You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters. You took a space that worked only because you were there to work it, and you turned it into one that works for everyone, on its own logic, drawn where all can see it. A room whose arrangement is shared asks less of every person in it, including you, and it keeps asking less tomorrow.
