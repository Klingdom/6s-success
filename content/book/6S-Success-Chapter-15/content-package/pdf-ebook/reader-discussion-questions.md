# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: The Room Map

*Chapter 15, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter draws a line between two things: building good homes one at a time, and seeing them all as one whole. Chapters 12 to 14 did the first; Chapter 15 does the second. Where in your own home is every spot well chosen, well placed, and self-explaining, and yet the whole arrangement still reads as separate clever spots with no single picture tying them together?

2. "So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head." Which groupings do you carry in your head right now, obvious to you and invisible to everyone else, because you are the one who runs them and you never drew them?

3. The chapter insists that "A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step." How does reading a private, in-your-head map as a natural stage, rather than a family's failing, change how it feels to notice that the room only works when you are the one running it?

4. The rule, in one line, is: "Group by what you do, not by what things are." Where in your home is a single task scattered across the room because things were grouped by category, and what would it look like to gather them around the activity instead?

5. A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens. Run the coffee example on your own kitchen: which everyday task lives in three or four places right now, and which four or five objects would collapse the circuit to a single reach if they sat together?

6. "Name the activity, gather what it needs, and you have drawn a zone." Zone by the verb, not the noun. Pick a busy corner of your home and try naming it in a single word for what happens there. What does the verb name include that a category name ("kitchen things," "office stuff") would leave scattered?

7. The chapter says the groups are already there; you just have not named them, and that watching your household for a day lets the zones announce themselves by where people keep colliding. Where do the people in your home keep colliding, and which zones are those collisions quietly pointing to?

8. The quiet reveal is that "It was one zone all along." The keys, leash, bag, umbrella, outgoing mail, and shoes were never six separate spots; they are the launch pad. What is the launch pad in your home, and what does naming that whole cluster in a single word change about how anyone else can use it?

9. "Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home." Try listing yours: launch pad, coffee, homework, charging, mail-and-admin, or whatever your life actually runs on. How many do you really need, and which few does your household truly run on?

10. The other word in the definition is shared, and the chapter says it matters more than the drawing. "The sharing matters more than the drawing, so if you do only one thing, do this: walk the household through it." Who in your home holds the arrangement only as a guest right now, and what would change if you walked them through the zones out loud?

11. "You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes." Drawing it is almost absurdly simple, no straighter than a napkin diagram. What has kept the map in your head until now, and what would it take to sketch it once this week and let people push back on it?

12. The reframe is meant to relieve and to share: "The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household." Where have you been on call to run a logic only you could see, and how would it feel to hand that design to the whole household?

13. "It is the difference between a house one person maintains and a home a family keeps, and it costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table." What would ten honest minutes at your kitchen table, everyone standing over the same sheet, actually look like, and who would you want in the room?

14. Nothing left the house in this chapter, yet the friction meter reaches the Straighten-complete mark, the second milestone on the dial, and the goal is still ahead because four S's remain. "For now, the map is drawn, the zones are named, the picture is shared, and Straighten, the second S, is complete." Looking back across the four Straighten chapters, what feels most different about your home now, and what are you most curious to carry into Shine, where the next chapter turns cleaning into a way of noticing?
