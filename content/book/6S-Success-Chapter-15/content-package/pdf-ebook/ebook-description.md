# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

You finished the last chapter with every keeper in a home, every home placed where your hand wants it, and every home now able to explain itself, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each of those is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. "The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer." Chapter 15 of *6S Success: Home Edition* closes that gap.

"The Room Map" is the fourth and final chapter of Part Four, and it completes Straighten, the second of the six S's. Chapter 12 assigned the homes; Chapter 13 placed them by how often you reach for each thing; Chapter 14 made each home speak; this chapter keeps the same keepers and the same room and gathers the homes into a handful of zones, then draws the whole arrangement into one shared picture. The chapter names its fence plainly: it does not re-choose, re-place, or re-mark homes, does not clean or teach cleaning, does not build routines or standards for keeping order over time, and does not re-open Sort. Today has one job, gather the homes you already have into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it.

The starting trouble is quiet, and nothing here is broken. Every home was built one at a time and arranged around itself, not around the errands you actually run. "So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head." You know, without thinking, that the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella are really one cluster, because you are the one who leaves the house, and nobody else can see that grouping, because you never drew it. That is not a failing, and it is worth saying plainly: "A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step." Making it shareable is a small, satisfying job.

The device is the room map. Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or every painting; it shows you the shape of the place, so in one glance you hold the whole layout. "A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home." Set out as a definition: "A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once." Two words do the heavy lifting, and they are the rest of the chapter: zones, which turns a scatter of separate homes into one readable thing, and shared, which is what a map only counts as when more than one person holds it.

The hero move is zones, and its rule is one line: "Group by what you do, not by what things are." Most homes, left to grow, get organized by category, and it quietly fails you every day, because you do not live by category; you live by activity. Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, "time to interact with my kitchen things." They think, "I want coffee," and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items a category-minded house scatters across three cupboards and a shelf. "A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room." You name it by the verb, not the noun: not "the cupboard of cups and coffee-related objects" but simply the coffee zone. "Name the activity, gather what it needs, and you have drawn a zone." And the beauty is that the groups are already there; you just have not named them.

Then the quiet reveal, the one four chapters have been building toward. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. "It was one zone all along." It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens, at the threshold between inside and out. "Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word." And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them: the coffee zone at the machine, the homework corner at the end of the table, the charging station by the sofa, the mail-and-admin spot near the front hall. "Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home."

The other word in the definition is shared, and it matters more than the drawing. "Drawing it is almost absurdly simple." Take a sheet of paper, sketch the rough shape of a room no straighter than a napkin diagram, and block in the zones. "You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes." So do not draw it alone. Draw it with whoever lives there, or at least walk them through it, and let them push back; someone will tell you the charging station is in the wrong place because nobody actually charges by the sofa, and they will be right, and the map will get better. "A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it." So if you do only one thing, walk the household through it, standing in each zone and saying its name out loud, and a child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told.

Then the chapter lands its reframe, meant to relieve and to share, not to scold. "The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run." For as long as the arrangement lived only in your head, you were on call to run it, and a shared map ends that. "It is the difference between a house one person maintains and a home a family keeps, and it costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table."

Nothing left the house in this chapter, yet the friction meter reaches a marked tick, the STRAIGHTEN COMPLETE milestone, the second on the dial after Sort complete. "Sort emptied the room of what did not belong; Straighten arranged what stayed until the space almost runs itself." Chapter 14's position is the faint dashed ghost just behind the live needle, with the earlier Straighten steps and the Sort-complete milestone further back, and the goal crosshair on the calm side is still ahead, because two of the six S's are now done and four remain, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain, to clean, protect, and hold what you have built. "For now, the map is drawn, the zones are named, the picture is shared, and Straighten, the second S, is complete." Next, Chapter 16 opens Part Five, Shine, the third S, which cleans and cares for what remains and turns the simple act of cleaning into a way of noticing what needs attention before it becomes a problem.
