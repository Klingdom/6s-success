# Back Cover Copy: The Room Map

## Back-cover text

Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home. Every home sits where your hand reaches for it. Every one of them can explain itself now, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each of those is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. "The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer."

Chapter 15 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the fourth and final chapter of Part Four, and it completes Straighten, the second of the six S's. Chapter 12 assigned the homes; Chapter 13 placed them by how often you reach; Chapter 14 made each home speak; this chapter gathers those good homes into a handful of zones and draws the whole arrangement into one shared picture. Nothing moves house. A private, in-your-head map is not a failing: "A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step."

The move is the room map, and its rule is one line: "Group by what you do, not by what things are." A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room. You name it by the verb, not the noun: the coffee zone at the machine, the homework corner at the table, the charging station by the beds. And the row of homes by the door turns out to be one zone all along, the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered at the threshold. "Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word."

The other word in the definition is shared, and it matters more than the drawing. "A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it." So sketch it no straighter than a napkin diagram, then walk the household through it, standing in each zone and saying its name out loud. Nothing left the house, yet the friction meter reaches a marked tick, the STRAIGHTEN COMPLETE milestone, the second on the dial after Sort complete. The step is real, and the goal is still ahead, because Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain remain. Pick one busy corner, name the activity in a single word, gather what it needs, and you have drawn your first zone.
