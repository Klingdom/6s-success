# 6S Success: Home Edition (Sales Page Copy)

## Every home speaks now, but the whole picture lives in your head.

Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home. Every home sits where your hand reaches for it. Every one of them can explain itself now, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. Three chapters of patient work are standing in that hallway, and all of it holds.

And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each of those is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. A newcomer meets them the way you meet strangers at a party: one at a time, in the moment you happen to need each one. "The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer."

## Welcome to the last step of Straighten.

Chapter 15 is the fourth and final chapter of Part Four, and it completes Straighten, the second S. Chapter 12 gave every keeper a home; Chapter 13 placed each home by how often you reach for it; Chapter 14 made each home speak; this chapter keeps the same keepers and the same room and gathers the homes into a handful of zones, then draws the whole arrangement into one shared picture. Building good homes and seeing them as one whole are two separate acts, and only the first was done.

Nothing moves house. The room holds exactly what it held this morning. All that changes is that the homes gather into zones and the picture becomes shared, so the arrangement stops living in one person's head and starts living in the whole household.

## The picture lives in exactly one place: behind your eyes.

Look closely at why the room works and you find something quiet. Every home was built one at a time and arranged around itself, not around the errands you actually run. "So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head." You know, without thinking, that the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella are really one cluster, because you are the one who leaves the house, and nobody else can see that grouping, because you never drew it. That is not a failing. "A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step."

## A room map turns a scatter into a single view.

Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or every painting; it shows you the shape of the place, so in one glance you hold the whole layout. "A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home." You are not adding storage or buying a system; you are stepping back far enough to see it all at once, gathering the homes into groups, and drawing that grouping where everyone can read it.

> A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once.

## The hero move is one rule.

How do you turn a scatter of clever individual homes into a short list of zones anyone can point to? There is one rule, and it is the center of this chapter.

> Group by what you do, not by what things are.

Most homes get organized by category, and it quietly fails you every day, because you do not live by category; you live by activity. "A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room." Name it by the verb, not the noun. The morning coffee, by category, lives in four places, and making a cup is a small circuit of the kitchen; by activity, all four sit at the machine and the circuit collapses to a reach, though nothing was bought and nothing thrown out.

## The entryway was a zone all along.

That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. "It was one zone all along." It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens. On the way out it hands you what you cannot leave without; on the way in it catches all of it back. "Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word." And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way: "Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home."

## The map only counts when it is shared.

> A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it.

The other word in the definition is shared, and it matters more than the drawing. "Drawing it is almost absurdly simple." Sketch the rough shape of your home no straighter than a napkin diagram and block in the zones. Then, if you do only one thing, walk the household through it: stand in each zone, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told.

## You carried the whole arrangement. Now hand it around.

> The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run.

This is meant to relieve and to share, not to scold. For as long as the arrangement lived only in your head, you were on call to run it, and everyone else was a guest following rules they could feel but not see. A shared map ends that. "It is the difference between a house one person maintains and a home a family keeps, and it costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table."

## The needle reaches the mark.

Nothing leaves the house in this chapter. Not one object arrives and not one departs. And still the friction meter reaches a marked tick, the STRAIGHTEN COMPLETE milestone, the second on the dial after Sort complete, because the homes gathered into shared zones and the room's logic stopped being a private thing and became a public one. Every task now sits in one spot instead of spread across three; nobody carries the layout in their head as a standing duty; the room can be read at a glance instead of discovered home by home. "Sort emptied the room of what did not belong; Straighten arranged what stayed until the space almost runs itself." The milestone is real, and the goal is still ahead, because two of the six S's are now done and four remain, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain.

"You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters." Pick one busy corner and name, in a single word, the activity that happens there. Gather everything that activity needs into that spot, and move out anything that belongs to a different activity. You just drew your first zone. "For now, the map is drawn, the zones are named, the picture is shared, and Straighten, the second S, is complete."
