**The recurring motif.** The hero device of the chapter is the room map: zones plus a shared picture. Individual homes are good, but until they cohere into an overall logic and that logic is shared, the arrangement still lives as scattered pieces in one person's head. A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens; the room map is the whole-room view, the zones drawn once and shared, so the entire space reads at a glance and belongs to everyone. Two images carry the whole set. The first is the pencilled "?" and the thought-bubble sketch in soft #6A625A: the picture that ties the good separate spots together, existing in exactly one place, behind the builder's eyes. The second is the drawn floor plan with its five named zones lit in their fixed colours: the launch pad terracotta #BC4B2A, the coffee zone honey #DDA63A, the homework corner slate #3C5A6B, the charging station green #6E8B5B, the mail-and-admin spot soft #6A625A. The arc of the chapter is the move from the first image to the second: the private map comes out of the head, lands on paper, and gets walked through, so it belongs to the household. Green also marks the shared map that everyone holds, the calm side of the meter, and the STRAIGHTEN COMPLETE mark; slate carries the chapter labels, the GOAL crosshair, and the milestone ticks; friction spark #CB4B36 is reserved for the friction side of the meter; terracotta doubles as the chapter kicker; the ghost needle #8A8073 is Chapter 14's position, now the dashed ghost, with the earlier Straighten steps fainter still.

**Two fences to hold visually.** First, this is gathering the already-made, well-placed, self-explaining homes into zones and making the picture shared, not re-choosing them (Chapter 12), re-placing them by frequency (Chapter 13), or re-marking them (Chapter 14): nothing moves house, so never redraw the giving-a-home pass, never re-sort the homes by reach, never redraw the labels and outlines, and never reopen Sort. This chapter only groups the finished homes into a few zones and draws the whole arrangement into one shared view. Second, do not clean, scrub, or teach cleaning as inspection (that is Chapter 16, Shine, a teaser only), and do not build routines, schedules, checklists, or written standards to keep the arrangement up over time (that is the later Standardize and Sustain S's, a teaser only). The room map is a one-time arranging act of structure plus sharing, not a maintenance system. Keep the focus on gathering into zones and sharing the picture.

## Beat 1 · The homes are all here, and there is no picture

The chapter and both decks open the same way: the finished, well-placed, self-explaining entryway, seen all at once, every spot correct and separate, with no single picture tying them together and the only picture living in one head.

- Composition: full-bleed, warm daylight. The entryway from the last three chapters, seen all at once. Along the wall by the door sit a handful of good, finished spots, each clearly complete and clearly separate, with a little gap of blank wall between one and the next: a labeled hook for the keys, a labeled hook for the leash, a marked basket for the bags, an umbrella stand, a tray marked out for the mail. Floating faintly above the row, sketched in light pencil, is a large "?" where a single connecting picture would sit.
- Palette: paper #F7F2E9 ground, ink #2B2622 outlines, soft #6A625A on the pencilled "?" and the faint sketch. No zone colours yet; the point is the missing picture.
- Motif: the parts are all here and all correct; they simply do not yet form one thing. The good separate spots have no logic binding them, and nothing shows the whole at a glance.
- Caption line, from the book: "Every home here is finished: chosen, placed, and able to explain itself. But they stand as separate good spots with no picture that ties them together, and the only place that picture exists is inside the person who built them. That gap is what this chapter closes."

## Beat 4 · A private map is a natural stage, not a failing

The anti-shame beat, held gently, so the reader never feels behind for having carried the map in their head.

- Composition: the four entryway keepers, keys, leash, bag, umbrella, softly ringed together as one cluster in soft #6A625A, with a dashed thread running up to the single thought-bubble over one person's head. The cluster is real and drawn clearly; the thread makes plain it is visible to only one person.
- Palette: soft #6A625A on the cluster ring, the thread, and the thought-bubble, slate #3C5A6B on the tag. Warm and gentle, no verdict colours; this beat lifts a weight, it does not add one.
- A slate #3C5A6B tag: "real, and invisible, and yours alone."
- Point: you know, without thinking, that the keys and leash and bag and umbrella are one cluster, the leaving-the-house cluster, because you are the one who leaves. ## Beat 5 · What a room map is

The chapter's core idea, carried by the you-are-here floor plan and the garden or museum diagram it borrows from.

- Composition: a flattened, friendly floor plan of a home on paper ground, calm and legible at a glance, with a few blocks of colour labeled by activity: the launch pad by the door in terracotta #BC4B2A, the coffee zone at the counter in honey #DDA63A, the homework corner at the table in slate #3C5A6B, the charging station in green #6E8B5B, the mail-and-admin spot in soft #6A625A. A small "you are here" dot, like the diagram at the entrance of a garden or a museum.
- Palette: the five zone colours on their blocks, ink outlines on paper, slate #3C5A6B on the caption. Calm and ordinary, friendly and readable, not a blueprint.
- Caption, in slate #3C5A6B italic: "Grouped by what you do, not by what things are."
- Point: the you-are-here diagram does not list every plant or painting; it shows the shape of the place, so in one glance you hold the whole layout. A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home, brought home from the workshop floor where a good shop keeps an overall layout anyone can read.
- The frozen definition box sits beneath, in Fraunces on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once."

## Beat 6 · Zones: group by what you do

The hero move, made concrete by the coffee example, side by side.

- Composition: the coffee example on paper ground, two panels. Left, "BY CATEGORY": a kitchen with mugs on the crockery shelf, beans in the dry-goods cupboard, grinder in the appliance drawer, filters on a fourth shelf, joined by a soft #6A625A looping path that circuits the room. Right, "BY ACTIVITY": the same four items gathered at the machine inside one honey #DDA63A coffee-zone boundary, the path collapsed to a single reach.
- Palette: soft #6A625A on the scattered path, honey #DDA63A on the coffee-zone boundary, a single green #6E8B5B calm-dot on the gathered zone. Paper ground, ink outlines.
- Caption, verbatim from the book: "the same objects simply gathered around the thing you actually do with them." Nothing was bought and nothing thrown out; the circuit collapsed to a reach.
- Point: most homes get organized by category, and it quietly fails you every day, because you live by activity, not by category. Grouping by what things are spreads a single task across the room; grouping by what you do pulls it back into one spot, and that one spot is a zone.
- The frozen rule sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "Group by what you do, not by what things are." The frozen woven definition may accompany it: "A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room." Beneath, the 6S Tip in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Zone by the verb, not the noun. A coffee zone (mug, beans, grinder, filters, all at the machine) beats a "kitchen things" cupboard, because you live by what you are doing, not by what category a thing happens to fall in."

## Beat 10 · Share it: walk the household through it

The second act and the heart of the chapter, given the pull-quote treatment. The sharing matters more than the drawing.

- Composition: a family gathered over one sheet at the kitchen table on paper ground, hands pointing to different zones, one person adding a correction to the charging station because nobody actually charges by the sofa. A green #6E8B5B calm-dot rests on the shared map, meaning it is now held by everyone.
- Palette: green #6E8B5B on the shared map and its calm-dot, the zone colours on the sketch, slate #3C5A6B on the idea-box rules. Warm and light; the room is becoming theirs.
- The two frozen pull-quotes, set large in Fraunces medium #2B2622 on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules. The shared-map completion: "A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it." And the reframe: "The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run."
- Beneath, the Family Challenge in a honey #DDA63A bordered callout, verbatim: "Walk the house together and give each area a one-word job: launch pad, homework, coffee, charging. Then sketch a simple map of where those zones sit. When everyone can point to the same picture, the map is shared." And the Common Mistake in a friction spark #CB4B36 bordered callout, verbatim: "Keeping the map in your own head and expecting the household to absorb it. If you never draw it and never walk everyone through it, you are still the map, and the room still stops working the day you are away."
- Point: stand in each zone in turn, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told, and the questions that used to arrive at your elbow now have a place to point instead.
