### Slide 2 · The view exists in exactly one place

- Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home, every home sits where your hand reaches for it, and every one can explain itself now, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. Three chapters of patient work are standing in that hallway, and all of it holds.
- And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. A newcomer meets them the way you meet strangers at a party: one at a time, in the moment they happen to need each one. There is no single view of the space, nothing you could point a phone camera at and say, in one breath, here is how this corner works.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer." That is the last gap in Straighten, and it is a quiet one: nothing is broken, the homes are right, the placement is right, the cues are right; what is missing is a way to see the arrangement as a whole and a way to lift that view out of your head.

### Slide 3 · Good homes, no shared picture

- Gather up what the last three chapters built, because it is the ground this one stands on. Chapter 12 gave every keeper a single home; Chapter 13 placed each home by how often your hand goes there; Chapter 14 made each home speak. Three real gains, and none of them unwinds today.
- But notice the shape of all three. Every one works on a home, one at a time, and that is exactly the right way to build, and it leaves a particular thing undone. A house is not experienced one hook at a time; you move through it in errands, leaving for work, making coffee, getting the kids out the door, sitting down to pay a bill, and each errand touches several homes at once. Nothing so far has arranged the homes around the errands. They are arranged around themselves.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head." Keep the fences honest: not re-choosing, re-placing, or re-marking homes (Chapters 12 to 14), not cleaning (Shine, Chapter 16), and not building routines or schedules to keep this up (a later S). Today has one job: gather the homes into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it.

### Slide 4 · A private map is a natural stage, not a failing

- Before the move itself, a word that matters, because it is easy to feel behind here. You know, without thinking, that the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella are really one cluster, the leaving-the-house cluster, because you are the one who leaves the house. Nobody else can see that grouping, because you never drew it. It is real, and it is invisible, and it belongs to you alone.
- That is not a failing, and it is worth saying plainly. The frozen line, verbatim: "A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step." It is simply the last thing that has not been made shareable, and making it shareable is a small, satisfying job.

### Slide 5 · What a room map is

- Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or every painting; it shows you the shape of the place: this wing is the sculpture, that corner is the cafe, the gardens are out the back. In one glance you hold the whole layout, and from then on you can find anything without being led by the hand.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home." The idea comes, like much of Straighten, from the workshop floor, where a good shop keeps an overall layout anyone can read, so a newcomer or a tired regular can see how the space flows without carrying it all in memory. Bring that home and shrink it, and you get a single view of your rooms that shows not just where each thing lives but how the space is organized.
- Definition box, verbatim: "A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once." Two words in that definition do the heavy lifting and are the rest of the chapter: shared, which comes soon, and zones, which comes next.

### Slide 6 · Zones: group by what you do

- Here is the move that turns pieces into a picture, a small change in how you look, not a change in what sits on the shelf. Most homes, left to grow on their own, get organized by category, all the "kitchen things" in one cupboard, all the "office stuff" in one drawer. It feels tidy, and it quietly fails you every single day, because you do not live by category. You live by activity.
- Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, "time to interact with my kitchen things." They think, "I want coffee," and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items a category-minded house happily scatters across three cupboards and a shelf. Grouping by what things are spreads a single task across the room. Grouping by what you do pulls that task back into one spot. That one spot is a zone.
- The rule, verbatim: "Group by what you do, not by what things are."
- Definition, woven in, verbatim: "A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room." Name it by the verb, not the noun: not "the cupboard of cups and coffee-related objects" but simply the coffee zone. The groups are already there; you just have not named them.
- 6S Tip, verbatim: "Zone by the verb, not the noun. A coffee zone (mug, beans, grinder, filters, all at the machine) beats a "kitchen things" cupboard, because you live by what you are doing, not by what category a thing happens to fall in."

### Slide 7 · The entryway was a zone all along

- Now for the quiet reveal, the one four chapters have been building toward without quite saying so. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. The frozen line, verbatim: "It was one zone all along." It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens, at the threshold between inside and out.
- On the way out, the launch pad hands you, in one motion, the things you cannot leave without; on the way in, it catches all of that back. Every item serves the same verb, leaving and returning, which is why they belong side by side and why keeping them together is what makes the door work. We built the launch pad one keeper at a time across three chapters. The frozen line, verbatim: "Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word."
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Pick one busy corner and name, in a single word, the activity that happens there. Gather everything that activity needs into that spot, and move out anything that belongs to a different activity. You just drew your first zone."

### Slide 8 · Your home is a handful of zones

- Once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them. The kitchen counter by the machine is the coffee zone. The end of the dining table is the homework corner. The drawer and the power strip by the sofa are the charging station. The shelf near the front hall is the mail-and-admin spot, where the post lands and the bills get dealt with.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home." You do not need dozens. You need the few your life actually runs on, each holding what its activity needs, and suddenly the entire place has a logic you could explain to a visitor in the time it takes to walk them from the door to the kitchen.

### Slide 9 · Draw it

- A zone you can see in your mind is a good start, and it is only half the job, because the other word in the definition was shared. This is where the room map stops being a private insight and becomes something the household actually holds, and it takes two small acts: draw it, and walk everyone through it.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "Drawing it is almost absurdly simple." Take a sheet of paper and sketch the rough shape of a room, or the floor of your home, no straighter than a napkin diagram. Then block in the zones: a rectangle by the door labeled launch pad, a square at the counter labeled coffee, a patch at the table labeled homework, and so on.
- You are not drafting a blueprint. The frozen line, verbatim: "You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes." The moment it is on paper, it can be looked at, pointed to, argued with, and improved, none of which was possible while it lived only in you.

### Slide 10 · Share it: walk the household through it

- So do not draw it alone. Draw it with whoever lives there, or at least walk them through it once it is sketched, and let them push back. Someone will tell you the charging station is in the wrong place because nobody actually charges by the sofa, they charge by their beds, and they will be right, and the map will get better.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "The sharing matters more than the drawing, so if you do only one thing, do this: walk the household through it." Stand in each zone in turn, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it, this is the launch pad, this is where charging happens, this is the coffee corner. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told.
- The shared-map completion, verbatim: "A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it."
- The reframe, verbatim: "The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run."
- Family Challenge, verbatim: "Walk the house together and give each area a one-word job: launch pad, homework, coffee, charging. Then sketch a simple map of where those zones sit. When everyone can point to the same picture, the map is shared."
- Common Mistake, verbatim: "Keeping the map in your own head and expecting the household to absorb it. If you never draw it and never walk everyone through it, you are still the map, and the room still stops working the day you are away."

### Slide 12 · Straighten, complete

- Stand at the door one last time and take in the four chapters together, because they add up to more than any one of them looked like on its own. You gave every keeper a home. You placed each home where your hand wants it. You taught each home to speak for itself. And today you gathered them into zones and drew them into one shared picture. The frozen line, verbatim: "Sort emptied the room of what did not belong; Straighten arranged what stayed until the space almost runs itself." The corner by the door that used to swallow keys and pile up mail is now a launch pad anyone in the house can name, use, and reset without a word from you.
- The frozen line, verbatim: "You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters." You took a space that worked only because you were there to work it, and you turned it into one that works for everyone, on its own logic, drawn where all can see it.
