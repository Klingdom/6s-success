# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 15, The Room Map

Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home. Every home sits where your hand reaches for it, and every one of them can now explain itself, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you. Three chapters of patient work are standing in that hallway, and all of it holds. And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. There is no single view of the space, nothing you could point a phone camera at and say, in one breath, here is how this corner works. The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer. That is the last gap in Straighten, and this chapter closes it.

Notice the shape of the last three chapters. Every one of them worked on a home, one at a time: a hook, a shelf, a bin, a tray, each considered on its own and made good on its own. That is exactly the right way to build, and it leaves a particular thing undone. A house is not experienced one hook at a time. You move through it in errands: leaving for work, making coffee, getting the kids out the door, sitting down to pay a bill. Each of those touches several homes at once, and nothing so far has arranged the homes around the errands. They are arranged around themselves. So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head. That is not a failing, and it is worth saying plainly. A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step.

Welcome to the fourth and final step of Straighten. Giving each keeper a home was the right first move, placing each home by reach was the right second, teaching each home to speak was the right third, and none of it unwinds here. Today has one job: gather the homes you already have into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it. Nothing moves house. We are only stepping back far enough to see it all at once. Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly you are here dot. It shows you the shape of the place in a single glance. A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home: a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once.

Two words in that definition do the heavy lifting. The first is zones, which is how a scatter of separate homes becomes one readable thing. Here is the move that turns pieces into a picture, and it is the whole rule of the chapter: group by what you do, not by what things are. Most homes, left to grow on their own, get organized by category. All the kitchen things go in the kitchen cupboard, and it quietly fails you every single day, because you do not live by category. You live by activity. Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, time to interact with my kitchen things. They think, I want coffee, and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items a category-minded house happily scatters across three cupboards and a shelf. Gather those four at the machine and the circuit collapses to a reach. Nothing was bought and nothing was thrown out. That one spot is a zone: everything one activity needs gathered into the place that activity happens, named by the verb and not the noun. Do that for the handful of things your household actually does, and the room becomes a short list of zones anyone can point to.

Now for the quiet reveal, the one four chapters have been building toward without quite saying so. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. It was one zone all along: the launch pad, everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens. Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word. And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them: the coffee zone at the machine, the homework corner at the end of the table, the charging station by the beds, the mail-and-admin spot near the front hall. Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home. You do not need dozens. You need the few your life actually runs on.

A zone you can see in your mind is a good start, and it is only half the job, because the other word in the definition was shared. Drawing it is almost absurdly simple: take a sheet of paper, sketch the rough shape of a room no straighter than a napkin diagram, and block in the zones. You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes. Then do not draw it alone. A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it. The sharing matters more than the drawing, so if you do only one thing, do this: walk the household through it. Stand in each zone in turn, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it. The arrangement was yours; drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run.

That shift is the real prize, and it costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table. It also completes the second S. Sort emptied the room of what did not belong; Straighten arranged what stayed until the space almost runs itself. You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters. You took a space that worked only because you were there to work it and turned it into one that works for everyone, on its own logic, drawn where all can see it. The friction meter reaches the Straighten-complete mark, the second milestone on the dial after Sort complete. Be honest about what that means: two of the six S's are done, the room is cleared and fully arranged, and the goal is still ahead, because Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain remain to clean, protect, and hold what you have built.

Gather everything that activity needs into that spot, and move out anything that belongs to a different activity. You just drew your first zone.
