## Chapter Opening

Stand at the door and look at what you have made. Every keeper has a home. Every home sits where your hand reaches for it. Every one of them can explain itself now, so a friend locking up or a guest dropping off the dog can put a thing back without asking you a thing. Three chapters of patient work are standing in that hallway, and all of it holds.

And yet, step back and squint, and the entryway is still a row of separate clever spots. The keys have their hook, the leash has its hook, the bags have their basket, the mail has its tray, and each of those is finished and good, but nothing says they belong to one another. A newcomer meets them the way you meet strangers at a party: one at a time, in the moment you happen to need each one. There is no single view of the space, nothing you could point a phone camera at and say, in one breath, here is how this corner works. The view exists, but it exists in exactly one place, behind your eyes, and it comes out only when someone asks and you answer.

That is the last gap in Straighten, and it is a quiet one. Nothing here is broken. The homes are right, the placement is right, the cues are right. What is missing is a level up: a way to see the arrangement as a whole rather than as a heap of well-made parts, and a way to lift that view out of your head and into everyone else's. This chapter draws that view, gives it a name, and hands it around. Then the second S is done.

## Good Homes, No Shared Picture

Let me gather up what the last three chapters built, because it is a lot, and it is the ground this one stands on. In Chapter 12 you gave every keeper a single home, a definite spot it lives in and returns to. In Chapter 13 you placed each of those homes by how often your hand goes there, the daily things close and the rare things a polite step away. In Chapter 14 you made each home speak, so the spot itself says what belongs and whether it is there, and nobody has to consult you. Three real gains, and none of them unwinds today.

But notice the shape of all three. Every one of them works on a home, one at a time. A hook, a shelf, a bin, a tray, each considered on its own and made good on its own. That is exactly the right way to build, and it leaves a particular thing undone. A house is not experienced one hook at a time. You move through it in errands: leaving for work, making coffee, getting the kids out the door, sitting down to pay a bill. Each of those errands touches several homes at once, and nothing you have done so far has arranged the homes around the errands. They are arranged around themselves.

So the arrangement, good as it is, still lives as scattered pieces, and the thing that connects those pieces still lives in your head. You know, without thinking, that the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella are really one cluster, the leaving-the-house cluster, because you are the one who leaves the house. Nobody else can see that grouping, because you never drew it. It is real, and it is invisible, and it belongs to you alone. That is not a failing, and it is worth saying plainly. A map that lives in one person's head is a completely natural stage to reach; it is where every well-run home sits right before this step. It is simply the last thing that has not been made shareable, and making it shareable is a small, satisfying job.

Before we start, the usual fence, so we stay on one task. We are not re-choosing homes, re-placing them, or re-marking them; those are done and they stay done. We are not cleaning anything, which is the next part of the book. We are not building routines or schedules to keep this up over the coming weeks, which is a later S entirely. Today has one job: gather the homes you already have into a few natural groups, draw that grouping as one view, and share it. Nothing moves house. We are only stepping back far enough to see it all at once.

## What a Room Map Is

Think of the little diagram by the entrance of a big garden or a museum, the one with the colored blocks and the friendly "you are here" dot. It does not list every plant or every painting. It shows you the shape of the place: this wing is the sculpture, that corner is the cafe, the gardens are out the back. In one glance you hold the whole layout, and from then on you can find anything without being led by the hand. A room map is that diagram, shrunk to fit your home.

The idea comes, like much of Straighten, from the workshop floor, where a good shop keeps an overall layout that anyone can read: the welding happens here, the assembly there, the finished work waits by the door. The point was never decoration. It was that a newcomer, or a tired regular, could see how the space is meant to flow without carrying it all in memory. Bring that home and shrink it, and you get a single view of your rooms that shows not just where each thing lives but how the space is organized, so the arrangement can be read all at once instead of discovered piece by piece.

> A room map is a single shared picture of a space, organized into zones by the activities that happen there, so anyone in the household can see where everything lives, and why, all at once.

Two words in that definition are doing the heavy lifting, and they are the rest of this chapter. One is shared, which we come to soon: a map only counts when more than one person holds it. The other is zones, which is how a scatter of separate homes becomes one readable thing in the first place. That is where we go next.

## Zones: Group by What You Do

Here is the move that turns pieces into a picture, and it is a small change in how you look, not a change in what sits on the shelf.

> Group by what you do, not by what things are.

Most homes, left to grow on their own, get organized by category. All the "kitchen things" go in the kitchen cupboard. All the "office stuff" lands in the office drawer. It feels tidy, and it quietly fails you every single day, because you do not live by category. You live by activity. Nobody stands in the kitchen thinking, "time to interact with my kitchen things." They think, "I want coffee," and then they need the mug and the beans and the grinder and the filters, four items that a category-minded house happily scatters across three cupboards and a shelf. Grouping by what things *are* spreads a single task across the room. Grouping by what you *do* pulls that task back into one spot.

Try the two ways side by side and the difference is hard to miss. Organized by category, the morning coffee lives in four places: mugs on the crockery shelf with the plates, beans in the dry-goods cupboard with the flour, the grinder in the appliance drawer with the blender, filters wherever they last landed. Making a cup means a small circuit of the kitchen before the kettle has even boiled. Organized by activity, all four sit together at the machine, and the circuit collapses to a reach. Nothing was bought and nothing was thrown out; the same objects simply gathered around the thing you actually do with them.

That one spot is a zone. A zone gathers everything one activity needs into the place that activity happens, so a task is served by one spot instead of scattered across the room. You name it by the verb, not the noun: not "the cupboard of cups and coffee-related objects" but simply the coffee zone, the corner where coffee gets made and everything coffee needs is within arm's reach of the machine. Name the activity, gather what it needs, and you have drawn a zone. Do that for the handful of things your household actually does, and the room stops being a scatter of clever individual homes and becomes a short list of zones anyone can point to.

The beauty of grouping by activity is that the groups are already there; you just have not named them. Watch your household for a day and the zones announce themselves by where people keep colliding: the spot everyone passes leaving the house, the counter where the morning coffee ritual happens, the table that becomes homework central at four o'clock, the tangle of cables where every device charges overnight, the flat surface where the mail and the bills and the school forms pile up. Each of those is a zone waiting to be recognized. The homes you built already sit near the right activities; drawing the zone is mostly a matter of naming the activity out loud and nudging the last few strays into place.

> **6S Tip**
> Zone by the verb, not the noun. A coffee zone (mug, beans, grinder, filters, all at the machine) beats a "kitchen things" cupboard, because you live by what you are doing, not by what category a thing happens to fall in.

## The Entryway Was a Zone All Along

Now for the quiet reveal, the one four chapters have been building toward without quite saying so. That row of homes by the door, the keys and the leash and the bag and the umbrella and the outgoing mail and the shoes, was never really six separate spots. It was one zone all along. It is the launch pad: everything the single activity of leaving and arriving needs, clustered exactly where that activity happens, at the threshold between inside and out.

Look at what the launch pad actually does. On the way out, you sweep past it and it hands you, in one motion, the things you cannot leave without: keys in hand, leash if the dog is coming, bag over the shoulder, umbrella if the sky looks doubtful, the letter that needs posting, shoes on. On the way in, it catches all of that back. Every item in the cluster serves the same verb, leaving and returning, which is why they belong side by side and why keeping them together is what makes the door work. We built the launch pad one keeper at a time across three chapters, giving each a home, placing it, and teaching it to speak. Naming it as one zone is the last stitch, the one that turns six good homes into a single thing a person can understand in a word.

And once you can see one zone, you can see the rest of the house the same way, because your home is really just a handful of them. The kitchen counter by the machine is the coffee zone. The end of the dining table is the homework corner. The drawer and the power strip by the sofa are the charging station. The shelf near the front hall is the mail-and-admin spot, where the post lands and the bills get dealt with. Five or six zones, each named for what happens there, will describe almost any home. You do not need dozens. You need the few your life actually runs on, each holding what its activity needs, and suddenly the entire place has a logic you could explain to a visitor in the time it takes to walk them from the door to the kitchen.

> **Quick Win**
> Pick one busy corner and name, in a single word, the activity that happens there. Gather everything that activity needs into that spot, and move out anything that belongs to a different activity. You just drew your first zone.

## Draw It, and Share It

A zone you can see in your mind is a good start, and it is only half the job, because the other word in the definition was shared. This is where the room map stops being a private insight and becomes something the household actually holds, and it takes two small acts: draw it, and walk everyone through it.

Drawing it is almost absurdly simple. Take a sheet of paper and sketch the rough shape of a room, or the floor of your home, no straighter than a napkin diagram. Then block in the zones: a rectangle by the door labeled launch pad, a square at the counter labeled coffee, a patch at the table labeled homework, and so on. You are not drafting a blueprint. You are making the map in your head visible, so it exists somewhere other than behind your eyes. The moment it is on paper, it can be looked at, pointed to, argued with, and improved, none of which was possible while it lived only in you.

> A map that lives in one person's head is a private map, and a private map fails the moment that person steps out. A map the whole household has drawn together is the one that finally makes the room belong to everyone who lives in it.

So do not draw it alone. Draw it with whoever lives there, or at least walk them through it once it is sketched, and let them push back. Someone will tell you the charging station is in the wrong place because nobody actually charges by the sofa, they charge by their beds, and they will be right, and the map will get better. When everyone has stood over the same sheet and can point to the same zones, the map is shared, and something quietly shifts in who the room belongs to.

The sharing matters more than the drawing, so if you do only one thing, do this: walk the household through it. It takes a few minutes. Stand in each zone in turn, say its name out loud, and let people put their hands on it, this is the launch pad, this is where charging happens, this is the coffee corner. A child who has walked the map once can return the leash to the launch pad without being told, because they have seen the whole arrangement named, not just guessed at it. And the walk-through is where the map earns its keep for you, too: the questions that used to arrive at your elbow all day now have a place to point instead, and the pointing does the answering.

> The arrangement was yours. You chose it, you carried it, and everyone else lived as a guest inside a logic they never quite saw. Drawing the map together hands the design to the whole household, so it stops being your room that they live in and becomes their room that they run.

That shift is the real prize of this chapter, and it is easy to underrate. For as long as the arrangement lived only in your head, you were on call to run it, and everyone else was a guest following rules they could feel but not see. A shared map ends that. It is the difference between a house one person maintains and a home a family keeps, and it costs nothing but a sheet of paper and ten honest minutes at the kitchen table.

> **Family Challenge**
> Walk the house together and give each area a one-word job: launch pad, homework, coffee, charging. Then sketch a simple map of where those zones sit. When everyone can point to the same picture, the map is shared.

> **Common Mistake**
> Keeping the map in your own head and expecting the household to absorb it. If you never draw it and never walk everyone through it, you are still the map, and the room still stops working the day you are away.

## The Needle Reaches the Mark

Go and look at the friction meter, and take a moment with it, because this time it reaches something.

Three chapters of Straighten stepped the needle toward calm: a home for everything, then each home placed by reach, then each home able to speak. This chapter takes the last step of the four, and the needle does not just nudge, it arrives at a marked tick on the dial, the second milestone after Sort complete. Straighten is done. And, exactly as before, nothing left the house today to earn it. Not one object arrived and not one departed. The homes simply gathered into zones and the arrangement became shared, so the room's logic stopped being a private thing and became a public one.

That is real friction gone, and you can name where it went. Every task now sits in one spot instead of spread across three, so the small hunt at the start of every errand is over. Nobody carries the layout in their head as a standing duty, because it is drawn on a sheet everyone has seen. The room can be read at a glance instead of discovered home by home. Add those up across a household and a week and the calm is plainly earned, which is why the needle sits on the mark and not a millimetre short of it.

## Straighten, Complete

Stand at the door one last time and take in the four chapters together, because they add up to more than any one of them looked like on its own. You gave every keeper a home. You placed each home where your hand wants it. You taught each home to speak for itself. And today you gathered them into zones and drew them into one shared picture. Sort emptied the room of what did not belong; Straighten arranged what stayed until the space almost runs itself. The corner by the door that used to swallow keys and pile up mail is now a launch pad anyone in the house can name, use, and reset without a word from you. That is the whole of the second S, standing finished in a hallway.

It is worth pausing on that, quietly, before moving on. You did not buy a system or add an ounce of storage across these four chapters. You took a space that worked only because you were there to work it, and you turned it into one that works for everyone, on its own logic, drawn where all can see it. A room whose arrangement is shared asks less of every person in it, including you, and it keeps asking less tomorrow. That is a real thing to have made, and it deserves a moment's satisfaction before the book turns to what comes next.

Because the room is cleared and arranged, but it is not yet cared for. The surfaces still gather dust, the corners still need wiping, and there is a particular kind of attention, cleaning done as noticing, that turns a tidy room into one that stays healthy and tells you early when something is wrong. That is the third S, Shine, and it opens the next part of the book. For now, the map is drawn, the zones are named, the picture is shared, and Straighten, the second S, is complete.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> Group the homes into a few zones by what you do, and draw the map once with everyone, so the arrangement stops living in your head and starts living in the whole household. A room whose logic is shared is a room that runs itself, which is exactly where Straighten set out to go.
