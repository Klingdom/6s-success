## CORE SHOT LIST

### Shot 1: A good clean, already drifting (hero opener)
- The cleared, arranged, freshly cleaned home from the last chapter, caught in the first hours of drifting back. It is still basically good, but the slide has started: a single mug left on the wiped counter, a coat slipping off its hook toward the floor, a couple of shoes nudged out of the launch pad, the faint return of crumbs by the toaster, a small stack of mail beginning to pile. On the wall, a clock. Nothing is a mess yet.
- Critical detail: the home was cleaned properly, and within a day it is already drifting, because a one-time clean has no way to hold. The drift is small, daily, and inevitable without a rhythm to meet it. The point: clean once is not clean kept.
- Coverage: one wide holding the whole home and its dozen tiny signs of drift; one slow push across the mug, the coat, the crumbs, the mail, in turn; one tilt up to the clock on the wall.
- **On-screen text moment:** "A day after a good clean, the drift has already begun." then "Clean once is not clean kept."
- **Lower third:** Chapter 19 · The 15-Minute Reset

### Shot 2: Clean once is not clean kept (the missing mechanism)
- The home from Chapter 18, genuinely clean end to end, with a small time-lapse feel beside it: by tomorrow a few things out of place, by the weekend the counters collecting their drift, by a fortnight most of the way back to where it began. Beside it, the finished Shine list, the reframe, and the methods laid out, each labeled with what it gives, why, what, who, how, and a single blank card where "when" and "how often" should be.
- The point: a one-time clean has no mechanism to hold, and nothing built so far is that mechanism. The reframe tells you why to clean, the list tells you what and who, the methods tell you how. Not one of them tells you when, or how often, or what stops the slow slide back. A home is not a thing you clean; it is a thing you keep clean, and those are completely different problems.
- Coverage: one hold on the clean home; one slow drift across the time-lapse back toward cluttered; one move along the why, what, who, how cards to the blank "when" card.
- **On-screen text moment:** "The reframe, the list, the methods: why, what, who, how." then "Not one of them tells you when."
- **Lower third:** A home is a thing you keep clean, not a thing you clean once

### Shot 3: Why homes drift (small and constant)
- A beach at the edge of the frame filling with footprints one at a time, all day, until the smooth sand is gone and no single step was to blame, dissolving into a home collecting its drift the same way, one mug, one coat, one pile, at a time. Beside it, a second, kinder panel: an enormous vague card reading "clean the house" that is too big to pick up, sliding to a "someday" tray, next to a small definite card reading "fifteen minutes" that a hand lifts easily.
- The point: a home does not get messy in one big event you could brace for; it gets messy a little at a time, so a big clean every few weeks is the wrong tool for a small daily drift. And the kinder reason: "clean the house" is too big and vague to ever start, so it gets postponed, not because anyone does not care but because the job as pictured is too big to begin. Shrink the job small enough and the not-starting simply stops.
- Coverage: one hold on the beach filling with footprints; one dissolve into the home drifting the same way; one move from the enormous "clean the house" card sliding to "someday" to the small "fifteen minutes" card lifted easily.
- **On-screen text moment:** "A home drifts, a little at a time." then "Clean the house is too big to start. Fifteen minutes is not."
- **Lower third:** Small daily drift needs a small daily reset

### Shot 4: The 15-minute reset (the device)
- The whole household mid-reset, energetic and calm at once. A large timer on the wall or phone reads 15:00, mid countdown. In each zone a different member of the household is doing a quick light pass at the same moment. Green calm-dots bloom in each finished spot. On a card beside it, the definition, and beneath it, three words: a little, often, together.
- The point: you take the vague, enormous job of keeping the house clean and replace it with a small, definite, repeatable one, a fifteen-minute reset. At a set time everyone spends fifteen minutes, and only fifteen, returning their zones to baseline with the methods they already have, reading as they go, until the timer rings, then they stop. It is not a deep clean and does not try to be. Because it happens often it never has to do much; because it never has to do much it stays short; because it stays short it actually happens.
- Coverage: one wide of the whole home mid-reset with the timer counting down; one push across three zones with calm-dots blooming; one hold on the definition card and the three words beneath it.
- **On-screen text moment:** "The 15-minute reset: short, shared, regular." then "A little, often, together."
- **Lower third:** Return every zone to baseline, without an afternoon

### Shot 5: Set a timer, not a standard (the little)
- A single small timer, drawn larger than it should be to say it does more work than it looks like it should. On the left, the old way: an open-ended clean with no end in sight, a clock spinning, a person frozen at the doorway, unwilling to start because they cannot see the end. On the right, the reset: the same doorway, a timer set to fifteen minutes, the person stepping in easily, because the end is visible from the beginning.
- The point: the timer is not a detail; it is the device. The reason a clean gets postponed is almost never the cleaning itself; it is the fear of the open-ended, of not knowing whether starting means twenty minutes or all afternoon. A timer removes that fear: fifteen minutes is fifteen minutes. The old way aims at a standard, a level of done, and takes unknowable, dreadable amounts of time. The reset aims at a duration and accepts whatever level of done fifteen shared minutes produces, which is plenty, because you are only ever undoing one day of drift. Stopping when the timer rings, even mid-task, is not a compromise; it is the mechanism.
- Coverage: one hold on the timer set to fifteen minutes; one push from the open-ended clean and the frozen person on the left to the timed reset and the person stepping in on the right; one on the timer ringing and the person stopping mid-wipe, unbothered.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Set a timer, not a standard." then "Set the timer, and stop when it rings."
- **Lower third:** A hard stop is what makes people willing to start

### Shot 6: Everyone, together (the shared reset)
- A cutaway of the whole home mid-reset, each member in a different zone doing a quick light pass at the same moment: a child returning shoes to the launch pad and wiping a low handle, an adult clearing and wiping the kitchen counter, a teenager carrying two mugs to the dishwasher, someone straightening the living room. Small green calm-dots bloom in each finished spot. Off to one side, a faint greyed-out inset of a single lone person facing a long solo clean, the thing the shared reset replaces.
- The point: fifteen minutes is nothing on your own and a great deal shared out. Four people resetting together for a quarter of an hour is a full hour of cleaning that costs each of them only fifteen minutes, and it lands all at once, so the whole home returns to baseline in the time it takes one person to clean a single room. This is where Chapter 17's assignments finally pay their way: everyone goes to their own targets at the same moment, so nobody waits, nobody supervises, and nobody carries the whole home alone. A reset done together is not just faster; it is fairer, and the fairness is what makes people keep showing up for it.
- Coverage: one wide of all four zones moving at once; one push across each person at their own target; one on the greyed-out lone cleaner inset, still on the first room.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Four people, fifteen minutes: a full hour of cleaning, all at once." then "Not just faster. Fairer."
- **Lower third:** Fifteen shared minutes reset the whole home

### Shot 7: On a rhythm, and a reset in action (the often)
- A daily-cue strip: after dinner, before bed, the moment the kids' show ends, each with the fifteen-minute reset hung on it like a coat on a hook, borrowing that thing's reliability. Then the worked pass: it is just after dinner, someone sets the timer and says the word, and everyone moves. The kitchen, plates into the dishwasher, counter cleared and wiped, a glance at the hob. A child on the launch pad and the low handles. Someone on the living room, someone on the bathroom sink. Quick light passes reading the surfaces as they go. The timer rings; everyone stops, wherever they are; the home is back at baseline, all of it, at once.
- The point: the trick is not discipline; it is attachment. Anchor the reset to something you already do without thinking, and it stops being a decision and becomes simply what happens at that time. Decisions are fragile and a busy week breaks them; habits attached to daily cues survive busy weeks. Keep it a reset, not a deep clean: it returns the home to its baseline, it is not the time to scrub the oven, and kept short and light, the deep cleans grow rare. That is the deal: fifteen minutes a day, and you get your weekends back.
- Coverage: one hold on the daily-cue strip with the reset hung on each cue; one move across the four zones mid-reset reading as they go; one on the timer ringing and everyone stopping at once; one wide of the home back at baseline.
- **On-screen text moment:** "Anchor it to a daily cue. A habit, not a decision." then "Fifteen minutes a day, and you get your weekends back."
- **Lower third:** Reset, do not deep-clean
