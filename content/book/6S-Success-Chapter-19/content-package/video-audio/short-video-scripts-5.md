## SHORT 1: Clean once is not clean kept
**Topic: a one-time clean drifts back, because keeping clean is a different problem. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** You cleaned the whole house. In a week it looks like you never did.

**Script:**
You did everything right. You cleaned to see, you shared the work, you gave every target a quick method, and you cleaned the whole home end to end. And if you stop there, in about a week it will look almost exactly as it did before. Not because any of the work was wrong. Because a home is not a thing you clean; it is a thing you keep clean, and those are completely different problems. A one-time clean, however good, is a photograph. What you actually live in is the film, and the film drifts. The mug lands on the counter, the coat misses the hook, the mail begins its pile, and each is nothing, and together, over a week of nothings, they undo an afternoon of work. Every home that has slid from clean back to cluttered slid exactly this way, a little at a time, while nobody did anything wrong. So the last piece is not another way to clean. It is a way to keep clean.

## SHORT 2: Why homes drift
**Topic: the drift is small and constant, and "clean the house" is too big to start. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Your house does not get messy all at once. That is exactly the problem.

**Script:**
To keep a home clean you have to see how it gets messy, and the key is the shape of it: small, and constant. A home does not get messy in one big event you could brace for. It gets messy the way a beach fills with footprints, one at a time, all day, until the smooth sand is gone and no single step was to blame. And a problem that arrives a little at a time has to be met a little at a time. A big clean every few weeks is the wrong tool for a small daily drift, the way an occasional enormous meal is the wrong answer to daily hunger. There is a kinder reason too. "Clean the house" is enormous and vague, and enormous vague tasks do not get started; they get postponed, waiting for a free afternoon that never quite comes. It is not that people do not care. The job as they picture it is too big to begin. Shrink the job small enough and the not-starting simply stops.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "A home drifts a little at a time."
- 14s: "Like a beach filling with footprints."
- 28s: "Clean the house is too big to start."
- 40s: "Shrink the job, and the not-starting stops."

## SHORT 3: The 15-minute reset
**Topic: the hero device, short, shared, regular, and the rule. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Here is the whole thing that keeps a home clean. It takes fifteen minutes.

**Script:**
Here is the whole device, almost too simple to trust until you have done it a few times. You take the vague, enormous job of keeping the house clean and replace it with a small, definite, repeatable one: a fifteen-minute reset. At a set time, everyone spends fifteen minutes, and only fifteen, returning their zones to baseline with the methods they already have, reading as they go, until the timer rings. Then you stop. It is not a deep clean and it is not trying to be. It is a light touch that undoes the day's drift before it can accumulate. Because it happens often, it never has to do much; because it never has to do much, it stays short; because it stays short, it actually happens. Those three facts hold each other up. And that is the rule, three words, each one load-bearing: a little, often, together. A little, so the job is small enough to start. Often, so the drift never gets ahead. Together, so it is fast and fair.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**

- 12s: "Everyone. Fifteen minutes. Then stop."
- 30s: "Often, so it never has to do much."
- 42s: "A little, often, together."

## SHORT 4: Set a timer, not a standard
**Topic: the timer is the device, because a hard stop makes people willing to start. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The reason you never start cleaning is not the cleaning. It is not knowing when it ends.

**Script:**
The little rests on one small object that does more work than it looks like it should: a timer. And the timer is not a detail of the reset; it is the device. Here is why. The reason a clean gets postponed is almost never the cleaning itself. It is the fear of the open-ended, the sense that once you start you are in it until some vague standard of done, which could be twenty minutes or all afternoon, and not knowing which is exactly what makes you not start. A timer removes that fear. Fifteen minutes is fifteen minutes; it will be over, guaranteed, and you can see the end from the beginning. The old way aims at a standard and works until it gets there, which is why it takes unknowable, dreadable amounts of time. The reset aims at a duration and accepts whatever fifteen shared minutes produces, which is plenty, because you are only ever undoing one day of drift. So: set the timer, and stop when it rings. Stopping mid-task is not a compromise. It is the mechanism.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "Set a timer, not a standard."
- 14s: "A hard stop is what makes people willing to start."
- 28s: "Fifteen minutes is fifteen minutes."
- 40s: "Set the timer, and stop when it rings."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A single timer drawn larger than it should be. On the left, an open-ended clean with no end in sight, a spinning clock, a person frozen at the doorway. On the right, the same doorway, a timer set to fifteen minutes, the person stepping in easily. Warm daylight.
