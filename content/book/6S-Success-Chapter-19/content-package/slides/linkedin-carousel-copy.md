### Slide 1 (hook)

**You cleaned the whole house last week. Give it seven days and it is almost back.**

Not because anyone stopped caring, and not because the cleaning was wrong. Because "a home is not a thing you clean; it is a thing you keep clean, and those are completely different problems."

You have the reframe, the list, and the methods. The one thing missing is what stops the slow slide back. Swipe for the piece that keeps a home without costing anyone an afternoon.

### Slide 2

**A home does not get messy in one big event you could brace for.**

It gets messy the way a beach fills with footprints: one at a time, all day, until the smooth sand is gone and no single step was to blame.

"A one-time clean, however good, is a photograph. What you actually live in is the film, and the film drifts." The mug lands, the coat misses the hook, the mail begins its pile, and each is nothing. Together, over a week of nothings, they undo an afternoon of work.

### Slide 3

**Fight a slow daily drift with a rare heroic clean and you will always be behind.**

"A home does not get messy in one big event. It drifts, a little at a time, so the thing that keeps it is not a big clean now and then but a small reset, often. Fight a slow daily drift with a rare heroic clean and you will always be behind; meet it with a short daily reset and you are never more than fifteen minutes from baseline."

There is a kinder reason too: "clean the house" is too big and too vague to ever start, so it waits for a free afternoon that never comes. Shrink the job small enough and the not-starting simply stops.

### Slide 4

**The whole device is almost too simple to trust until you have done it a few times.**

You replace the vague, enormous job of keeping the house clean with a small, definite, repeatable one: a fifteen-minute reset.

"The 15-minute reset is a short, time-boxed pass through the home, done together on a regular rhythm, that returns every zone to its clean and read baseline, so the whole house stays cared for without anyone ever having to set aside an afternoon for it."

It is not a deep clean and it is not trying to be. It is a light touch that undoes the day's drift before it can accumulate.

### Slide 5

**Three words, and each one is load-bearing.**

"A little, often, together."

A little, so the job is small enough to start. Often, so the drift never gets ahead. Together, so it is fast and fair.

"Because it happens often, it never has to do much; because it never has to do much, it stays short; because it stays short, it actually happens." Those three facts hold each other up, and together they keep a home in a way a one-time clean never can.

### Slide 6

**Set the timer, not a standard.**

"Set the timer, and stop when it rings."

The reason a clean gets postponed is almost never the cleaning; it is the fear of the open-ended, of not knowing whether you are in it for twenty minutes or all afternoon. A timer removes that fear completely.

"The timer is the whole device, because a hard stop is what makes people willing to start. An open-ended clean is the dreaded afternoon all over again; a clean that is definitely over in fifteen minutes is one anyone will begin, and a clean you will begin is the only kind that keeps a home."

[VISUAL] The big slate #3C5A6B timer again, its ring nearly run down, a clear hard-stop mark at zero. An old open-ended clean fades beside it in ghost #8A8073, its clock running off the edge of the card with no end in sight. Slate #3C5A6B label: "a duration, not a standard."

### Slide 7

**Fifteen minutes is nothing on your own and a great deal shared out.**

"Fifteen minutes is nothing on your own and a great deal shared out. Four people resetting together for a quarter of an hour is a full hour of cleaning that costs each of them only fifteen minutes, and it lands all at once, so the whole home returns to baseline in the time it takes one person to clean a single room."

Everyone goes to their own targets at the same moment, so nobody waits, nobody supervises, nobody carries the home alone. "A reset done together is not just faster; it is fairer, and the fairness is what makes people keep showing up for it."

### Slide 8

**The trick is not discipline. It is attachment.**

Anchor the reset to something you already do without thinking, and it borrows that thing's reliability. After dinner. Last thing before bed. The moment the kids' show ends.

Pick a cue that happens every day whether you plan it or not, and hang the fifteen minutes on it, so the reset stops being a decision and becomes simply what happens at that time. "Habits attached to daily cues survive busy weeks, because they were never really decisions."

[VISUAL] A simple week strip in slate #3C5A6B, seven days, each carrying a small "after dinner" anchor marker with a green calm-dot #6E8B5B reset hung beneath it, the same moment every day. A flat, low green #6E8B5B baseline runs under the whole week. Label: "hung on a cue you already keep."

### Slide 9

**Fifteen minutes a day, and you get your weekends back.**

When a short reset runs every day, the drift never accumulates, the big jobs never build up, and the dreaded whole-Saturday clean simply stops being necessary.

"A little and often does not just replace the occasional heroic clean; it dissolves the need for it."

6S Tip: "Reset, do not deep-clean. The reset returns the home to its baseline; it is not the time to scrub the oven. Keep it short and light and the deep cleans grow rare, because nothing ever gets far enough gone to need one."

WITH a short daily reset, a green #6E8B5B line stays low and nearly flat near a dashed baseline. Slate #3C5A6B axes. The 6S Tip in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout.

### Slide 10

**Just after dinner, someone sets the timer and says the word, and everyone moves.**

You take the kitchen: plates in, counter wiped, a glance at the hob, the drift of the meal gone in four minutes. A child takes the launch pad and the low handles. Someone takes the living room, someone the bathroom sink. Nobody is deep cleaning; everyone is undoing today's drift with the quick methods they already know, reading as they go.

The timer rings. You stop, wherever you are. And you look up at a home that fifteen minutes ago had started to slide, and is now back at baseline, all of it, at once.
