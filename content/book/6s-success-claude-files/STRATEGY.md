# 6S Success Strategy

> Strategic priorities, choices, sequencing, growth thesis, and decision guardrails for 6S Success.

## 1. Purpose

`STRATEGY.md` defines **how 6S Success intends to win and what the autonomous organization should prioritize now**.

Read with:

- `CLAUDE.md` — operating constitution
- `AUTONOMY.md` — execution authority
- `STATUS.md` — current state
- `BUSINESS.md` — business definition

This file should change less often than the backlog and more often than the core business definition.

Strategy is a set of choices.

It is not a list of everything the company could possibly build.

---

# 2. Strategic Objective

Build 6S Success into a trusted, scalable household-improvement platform that turns frustrating rooms and micro-zones into functional, maintainable systems through:

**Personal Values**
→ **Desired Function**
→ **Root-Cause Diagnosis**
→ **Short Quests**
→ **Visible Standards**
→ **Sustainment**
→ **Relevant Products and Services**

Commercial objective:

Develop a credible path to **$20,000+ in monthly revenue** with healthy economics and repeatable customer value.

This is a target, not a guarantee.

---

# 3. Strategic Thesis

Most home organization products begin too late.

They begin with:

**"What container should you buy?"**

6S Success should begin with:

**"What do you want this space to do?"**

The strategic advantage is the diagnostic and improvement system connecting:

- household values
- room function
- micro-zone function
- friction
- root causes
- 6S activities
- quests
- standards
- sustainment
- products

The moat should increasingly come from the **connected system and accumulated learning**, not from any individual card, article, bin, label, or feature.

---

# 4. Strategic Position

6S Success should occupy the space between:

- home organization
- cleaning
- household routines
- functional design
- family coordination
- continuous improvement
- gamified household action
- digital inventory
- practical organization products

Do not position the brand as Lean consulting for homeowners.

Lean principles should power the system invisibly.

Customers should experience simplicity, not methodology.

---

# 5. Strategic Differentiators

Build around five differentiators.

## 5.1 Function Before Organization

Determine what the space should accomplish before reorganizing it.

## 5.2 Micro-Zone Precision

Break overwhelming rooms into small functional systems.

## 5.3 Root Cause Before Product

Diagnose why the problem keeps returning.

## 5.4 Quest-Based Action

Convert recommendations into achievable 15–90 minute activities.

## 5.5 Sustainment

Define a simple standard so the improvement survives after the initial reset.

These should be evident across content, decks, software, products, and services.

# 7. Why Entryway First

Entryway is strategically attractive because it is:

- frequently used
- easy to understand
- visibly affected by friction
- naturally divided into micro-zones
- relevant to individuals and families
- connected to daily routines
- suited to short quests
- suited to cards
- suited to labels
- suited to organization products
- suited to visual before/after evidence

It also connects to a strong functional promise:

**Leave home more easily and return home with less friction.**

---

# 8. Entryway Success Criteria

Do not declare Entryway validated merely because the pages or cards exist.

Evidence should eventually demonstrate some combination of:

- people understand the desired-function concept
- people can identify relevant micro-zones
- people complete quests
- quests create visible/functional improvements
- customers understand root causes
- customers establish standards
- customers return to additional micro-zones
- users invite or involve household members
- some users purchase relevant products
- organic discovery begins producing qualified traffic
- customers report that improvements persist

Exact thresholds belong in `METRICS.md` and experiment plans.

# 12. WIP Strategy

Maintain a maximum of **three major workstreams** unless explicitly changed.

Recommended current workstreams:

1. Autonomous operating/data foundation
2. GitHub/production reliability
3. Entryway customer and monetization loop

Small maintenance tasks do not necessarily count as major workstreams.

Do not allow autonomous agents to create uncontrolled parallel initiatives.

---

# 13. Build vs Learn

Early strategy should favor learning speed over feature volume.

Ask:

**What is the smallest implementation that can answer the strategic question?**

Examples:

Instead of building 20 room decks:

Build one Entryway deck and measure use.

Instead of manufacturing 50 products:

Offer/test a small set mapped to common root causes.

Instead of building a complex recommendation AI:

Start with explainable rules and collect evidence.

---

# 14. Product Strategy

The product should progressively become a **household improvement operating system**.

Core object model:

**Household**
→ **Room**
→ **Micro-Zone**
→ **Desired Function**
→ **Friction**
→ **Root Cause**
→ **Activity**
→ **Quest**
→ **Standard**
→ **Sustainment**
→ **Product**

Design software and structured content around reusable objects rather than disconnected pages.

---

# 15. Personalization Strategy

Personalization should begin with useful, explainable variables.

Prioritize:

- desired function
- values
- micro-zone
- friction
- root cause
- available time
- number of players
- household constraints

Avoid opaque personalization when simple rules work.

Over time, observed outcomes may improve recommendations.

---

# 16. Recommendation Strategy

Recommendations should answer:

**Why this?**

Preferred recommendation logic:

**Observed Problem**
+ **Desired Outcome**
+ **Root Cause**
+ **Constraint**
→ **Recommended Quest / Standard / Product**

Every commercial recommendation should be explainable in customer language.

---

# 17. Card Strategy

Cards are both:

- physical/digital products
- interaction primitives

A card should represent useful action or understanding.

Potential card classes:

- room
- micro-zone
- desired function
- root cause
- 6S activity
- quest
- challenge
- team
- safety
- standard
- sustainment
- tool/product

Use shared IDs so cards can connect physical and digital experiences.

---

# 18. Quest Strategy

Quests should create fast, visible progress.

Prioritize:

- clear target
- short duration
- obvious completion
- meaningful outcome
- low setup
- cooperative potential
- repeatability where appropriate

A quest should not exist solely to earn points.

The real reward is a better-functioning space.

---

# 19. Multi-Player Strategy

Household collaboration can become a major differentiator.

Support:

- voluntary card selection
- card assignment
- shared quest
- timed event
- role selection
- parallel micro-zone work
- cooperative completion

For 1–10 players, avoid making the experience dependent on having a group.

Single-player must remain strong.

---

# 20. Gamification Strategy

Use game mechanics to reduce resistance to household work.

Potential mechanics:

- quests
- progress
- completion
- unlocks
- room maps
- team missions
- random card draws
- challenge modes
- streaks where healthy
- before/after evidence

Avoid:

- manipulative engagement loops
- meaningless point inflation
- excessive notifications
- punishment for missed household tasks

# 21. Content Strategy

Preferred architecture:

**Room**
↔ **Micro-Zone**
↔ **Desired Function**
↔ **Problem**
↔ **Root Cause**
↔ **Quest**
↔ **Standard**
↔ **Product**

# 22. SEO Strategy

Organic search should be built around high-intent, useful household questions.

Prioritize:

1. technical crawl/index health
2. strong room hubs
3. strong micro-zone pages
4. root-cause/problem pages
5. practical direct answers
6. internal relationships
7. structured data where valid
8. iterative improvement based on Search Console evidence

Do not chase page count.

---

# 23. AEO Strategy

Make important concepts easy for answer engines to interpret.

Pages should clearly state:

- what the thing is
- who it helps
- what problem it solves
- why the problem occurs
- recommended steps
- safety limitations
- related concepts

Use clear headings and concise answers.

Do not fabricate citations, authority, or statistics.

# 27. Digital-First Economics

- low marginal cost
- rapid iteration

# 28. Physical Product Strategy

Physical products should solve specific root causes.

Examples:

**Keys lack a home**
→ key tray / hook system

**Cable reset is difficult**
→ cable organizer

**Small items migrate**
→ divided module

**Replenishment is invisible**
→ min/max visual control

Avoid generic products with weak system connection.

---

# 29. 3D Printing Strategy

Use 3D printing initially for:

- rapid prototyping
- small-batch testing
- specialized micro-zone solutions
- Gridfinity-compatible modules
- product learning

Do not assume 3D printing is the best scaled manufacturing method.

Use evidence from demand and economics.

# 37. Experiment Strategy

Every major strategic uncertainty should become a measurable experiment.

High-value questions include:

- Do people understand desired-function discovery?
- Does root-cause diagnosis improve quest selection?
- Are 15-minute quests more likely to complete?
- Does multi-player increase completion?
- Which Entryway micro-zone drives the most demand?
- Will users pay for a digital deck?
- Will users pay more for a physical deck?
- Do relevant product recommendations convert without reducing trust?
- Does a completed quest increase next-room intent?

---

# 38. Experiment Portfolio

Balance experiments across:

- acquisition
- activation

- retention

# 39. Kill Criteria

Autonomous systems need permission to stop weak ideas.

Consider stopping or redesigning an initiative when:

- customers do not use it
- repeated experiments fail
- operational burden exceeds value
- economics are poor
- safety risk is disproportionate
- better alternatives exist
- strategic fit is weak

Do not preserve features merely because effort was invested.

---

# 40. Expansion Gate

Before adding a major new room, ask:

1. Is Entryway architecture stable enough to reuse?
2. Do we understand the required data model?
3. Are quests measurable?
4. Are root causes reusable?
5. Are standards reusable?
6. Is there evidence of demand for the next room?
7. Can content/products be produced without creating a parallel system?

If not, improve the foundation first.

# 68. What Winning Looks Like

A strong future customer journey looks like:

**"My entryway is always a mess."**

→ 6S Success identifies what the customer needs the entryway to accomplish.

→ The customer identifies the shoe zone as the biggest friction point.

→ The system identifies excess quantity and poor access as likely causes.

→ The customer selects a 30-minute quest.

→ Household members participate.

→ Shoes are sorted and assigned a functional capacity/location.

→ A simple standard is established.

→ The system recommends a product only if it materially helps.

→ The customer returns later to improve the mail zone.

→ Eventually the household progresses to another room.

That is the business model expressed as customer value.

# 70. Final Strategic Principle

Do not attempt to become the largest home-organization website first.

Become the **most useful system for turning a frustrating household micro-zone into a functional, sustainable one**.

Then repeat that system.

**Depth before breadth.**

**Diagnosis before recommendation.**

**Outcome before engagement.**

**Evidence before scale.**

**Customer trust before monetization pressure.**

**Repeatable system before expansion.**
