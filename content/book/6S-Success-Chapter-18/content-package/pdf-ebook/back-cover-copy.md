# Back Cover Copy: Cleaning Methods That Actually Work

## Back-cover text

You have a list now, and every line on it has a name. That is further than most homes ever get, and it is still not a clean house, because between a named target and a cleaned one sits a small, stubborn gap, and the gap is the how. A job can be perfectly assigned to a perfectly willing person and still not happen, for the oldest reason there is: it is a pain to do. Nobody skips the bathroom because they forgot whose job it was. They skip it because, done the way they know how, it takes forty minutes and a cupboard of sprays and leaves them on their knees, so it quietly slides to next weekend. "The reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy, or busy, or disorganized. It is that the method you are using is slow and horrible, and a slow horrible job is one any sane person postpones."

Chapter 18 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the third chapter of Part Five, and Shine continues. It does not remake the list and it reassigns no one; it closes the gap between a target that is owned and a target that is clean. A method that works has to do two things at once. "A working method is the quickest effective way to clean and read a given target: effective enough that the surface actually comes clean and shows what it needs, and easy enough that you will do it again without dreading it." Miss either half and the method fails, so the one you want sits in the small overlap of the two, which points at the single most useful rule in this part of the book: "The best method is the one you will actually do." The chapter hands you five working methods that lift almost any target into that overlap: right tool for the surface; top to bottom, dry before wet; fewer, simpler products; keep the tool where the target is; and clean in passing.

Then it says the kind thing almost no one says out loud. "You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem. The jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in themselves; they are jobs done the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost always to find the method that makes the job small." You are not lazy and you are not failing; you have been handed slow methods, and the moment the method gets quick the avoidance stops on its own. The speed never costs you the reading either. "A working method still leaves you having looked. Do not spray a surface and walk away; the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one, so the best method cleans the target and shows you its condition in the same easy pass." Because the targets on your list actually get cleaned this chapter, the friction meter follows the dashed arrow the last chapter drew and takes its next Shine step toward calm, honest, with the goal still ahead. Take the one cleaning job you dread most and find a faster method for it: a better tool, fewer products, a different order. Do it once the new way and time it. "Every target on your list has a method you will actually do."
