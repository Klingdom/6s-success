# Cleaning Methods That Actually Work

**Part Five, Shine**
Chapter 18 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

Subtitle · You know what to clean and who cleans it, but a target you dread is a target that waits. This chapter gives each one a method that is quick enough to actually do and good enough to actually work, so the list gets done instead of postponed.

---

## Chapter descriptor

The third chapter of Part Five, and Shine continues. Chapter 16 reframed cleaning as inspection and did one first pass; Chapter 17 built the Shine list, targets by kind with a name against every line, and held the meter with a dashed forward projection. But a list is not a clean house, because between a named target and a cleaned one sits the how, and a job that is a pain to do quietly slides to next weekend no matter whose name is next to it. Today you close that gap. You learn what makes a method actually work, that it must be both effective and easy, and you pick up five working methods that lift almost any target into that overlap: right tool for the surface; top to bottom, dry before wet; fewer, simpler products; keep the tool where the target is; and clean in passing. Nothing is remade and no one is reassigned; today you give each target a way of being cleaned that is quick enough to do and good enough to work.

## How to read this chapter

This is the third chapter of Part Five, Shine, and it picks up exactly where the last one left off, with a Shine list built, grouped by kind, a name against every line, and several of those lines already filling you with a small sinking feeling because you know how they go. It is not about remaking that list; the what and the who were settled last chapter. It is about one job: giving each target a working method, so a target that is owned finally becomes a target that is clean.

Read it through once for the idea, then do it on the list you already have. A method that works has to do two things at once. It has to be effective, meaning the target actually comes clean and you can read its condition afterward, and it has to be easy, meaning it is quick enough that you will genuinely do it again without a fight. Keep the rule that follows from that in front of you: the best method is the one you will actually do. Then run the five working methods across your dreaded targets, right tool for the surface, top to bottom and dry before wet, fewer and simpler products, the tool kept where the target is, and small cleans folded into moments you are already there. Give the launch pad a cloth in the entry drawer so wiping it is a ten-second thing you do in passing; swap the hour of caustic scrubbing on the oven for a gentler method left to sit while you do something else; wipe the door handles with the cloth already in your hand.

Keep the reading in as the speed arrives, because the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one; let your eyes cross the surface as your cloth does. Nothing here rebuilds the Shine list or reassigns owners; that was Chapter 17. Nothing here builds the repeatable routine or reset; clean in passing is a method for a single target, not the whole-home rhythm, and that rhythm is Chapter 19. Nothing here writes documented standards or schedules; those belong to Standardize and Sustain. Nothing re-opens Sort or Straighten. Because the targets actually get cleaned this chapter, the friction meter follows the dashed arrow the last chapter drew and takes its next Shine step, honestly, with the goal still ahead.

If you only do one thing: take the one cleaning job you dread most and find a faster method for it, a better tool, fewer products, a different order. Do it once the new way and time it. Dread usually turns out to be a method problem.
