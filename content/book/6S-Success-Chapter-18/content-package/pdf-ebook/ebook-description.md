# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

You have a list now, and every line on it has a name. That is further than most homes ever get, and it is still not a clean house, because between a named target and a cleaned one sits a small, stubborn gap, and the gap is the how. Chapter 18 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where a list that is owned finally becomes a home that is clean.

"Cleaning Methods That Actually Work" is the third chapter of Part Five, and Shine continues, the third of the six S's. It remakes nothing and reassigns no one; it changes one thing, the how. A job can be perfectly assigned to a perfectly willing person and still not happen, for the oldest reason there is: it is a pain to do. Nobody skips the bathroom because they forgot whose job it was. They skip it because, done the way they know how, it takes forty minutes and a cupboard of sprays and leaves them on their knees, so it quietly slides to next weekend, and the weekend after that. "The reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy, or busy, or disorganized. It is that the method you are using is slow and horrible, and a slow horrible job is one any sane person postpones." Change the method and the same job becomes a two-minute wipe you barely notice doing, and a two-minute wipe you barely notice is a job that actually gets done. The whole of this chapter lives in that swap.

The chapter names its fence plainly. It does not re-argue why we clean, which was Chapter 16; it does not rebuild the Shine list or reassign owners, which was Chapter 17; it does not build the repeatable routine or reset, which is Chapter 19; and it does not write documented standards or schedules, which belong to Standardize and Sustain. It does not re-open Sort or Straighten. Today has one job: give each target on the list a way of being cleaned that is quick enough to do and good enough to work.

Start where the last chapter left you, because it is a good place and an incomplete one. You have a Shine list, grouped by kind, with a name against every line, and if you are honest, several lines on it already fill you with a small sinking feeling, because you know how they go: the oven that means an hour and a caustic spray, the shower screen that never really comes clear, the floors that mean moving furniture. "The target is owned. It is also dreaded. And dread, quietly, beats ownership almost every time." That is the failure this chapter exists to prevent, because a plan that dies of dread dies just as dead as a plan with no names at all. The person who minds most starts quietly doing the dreaded ones anyway, and you are right back to one pair of hands carrying the load. "The list does not fail because it was unfair. It fails because the jobs on it were miserable, and miserable jobs do not get done no matter whose name is next to them." So the missing piece is not more willpower and it is not a stricter chart. It is a better way to do each job. "Give a dreaded target a method that is quick and works, and the dread simply drains out of it, because there is nothing left to dread."

A method that works has to do two things at once, and most cleaning advice only ever gives you one of them. It has to be effective, meaning the target actually comes clean and you can read its condition afterward. And it has to be easy, meaning it is quick and painless enough that you will genuinely do it again next time without a fight. A method that is easy but not effective is the spray-and-wipe that smears the grease around and leaves the hob no cleaner than it was; it gets done and it does nothing. A method that is effective but not easy is the full hour of scrubbing that leaves the oven gleaming and that you will not repeat until next year; it works once and never again. Neither is a working method. "A working method is the quickest effective way to clean and read a given target: effective enough that the surface actually comes clean and shows what it needs, and easy enough that you will do it again without dreading it." Which points at the single most useful rule in this part of the book: "The best method is the one you will actually do." Thorough is worth nothing if it does not happen, because the home is kept by what you actually do, not by what you know you should.

Then the chapter hands you a small kit of methods that move almost any target toward that corner. None is a trick or a special product. Right tool for the surface: most struggle at the sink is really the wrong tool applied with extra effort, so match the tool to the material and the muscle disappears. Top to bottom, dry before wet: order is free speed, so clean the high things before the low ones and do the dry jobs before the wet ones, or you dirty what you just cleaned and do half of it twice. Fewer, simpler products: a small handful covers almost everything, costs less, is safer around children, and is simple enough that you actually restock it and actually use it. Keep the tool where the target is: a cloth kept by the bathroom sink gets used on the bathroom sink, and storing each target's tool at the target removes the largest hidden cost of cleaning, which is the effort of starting. And clean in passing: a quick wipe of the shower wall while the water is still running, the counter while the pasta boils, the sink after you brush, folded into a moment you were spending anyway.

Stop for a moment on what these methods quietly imply, because it is kinder than the story most people tell themselves. "You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem. The jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in themselves; they are jobs done the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost always to find the method that makes the job small." If you have spent years feeling vaguely bad about the state of some corner of your home, this is worth letting land. You are not lazy and you are not failing. You have been handed slow methods, by habit, by how you were taught, and slow methods make jobs that any reasonable person avoids. "The moment the method gets quick, the avoidance stops on its own, without any change in your character." Nobody has to become a more disciplined person to keep a clean home. They just have to stop using the forty-minute method for a two-minute job.

One caution, so the speed does not cost you the thing Shine was for in the first place. It is possible to get so good at fast that you clean without looking, and a target cleaned blind is a target you did not inspect. "A working method still leaves you having looked. Do not spray a surface and walk away; the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one, so the best method cleans the target and shows you its condition in the same easy pass." Keeping the reading in costs no time; it is a matter of attention, not effort. As your cloth crosses the surface, let your eyes cross it too. The quick methods actually help here, because a method that puts your hand and eye right on the target inspects by its nature.

Do it once, on the list you already have. Give the launch pad a single cloth kept in the entry drawer, right where the target is, so wiping the shelf and checking the hooks is a ten-second thing you do in passing. Take the oven and swap the hour of caustic scrubbing for a gentler method left to sit while you do something else, fewer products, far less effort, the same clean result. Wipe the door handles with the cloth already in your hand as you pass, read in the same second. Nothing on the list changed; each target simply got a way of being cleaned that is quick enough to actually do and good enough to actually work. "A rare heroic scrub loses to a small easy pass every time, because the small easy pass actually happens. Cleaning that is quick enough to fold into an ordinary day stops being an event you postpone and becomes a background hum that keeps the whole home clean and read."
