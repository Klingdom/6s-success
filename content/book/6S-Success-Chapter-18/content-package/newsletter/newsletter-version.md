# Newsletter Edition: The Best Method Is the One You Will Actually Do

**Subject line (placeholder):** The best method is the one you will actually do.

**Preview text (placeholder):** You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem.

---

Hi friend,

Last issue you built the plan. You listed what needs cleaning across the whole home, grouped it by kind so nothing hid, and put a name against every line. That is further than most homes ever get, and it is still not a clean house, because between a named target and a cleaned one sits a small, stubborn gap, and the gap is the how.

A job can be perfectly assigned to a perfectly willing person and still not happen, for the oldest reason there is: it is a pain to do. Nobody skips the bathroom because they forgot whose job it was. They skip it because, done the way they know how, it takes forty minutes and a cupboard of sprays and leaves them on their knees, so it quietly slides to next weekend, and the weekend after that.

Here is the thing almost no one says out loud about cleaning. The reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy, or busy, or disorganized. It is that the method you are using is slow and horrible, and a slow horrible job is one any sane person postpones. Change the method and the same job becomes a two-minute wipe you barely notice doing, and a two-minute wipe you barely notice is a job that actually gets done. The whole of this issue lives in that swap.

So today we take the list you already have and give each target something it has been missing: a method quick enough that you will actually do it and good enough that it actually works. We are not remaking the list, and we are not reassigning anyone. We are closing the gap between a target that is owned and a target that is clean.

Start with the honest problem. Several lines on that list already fill you with a small sinking feeling, because you know how they go: the oven that means an hour and a caustic spray, the shower screen that never really comes clear, the floors that mean moving furniture. The target is owned. It is also dreaded. And dread, quietly, beats ownership almost every time. All the fairness you built into the list evaporates the first week if half the targets are jobs people cannot face. The list does not fail because it was unfair. It fails because the jobs on it were miserable, and miserable jobs do not get done no matter whose name is next to them.

So the missing piece is not more willpower and it is not a stricter chart. It is a better way to do each job. And a method that works has to do two things at once, where most cleaning advice only ever gives you one. It has to be effective, meaning the target actually comes clean and you can read its condition afterward. And it has to be easy, meaning it is quick and painless enough that you will genuinely do it again next time without a fight. Miss either half and the method fails, just in different ways.

**A working method is the quickest effective way to clean and read a given target: effective enough that the surface actually comes clean and shows what it needs, and easy enough that you will do it again without dreading it.**

Which points at the single most useful rule in this issue, and maybe in this whole part of the book.

**The best method is the one you will actually do.**

Read that against everything you have ever been told about cleaning, most of which is about the most thorough method, the deep clean, the proper way. Thorough is worth nothing if it does not happen. A merely good method you will run every week beats a perfect method you run once a year and then avoid, because the home is kept by what you actually do, not by what you know you should.

So here is a small kit of methods that move almost any target toward quick-and-good. None is a trick or a special product.

**Right tool for the surface.** Most struggle at the sink is really the wrong tool applied with extra effort. The right cloth, the right brush, the right cheap scraper does in one easy pass what a paper towel and determination do in ten.

**Top to bottom, dry before wet.** Order is free speed. Dust and crumbs fall, so clean the high things before the low ones, and do the dry jobs before the wet ones. Work the other way and you dirty what you just cleaned and do half of it twice.

**Fewer, simpler products.** A cupboard of specialist sprays is slower, not faster, because you spend the time choosing and reaching and running out. A small handful of simple products covers almost everything, costs less, is safer around children, and is simple enough that you actually restock it and actually use it.

**Keep the tool where the target is.** A cloth kept by the bathroom sink gets used on the bathroom sink; the same cloth kept in a cupboard down the hall does not. Storing each target's tool at the target removes the largest hidden cost of cleaning, which is the effort of starting.

**Clean in passing.** The cheapest clean is the one you never had to make time for, because you did it while you were already there: a quick wipe of the shower wall while the water is still running, the counter while the pasta boils, the sink after you brush.

Stop for a moment on what these methods quietly imply, because it is kinder than the story most people tell themselves.

**You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem. The jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in themselves; they are jobs done the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost always to find the method that makes the job small.**

If you have spent years feeling vaguely bad about the state of some corner of your home, this is worth letting land. You are not lazy and you are not failing. You have been handed slow methods, by habit, by how you were taught, by adverts selling effort and products, and slow methods make jobs that any reasonable person avoids. The moment the method gets quick, the avoidance stops on its own, without any change in your character. Nobody has to become a more disciplined person to keep a clean home. They just have to stop using the forty-minute method for a two-minute job.

One caution, so the speed does not cost you the thing Shine was for in the first place. It is possible to get so good at fast that you clean without looking, spraying and wiping on autopilot while your mind is elsewhere, and a target cleaned blind is a target you did not inspect.

**A working method still leaves you having looked. Do not spray a surface and walk away; the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one, so the best method cleans the target and shows you its condition in the same easy pass.**

The quick methods above actually help here, because a method that puts your hand and eye right on the target inspects by its nature. It is only the lazy shortcuts, the spray you never touch, the mop that never reaches the corners, that clean without reading. Choose methods that bring you into contact with the target, and the inspection comes free.

Now do it once, on the list you already have. Take the launch pad, old friends by now: give it a single cloth kept in the entry drawer, right where the target is, so wiping the shelf and checking the hooks is a ten-second thing you do in passing, not a job you schedule. Then take a target you dread, the oven, say, and swap the hour of caustic scrubbing for a gentler method left to sit while you do something else, so the work happens without you on your knees, fewer products, far less effort, the same clean result. Then the door handles, which need no method grander than the cloth already in your hand as you pass, wiped in a second, read in the same second. Nothing on the list changed. Each target just got a way of being cleaned that is quick enough to do and good enough to work. That is the whole chapter, applied: the same list, suddenly doable.

One more thing, since it saves you from the trap most plans fall into.

**Small, frequent, easy passes beat a rare heroic scrub, because the small easy thing actually happens.**

Then go and look at the friction meter, because the arrow the last issue drew has come true. Last time the needle held still, and a dashed arrow projected forward along the dial to show where it would move once the plan was actually carried out. This is that chapter. The targets got their methods and, doing them, they got cleaned, really cleaned, across the home and not just on one launch pad. So the needle follows the dashed arrow and steps again toward calm, and the step is honest, because this time the room's condition genuinely changed. Grease that was on the hob is gone. Handles that were grubby are clean and read. A dreaded oven is a done oven.

And, as ever, the honest boundary. This is a real step and it is not the finish of Shine. The goal, the crosshair out on the calm side, is still ahead, because a home cleaned once, however well, is not yet a home that stays clean. The needle stepped today. One more step finishes Shine.

Look at what Shine has now. A reason to clean, which is to see. A list of what to clean and who does it. And, as of today, a way to clean each target that is quick enough to do and good enough to work. The what, the who, and the how are all in place. Every target on your list has a method you will actually do.

**The best method is the one you will actually do. Match a quick, effective method to each target, keep it small enough to fold into an ordinary day, and Shine stops being a dreaded event and becomes a handful of easy passes that keep the whole home clean and read.**

**To try this week:** Take the one cleaning job you dread most and find a faster method for it: a better tool, fewer products, a different order. Do it once the new way and time it. Dread usually turns out to be a method problem.
