# Infographic Spec: The Working Method

**Chapter 18, "Cleaning Methods That Actually Work"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Five · Shine**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: a target you
dread is a target that waits, and the reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy but
that the method is slow and horrible, so this chapter gives each target a working method, one that is both
effective (it actually cleans the surface and lets you read it) and easy (quick enough that you will do it
again without dreading it). The concept pairs the hero device, **the working method**, with its frozen
rule, **The best method is the one you will actually do.**, and shows the two-axis picture at its heart:
the working method sits in the small corner where effective and easy overlap, and the two common failures,
only easy or only effective, both fall short. So the viewer sees the whole move at once, the test a method
has to pass, and the rule that decides it.

It stays consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_18_final.html`. Slate carries the rule,
the definition, the axes, the headings, and the tags; wood fill warms the small object glyphs; soft green
marks the working-method corner and its calm-dot; a single small warm red is held back for the one
friction-spark of wasted effort on the dreaded side and is used nowhere else on this calm image.
"Spotless" does not appear; the target is a home that is clean and read, kept by methods quick enough to
do and good enough to work, not a spotless one. Anti-shame throughout: a job you dread is a method
problem, not a character flaw, and the fix is a quicker method, not more willpower.

## Layout notes

- The rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the test the chapter makes: a method has to be
  both effective and easy, and the best one is simply the one you will actually do. The eye should read
  the rule, then see the two-axis picture prove it, the working method sitting where effective and easy
  overlap while the two failures fall short.
- The two-axis picture is the hero, so make the both-corner unmistakable: three plotted approaches and no
  more, the working method ringed with the one green calm-dot in the top-right corner where easy and
  effective meet. Keep the two failures calm and equal, neither dramatized; the whole-Saturday scrub is
  not a villain, it simply never repeats, and the spray-and-walk-away is not lazy, it simply does nothing.
- Hold the color discipline: the single small warm red is reserved for a friction-spark of wasted effort
  if the dreaded side is shown at all, and is used nowhere else; slate carries the rule, the definition,
  the axes, and the headings; soft green marks only the working-method corner and its calm-dot. This image
  is calm, so no fill-line red anywhere except that one small spark.
- This image makes the test, the working method and its rule. Do NOT re-argue why we clean (Chapter 16,
  done). Do NOT rebuild the Shine list or reassign owners (Chapter 17, done and closed); the methods apply
  to the list that already exists. Do NOT build a routine, reset, or schedule (Chapter 19); "clean in
  passing" is a method for a single target, not the whole-home routine. Do NOT build documented standards
  or maintenance systems (the later Standardize and Sustain S's); a working method is a practical
  technique, not a documented standard. And name no brand and draw no specific product; the glyphs are
  plain and generic, teaching principles, not a shopping list.
- Anti-shame: a job you dread is a method problem, not a character flaw, and the fix is a quicker method,
  not more willpower. The moment the method gets quick, the avoidance stops on its own, without any change
  in the reader's character. Keep the register relief and permission, no guilt cues, no pressure framing,
  no force framing anywhere. "Spotless" does not appear; the target is a home that is clean and read, kept
  by methods quick enough to do and good enough to work.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the axes drawn by hand
  rather than as a clinical chart, the glyphs sketched rather than typeset, the whole image the calm, warm
  register of a good idea rather than an instruction poster.
