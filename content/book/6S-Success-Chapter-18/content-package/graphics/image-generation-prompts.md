# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 18

**"Cleaning Methods That Actually Work"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Five · Shine*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to
hold across all five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`,
terracotta `#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B`, soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single warm
red `#CB4B36` reserved for rare small accents only. Natural soft daylight, shallow but not showy
depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the home the reader has built and listed across Chapters 12 to 17: the
settled launch pad by the door, and the whole-home Shine list from the last chapter, grouped by four
kinds of target with a name against every line. Chapter 17 made that list and its names but cleaned
nothing new, so it HELD the friction needle and drew a dashed forward projection. This chapter, the third
of Part Five, continues Shine and does one narrow thing: it gives each target on that list a working
method, a way to clean it that is quick enough to actually do and good enough to actually work, and,
doing them, the targets actually get cleaned across the home. Nothing on the list is rebuilt or
reassigned here (Chapter 17, done and closed; the methods apply to the list that already exists), no
repeatable routine, reset, or schedule is built (Chapter 19), and no documented standard or maintenance
system is written (the later Standardize and Sustain S's). Keep the tone warm and kind: a job you dread
is a method problem, not a character flaw, and the reader is not lazy, their method is just slow, and the
avoidance stops on its own the moment the method gets quick. The register is relief and permission, never
lecture, never guilt, never a scare tone. No force or violence framing anywhere, no attacking grime, no
war on dirt, no blitz, no blast, no purge. No hype words. Avoid brand names and specific product
endorsements; the bottles and cloths are plain and generic, teaching principles, not a shopping list.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical object
in the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real sheet, or the reading on a real clock
face, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental hand-lettered label: use the
middot, for example `CHAPTER 18 · SHINE`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot between words.
Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: point-of-use, dry-before-wet, two-minute, forty-minute,
whole-Saturday, whole-home, first-Shine-step, forward-projection, calm-dot, friction-spark.

## 1. Same target, two methods (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of one kitchen hob cleaned two ways, set as two facing scenes in soft
morning daylight. On the left, the dreaded way: a person on their knees scrubbing the hob hard with both
hands, a bucket beside them crowded with about ten plain spray bottles, a wall clock overhead showing a
long stretch of time gone, and a small quiet red spark of wasted effort hanging over the scene, the whole
side tiring rather than dirty. On the right, the same hob done the working way: the same person standing,
giving the hob one easy one-handed wipe with a single cloth, just two simple plain bottles on the counter,
a small clock showing only a couple of minutes, and a soft green dot marking the hob as clean and read.
Pinned to the wall between the two scenes is the household's handwritten Shine list from the last chapter,
a real sheet a little askew, with one line on it, the hob, circled in a soft hand-drawn ring. Warm paper
cream and honey wood tones, ink-brown shadows, one soft warm-red accent held only for the small spark on
the left. Editorial cookbook-style illustration. The same job, dreadful on one side and easy on the other,
with only the method changed.

- Mood: the same target and the same person, two methods; on the left the reason the job never gets done,
  on the right the reason it suddenly does, and the pinned list says this chapter is about doing the list,
  not remaking it; Shine does not need you to try harder, it needs the method on the right.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, floating typeset text overlays, any sense of filth,
  shame, or a scolded person, a dirty or neglected kitchen, force or violence cues, a warm-red accent
  sprayed anywhere but the one small spark on the left, brand names or recognizable product labels, a
  triumphant makeover feel on the right; keep both sides warm and matter-of-fact, the same hob and the
  same person throughout, and the circled line on the list an honest household mark.

## 2. A working method is both (the hero, effective and easy)

A warm editorial illustration of a calm two-axis picture drawn by hand rather than as a clinical chart,
showing how a working method is both effective and easy. The horizontal direction runs from hard on the
left to easy on the right; the vertical direction runs from a job that does not really clean at the bottom
to a job that actually cleans and lets you read the surface at the top. Three ordinary cleaning approaches
are placed on it as small hand-drawn scenes. Top-left, hard but effective: a whole-Saturday deep scrub,
thorough and gleaming but exhausting, the kind you do once and never repeat. Bottom-right, easy but
ineffective: a spray-and-walk-away, a quick mist over a surface nobody touches, done in a second and
changing nothing. Up in the top-right corner, both easy and effective, the working method, a simple
one-cloth wipe, ringed by a soft green calm-dot to mark it as the useful one, the small overlap where a
job is clean enough to matter and easy enough to repeat. The both-corner is gently washed in soft green.
Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, soft slate for the two directions, honey and green kept
quiet, friendly but not childish. A picture of one plain idea: the method that keeps a home clean sits
where effective and easy overlap.

- Mood: a method has to do two things at once, be effective and be easy, and most advice gives only one;
  the two common failures, only easy or only effective, both fall short, and the method that actually
  keeps a home clean sits in the corner that is both; the best method is the one you will actually do.
- Avoid: a busy clinical chart, more than three plotted approaches, a fourth axis or a numeric score, cold
  clinical light, floating typeset text, any spark red anywhere (the green calm-dot ring is the one
  accent), dramatizing either failure into a villain, brand names or specific products, harsh contrast;
  keep the two failures calm and equal and the working corner quietly marked, legible at a glance.

## 3. The five working methods (the reusable kit)

A warm editorial illustration of a calm reference card of the five working methods, loose and hand-made
rather than a clinical chart, arranged as five gentle rows, each a single small hand-drawn icon beside a
short hand-lettered label. The first row, right tool for the surface, shows a folded cloth beside a small
brush, matching the method to the material. The second, top to bottom, dry before wet, shows a soft
downward arrow between a high shelf and a low floor, the high dry work done before the low wet work. The
third, fewer, simpler products, shows just two plain capped bottles, a small handful rather than a
cupboard. The fourth, keep the tool where the target is, shows a cloth hanging on a hook right beside a
sink, supplies at the point of use. The fifth, clean in passing, shows a hand wiping a tiled shower wall
with a few soft water lines above it, a small clean folded into a moment you are already there. Warm paper
cream background, ink-brown line work, soft slate headings, honey and wood-fill warmth on the objects, no
single row louder than the rest. Documentary and hand-made, as if a household had sketched the five habits
that make cleaning quick, the kind of card you would pin up by the door.

- Mood: a small, reusable kit of five methods, any of which lifts almost any target toward quick-and-good;
  none is a trick or a special product, they are the handful of habits that separate cleaning that drags
  from cleaning that flies.
- Avoid: a busy clinical chart, more than five rows, cold clinical light, floating typeset text, any spark
  red anywhere on this calm card, one method drawn louder than the others, brand names or recognizable
  products, scrubbing drama; keep it to the five calm rows, one clear icon each, legible at a glance,
  every method drawn as an equal.

## 4. You do not have a cleaning problem (the reframe)

A calm, warm illustration of the same one person facing the same one dreaded job, read two ways, set as
two facing panels. On the left, the story people tell themselves: the person on their knees at a job that
seems to have no end, a cupboard of sprays open beside them, a long clock overhead, the whole scene
carrying the quiet, mistaken sense that they are simply bad at keeping a house. On the right, what is
actually true: the same person, the same job, but standing now, the wrong tool set down and a right one in
hand, the cupboard closed to two simple bottles, the long clock swapped for a short one, and a soft green
dot marking the job as small and done, the quiet, truer sense that the method was slow, not the person.
Between them, the plain feeling that only the method changed, not the person. Warm paper cream background,
honey light, ink-brown shadows, one soft warm-red note held only on the long clock in the left panel,
everything else calm. The image reads as one kind idea: the jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in
themselves, they are jobs done the slow way.

- Mood: you do not have a cleaning problem, you have a method problem; the jobs you dread are jobs done
  the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost
  always to find the method that makes the job small; the moment the method gets quick the avoidance stops
  on its own, without any change in your character; relief, not reproach.
- Avoid: a scare poster, guilt or shame cues, drawing the person as lazy, careless, or scolded, a
  triumphant makeover on the right, dramatic filth, a warm-red accent anywhere but the one long clock on
  the left, force framing, harsh contrast, floating typeset text; keep the person and the job identical
  across the panels, the same scene told as a fault and then as a method, warm and kind throughout.

## 5. The needle steps again (the friction meter, MOVES)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge dialing
from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right. This chapter the
dark live needle MOVES: it follows the single dashed forward-projection arrow the last chapter drew and
steps a clear, honest distance further toward the calm side, because this time the targets on the list
actually got cleaned across the home and the room's condition genuinely changed. Just behind the live
needle, where it rested last chapter, sits a faint dashed ghost marking Chapter 16 and 17's position, so
the eye reads the ground this step covered. Chapter 15's Straighten-complete milestone is a small slate
marker further back; fainter dashed ghost needles mark Chapters 14, 13, and 12 further back in order, and
a slate marker for the earlier Sort-complete milestone sits furthest back still. A small terracotta marker
flags the live needle's reached position as the second Shine step. A slate crosshair labeled the goal,
carrying its purpose flag, still sits further out on the green calm side, clearly ahead of the live
needle, with visible room left between them, because a home cleaned once is not yet a home that stays
clean and Shine finishes only when the whole home runs on a rhythm. Warm paper cream background, ink line
work, consistent stroke weight, friendly but not childish.

- Mood: an honest step, earned, the payoff of the dashed projection the last chapter drew; the targets
  actually got cleaned this chapter, so the needle follows the arrow and steps again toward calm, but the
  goal is still ahead, with clear room left for the next chapter's Shine-complete milestone; a real step
  and an unfinished journey, both true at once. The needle stepped today; one more step finishes Shine.
- Avoid: the live needle held or unmoved this chapter (it must step, following the dashed arrow), the
  needle reaching or passing the goal crosshair, no room left between the needle and the goal, the step
  drawn as the finish of Shine, the dashed ghosts drawn solid or bright, a milestone banner or
  completion glow, celebratory confetti, hype, neon color, any sense of the journey being finished. The
  needle steps on purpose, and clear room remains between it and the goal.
