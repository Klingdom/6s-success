## 1. Not laziness, a slow method

The reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy, or busy, or disorganized.

It is that the method you are using is slow and horrible, and a slow horrible job is one any sane person postpones.

Change the method and the same job becomes a two-minute wipe you barely notice doing. And a two-minute wipe you barely notice is a job that actually gets done.

Nobody skips the bathroom because they forgot whose job it was. They skip it because, done the way they know how, it takes forty minutes and leaves them on their knees.

The fix is not more willpower. It is a quicker method.

## 2. Dread beats ownership

You built a fair list. Every target has a name. And several lines still fill you with a small sinking feeling.

The oven that means an hour and a caustic spray. The shower screen that never really comes clear. The floors that mean moving furniture.

The target is owned. It is also dreaded. And dread, quietly, beats ownership almost every time.

The list does not fail because it was unfair. It fails because the jobs on it were miserable, and miserable jobs do not get done no matter whose name is next to them.

So give a dreaded target a method that is quick and works, and the dread simply drains out of it, because there is nothing left to dread.

## 3. Effective AND easy

A method that works has to do two things at once, and most cleaning advice only ever gives you one.

It has to be effective, so the target comes clean and you can read its condition. And it has to be easy, so you will genuinely do it again next time without a fight.

Miss either half and it fails. The spray-and-wipe that smears the grease around gets done and does nothing. The full hour of scrubbing works once and never again.

A working method is the quickest effective way to clean and read a given target: effective enough that the surface actually comes clean and shows what it needs, and easy enough that you will do it again without dreading it.

## 4. The one rule

Read this against everything you have been told about cleaning, most of which is about the most thorough method, the deep clean, the proper way.

The best method is the one you will actually do.

Thorough is worth nothing if it does not happen. A merely good method you run every week beats a perfect method you run once a year and then avoid.

Because the home is kept by what you actually do, not by what you know you should.

So from here on, when you choose how to clean a target, you are not looking for the most complete method. You are looking for the best one you will actually, reliably, do.

## 5. The five working methods

None of these is a trick or a special product. Any of them lifts almost any target from a slog to a quick job.

Right tool for the surface: match the tool to the material and the muscle disappears.

Top to bottom, dry before wet: order is free speed.

Fewer, simpler products: a small handful covers almost everything, and you actually restock it.

Keep the tool where the target is: a cloth by the sink gets used; the same cloth down the hall does not.

Clean in passing: fold a small clean into a moment you were already spending.

## 6. A method problem, not a character flaw

If you have spent years feeling vaguely bad about some corner of your home, let this land.

You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem. The jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in themselves; they are jobs done the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost always to find the method that makes the job small.

You are not lazy and you are not failing. You were handed slow methods, by habit and by how you were taught.

The moment the method gets quick, the avoidance stops on its own, without any change in your character.

## 7. Keep the tool where the target is

The largest hidden cost of cleaning is not the cleaning. It is the effort of starting.

A cloth kept by the bathroom sink gets used on the bathroom sink. The same cloth kept in a cupboard down the hall does not.

So store each target's tool at the target. Then wiping the shelf, checking the hooks, catching the grime becomes a ten-second thing you do in passing, not a job you have to schedule.

This is the point-of-use idea from Straighten, pointed at Shine. The easiest method is the one whose tools are already in reach.

## 8. Still leaves you having looked

One caution, so speed does not cost you the thing Shine was for in the first place.

It is possible to get so good at fast that you clean without looking, spraying and wiping on autopilot while your mind is elsewhere. A target cleaned blind is a target you did not inspect.

A working method still leaves you having looked. Do not spray a surface and walk away; the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one, so the best method cleans the target and shows you its condition in the same easy pass.

Choose methods that put your hand and eye right on the target, and the inspection comes free.

## 9. Small and often

The whole-Saturday deep clean is so large you keep putting it off. And a job you keep putting off is a job that does not happen.

Small, frequent, easy passes beat a rare heroic scrub, because the small easy thing actually happens.

A wipe of the shower wall while the water is still running. The counter while the pasta boils. The sink after you brush. Folded into a moment you were spending anyway, a small clean costs almost nothing and keeps a target from ever needing a big session.

Cleaning that is quick enough to fold into an ordinary day stops being an event you postpone and becomes a background hum that keeps the whole home clean and read.
