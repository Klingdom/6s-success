# The Reason You Never Clean the Oven Is Not Laziness. It Is a Slow Method. Here Is How to Fix That.

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

You have a list now, and every line on it has a name. That is further than most homes ever get, and it is still not a clean house, because between a named target and a cleaned one sits a small, stubborn gap, and the gap is the how. A job can be perfectly assigned to a perfectly willing person and still not happen, for the oldest reason there is: it is a pain to do. Nobody skips the bathroom because they forgot whose job it was. They skip it because, done the way they know how, it takes forty minutes and a cupboard of sprays and leaves them on their knees, so it quietly slides to next weekend.

Here is the thing almost no one says out loud about cleaning: the reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy, or busy, or disorganized. It is that the method you are using is slow and horrible, and a slow horrible job is one any sane person postpones. Change the method and the same job becomes a two-minute wipe you barely notice doing, and a two-minute wipe you barely notice is a job that actually gets done.

So today we take the list you built and give each target something it has been missing: a method quick enough that you will actually do it and good enough that it actually works. We are not remaking the list, and we are not reassigning anyone. We are closing the gap between a target that is owned and a target that is clean.

## A list you still dread

You have a Shine list, grouped by kind, with a name against every line. The what is decided and the who is decided. And if you are honest, several lines already fill you with a small sinking feeling: the oven that means an hour and a caustic spray, the shower screen that never really comes clear, the floors that mean moving furniture. The target is owned. It is also dreaded. And dread, quietly, beats ownership almost every time.

That is the failure this chapter exists to prevent. The list does not fail because it was unfair. It fails because the jobs on it were miserable, and miserable jobs do not get done no matter whose name is next to them. So the missing piece is not more willpower and it is not a stricter chart. It is a better way to do each job. Give a dreaded target a method that is quick and works, and the dread simply drains out of it, because there is nothing left to dread.

## What makes a method actually work

A method that works has to do two things at once, and most cleaning advice only ever gives you one. It has to be effective, so the target comes clean and you can read its condition afterward. And it has to be easy, so it is quick and painless enough that you will genuinely do it again next time without a fight. Miss either half and the method fails, just in different ways: the spray-and-wipe that smears the grease around gets done and does nothing; the full hour of scrubbing works once and never again.

**A working method is the quickest effective way to clean and read a given target: effective enough that the surface actually comes clean and shows what it needs, and easy enough that you will do it again without dreading it.**

Which points at the single most useful rule in this chapter.

**The best method is the one you will actually do.**

Thorough is worth nothing if it does not happen. A merely good method you run every week beats a perfect method you run once a year and then avoid, because the home is kept by what you actually do, not by what you know you should.

## The methods that work

None of these is a trick or a special product. They are the handful of habits that separate people for whom cleaning is quick from people for whom it is a slog, and any of them can be lifted onto any target on your list.

**Right tool for the surface.** Most struggle at the sink is really the wrong tool applied with extra effort. Match the tool to the material and the muscle disappears.

**Top to bottom, dry before wet.** Order is free speed. Dust and crumbs fall, so clean the high things before the low ones, and do the dry jobs before the wet ones. Work the other way and you dirty what you just cleaned.

**Fewer, simpler products.** A cupboard of specialist sprays is slower, not faster. A small handful of simple products covers almost everything, costs less, is safer around children, and is simple enough that you actually restock it.

**Keep the tool where the target is.** A cloth kept by the bathroom sink gets used on the bathroom sink; the same cloth kept in a cupboard down the hall does not. Storing each target's tool at the target removes the largest hidden cost of cleaning, which is the effort of starting.

**Clean in passing.** The cheapest clean is the one you never had to make time for, because you did it while you were already there: a wipe of the shower wall while the water is still running, the counter while the pasta boils, the sink after you brush.

## You do not have a cleaning problem

**You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem. The jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in themselves; they are jobs done the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost always to find the method that makes the job small.**

If you have spent years feeling vaguely bad about the state of some corner of your home, let this land. You are not lazy and you are not failing. You have been handed slow methods, by habit, by how you were taught, and slow methods make jobs that any reasonable person avoids. The moment the method gets quick, the avoidance stops on its own, without any change in your character. Nobody has to become a more disciplined person to keep a clean home. They just have to stop using the forty-minute method for a two-minute job.

## A good method still reads the surface

One caution, so the speed does not cost you the thing Shine was for in the first place. It is possible to get so good at fast that you clean without looking, spraying and wiping on autopilot, and a target cleaned blind is a target you did not inspect.

**A working method still leaves you having looked. Do not spray a surface and walk away; the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one, so the best method cleans the target and shows you its condition in the same easy pass.**

The quick methods actually help here, because a method that puts your hand and eye right on the target inspects by its nature. Choose methods that bring you into contact with the target, and the inspection comes free.

## The assignment

Take the one cleaning job you dread most and find a faster method for it: a better tool, fewer products, a different order. Do it once the new way and time it. Dread usually turns out to be a method problem. Then, if you can, sit down with the people you live with and have each person show the others the quickest way they have found to clean their target, and steal each other's best methods. The house gets a shared toolkit and nobody has to work it out alone.

**The best method is the one you will actually do. Match a quick, effective method to each target, keep it small enough to fold into an ordinary day, and Shine stops being a dreaded event and becomes a handful of easy passes that keep the whole home clean and read.**
