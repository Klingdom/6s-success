### Slide 2 · The missing piece is the how

- You have a list now, and every line on it has a name. That is further than most homes ever get, and it is still not a clean house, because between a named target and a cleaned one sits a small, stubborn gap, and the gap is the how. A job can be perfectly assigned to a perfectly willing person and still not happen, for the oldest reason there is: it is a pain to do. Nobody skips the bathroom because they forgot whose job it was. They skip it because, done the way they know how, it takes forty minutes and a cupboard of sprays and leaves them on their knees, so it quietly slides to next weekend.
- Here is the thing almost no one says out loud: the reason a job does not get done is usually not that you are lazy, or busy, or disorganized. It is that the method you are using is slow and horrible, and a slow horrible job is one any sane person postpones. Change the method and the same job becomes a two-minute wipe you barely notice doing, and a two-minute wipe you barely notice is a job that actually gets done. The whole of this chapter lives in that swap.
- So today we take the list you built and the names you assigned, and we give each target something it has been missing: a method quick enough that you will actually do it and good enough that it actually works. We are not remaking the list, and we are not reassigning anyone. We are closing the gap between a target that is owned and a target that is clean. And, because Shine has been about reading surfaces from the start, we will make sure the methods leave you having looked, not just having wiped.

### Slide 4 · A list you still dread

- Let me name the exact place the last chapter left you, because it is a good place and an incomplete one. You have a Shine list, grouped by kind, with a name against every line. Give a dreaded target a method that is quick and works, and the dread simply drains out of it, because there is nothing left to dread. That is the entire move of this chapter, and everything below is how to make it.

### Slide 5 · What makes a method actually work

- A method that works has to do two things at once, and most cleaning advice only ever gives you one of them. It has to be effective, meaning the target actually comes clean and you can read its condition afterward. And it has to be easy, meaning it is quick and painless enough that you will genuinely do it again next time without a fight. Miss either half and the method fails, just in different ways.
- A method that is easy but not effective is the spray-and-wipe that smears the grease around and leaves the hob no cleaner than it was; it gets done and it does nothing. A method that is effective but not easy is the full hour of scrubbing with three products that leaves the oven gleaming and that you will not repeat until next year, if then; it works once and never again. Neither is a working method. The one you want sits in the small overlap of the two, clean enough to matter and easy enough to repeat, and finding that overlap for each target is the skill.
- Definition box, verbatim: "A working method is the quickest effective way to clean and read a given target: effective enough that the surface actually comes clean and shows what it needs, and easy enough that you will do it again without dreading it." And the single most useful rule, verbatim: "The best method is the one you will actually do." Thorough is worth nothing if it does not happen; the home is kept by what you actually do, not by what you know you should.

### Slide 6 · The methods that work

- Here is a small kit of methods that move almost any target toward that corner. None of them is a trick or a special product. They are the handful of habits that separate people for whom cleaning is quick from people for whom it is a slog, and any of them can be lifted onto any target on your list.
- **Right tool for the surface.** Most struggle at the sink is really the wrong tool applied with extra effort. The right cloth, the right brush, the right cheap scraper does in one easy pass what a paper towel and determination do in ten. **Top to bottom, dry before wet.** Order is free speed. Dust and crumbs fall, so clean the high things before the low ones, and do the dry jobs before the wet ones; work the other way and you dirty what you just cleaned. **Fewer, simpler products.** A cupboard of specialist sprays is slower, not faster; a small handful covers almost everything, costs less, is safer around children, and is simple enough that you actually restock it and use it.
- **Keep the tool where the target is.** A cloth kept by the bathroom sink gets used; the same cloth in a cupboard down the hall does not. Storing each target's tool at the target removes the largest hidden cost of cleaning, the effort of starting. This is the point-of-use idea from Straighten, pointed at Shine. **Clean in passing.** The cheapest clean is the one you never had to make time for, because you did it while you were already there: a wipe of the shower wall while the water runs, the counter while the pasta boils. 6S Tip, verbatim: "Keep the tool where the target is. A cloth kept by the sink gets used; a cloth kept in a cupboard down the hall does not. The easiest method is the one whose tools are already in reach."

### Slide 7 · You do not have a cleaning problem

- Stop for a moment on what these methods quietly imply, because it is kinder than the story most people tell themselves. If you have spent years feeling vaguely bad about the state of some corner of your home, this is worth letting land: you are not lazy and you are not failing. You have been handed slow methods, by habit, by how you were taught, by adverts selling effort and products, and slow methods make jobs that any reasonable person avoids.
- The moment the method gets quick, the avoidance stops on its own, without any change in your character. Nobody has to become a more disciplined person to keep a clean home. They just have to stop using the forty-minute method for a two-minute job.
- The frozen reframe, verbatim: "You do not have a cleaning problem. You have a method problem. The jobs you dread are almost never dreadful in themselves; they are jobs done the slow way, with the wrong tool, at the wrong time, so the fix is rarely to try harder and almost always to find the method that makes the job small." Quick Win, verbatim: "Take the one cleaning job you dread most and find a faster method for it: a better tool, fewer products, a different order. Do it once the new way and time it. Dread usually turns out to be a method problem."

### Slide 8 · A good method still reads the surface

- One caution, so the speed does not cost you the thing Shine was for in the first place. It is possible to get so good at fast that you clean without looking, spraying and wiping on autopilot while your mind is elsewhere, and a target cleaned blind is a target you did not inspect.
- Keeping the reading in is easy and costs no time; it is a matter of attention, not effort. As your cloth crosses the surface, let your eyes cross it too. The quick methods above actually help here, because a method that puts your hand and eye right on the target, the wipe, the pass with the right tool, is a method that inspects by its nature. It is only the lazy shortcuts, the spray you never touch, the mop that never gets near the corners, that clean without reading. Choose methods that bring you into contact with the target, and the inspection comes free, exactly as it did on the launch pad.
- The frozen inspection line, verbatim: "A working method still leaves you having looked. Do not spray a surface and walk away; the point of Shine was never only a clean surface but a read one, so the best method cleans the target and shows you its condition in the same easy pass."

### Slide 9 · Putting a method to the list

- Now do it once, on the list you already have, so this stops being advice and becomes something you have felt. Pick two or three targets, and give each the working-method treatment. Start with the launch pad, since it is old friends by now: give it a single cloth kept in the entry drawer, right where the target is, so wiping the shelf and checking the hooks is a ten-second thing you do in passing on your way past, not a job you schedule.
- Then take a target you dread, the oven, say. Swap the hour of caustic scrubbing for a gentler method left to sit while you do something else, so the work happens without you on your knees, fewer products, far less effort, and the same clean result. Then a point-of-use target, the door handles, which need no method grander than the cloth already in your hand as you pass, wiped in a second, read in the same second.
- Notice what happened across those three. Nothing on the list changed; every target was one you already had and already owned. All that changed is that each now has a way of being cleaned that is quick enough to actually do and good enough to actually work, and each, done that way, put your eyes on the surface as your hand did the job. That is the whole chapter, applied: the same list, suddenly doable. Family Challenge, verbatim: "Have each person show the others the quickest way they have found to clean their assigned target, and steal each other's best methods. The house gets a shared toolkit and nobody has to work it out alone." Common Mistake, verbatim: "Saving it all up for one big deep-clean, the whole-Saturday method, which is so large you keep putting it off. Small, frequent, easy passes beat a rare heroic scrub, because the small easy thing actually happens."
