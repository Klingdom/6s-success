### Slide 3 · Route, do not decide

- Draw the line clearly before we start. Deciding a thing is unnecessary and deciding where it should go are two separate acts, and you already finished the first. What is left is the second, and it is smaller and calmer than it looks.
- Every unnecessary thing leaves through one of five doors, and a quick question picks the door for each one. Most of the box goes through a single door, quickly, and the whole thing can leave in one trip.
- So this is a routing chapter, not another round of deciding.

### Slide 4 · The five doors

- Every unnecessary thing leaves through one of five doors, and it helps to see them named together before we use them: Donate, Sell, Store, Recycle, Throw. That is the whole set. Nothing bound for out needs a sixth option.
- Most things do not even need you to think hard about which of the five they take, because a short cascade of questions sorts them almost automatically.
- Definition, verbatim: "Routing the out box means sending each unnecessary thing out through one of five doors, Donate, Sell, Store, Recycle, or Throw, and choosing the door that actually gets it gone. Donate and Recycle are the fast lanes and take most of the box; Sell and Store are the slow doors, worth it only for the rare item that truly earns them."

### Slide 5 · The quick cascade

- Run each item down this cascade and stop at the first door that fits.
- Broken or truly unusable? Then Recycle it if you can, and Throw it if you cannot. Genuinely valuable, and worth your time to sell? Then Sell it, with a deadline. Genuinely needed later, just not now? Then Store it, labeled and dated, somewhere out of the way. Still useful to someone? Then Donate it. This is where most of the box goes.
- The order is doing quiet work. The two doors that clear a box quickest, Donate and Recycle, sit at the top and the bottom, and between them they take the great majority of anything you route. Sell and Store are narrow on purpose, because a thing that goes through either does not actually leave your life today; it takes a to-do with it.

### Slide 6 · Donate is the default

- Start here, because most of your box ends here. A thing that still works, that someone could use tomorrow, has an easy and honorable exit: you give it to someone who will use it.
- The alternative to donating a usable thing is almost never keeping it, because you already decided you do not use it. The alternative is holding onto it to sell it later, or to find just the right charity eventually. Each of those is really just a way of keeping the thing in your house while you feel good about your intentions.
- Donating asks nothing of the future. The bag goes to a place that will pass the things on, and the transaction is over the same day. It is the exit that actually completes.

Most usable things you do not need should go to someone who does, today, not to a someday sale that keeps them in your house for months." ### Slide 7 · Giving it away is not waste

- Head off the small guilt that sometimes shows up at this door, the sense that giving something away is a kind of waste. It is not. The thing is not being destroyed; it is being handed to someone who wants it, which is the opposite of waste.
- Someone who needs a second umbrella gets one, and you get your corner back, and both of those things happen now rather than eventually.
- That is why it is the door most of the box should take: not because giving is noble, though it is pleasant that it is, but because it is the exit that actually finishes the job.

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the line set large in Fraunces medium #2B2622, verbatim: "You are not throwing value away. You are moving it to where it will finally get used, and taking your space back in the bargain." A soft green #6E8B5B glow, no sparks. Small Inter tag: "the opposite of waste."

### Slide 8 · The sell trap

- The first of the two slow doors. There is a real place for selling: a genuinely valuable item, the good bicycle nobody rides, the appliance you replaced that still works. For those, selling is sensible, and the money is real.
- The trouble is the temptation to sell far more than that. Selling is work: photographs, a listing, a price, messages, a stranger at your door, a no-show, a relisting. That work is worth it for a hundred-dollar item and a waste of a week for a five-dollar one.
- So set a hard limit before you start, and every item gets a sell-by date the day you list it. If it has not sold by that date, it goes straight into the donate bag. The deadline is what keeps a sale from quietly becoming permanent storage with a price tag on it.

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the line set large in Fraunces medium #2B2622, verbatim: "Selling turns clearing into a part-time job. Sell only what is genuinely worth the work, put a deadline on it, and donate whatever has not sold by the date." A narrow slate SELL door beside it with a small dated tag: "sell-by, then the donate bag." No sparks.

### Slide 9 · The store trap

- The second slow door has the same shape and needs the same kind of fence. There is a real place for it too: the winter coats in July, the camping gear you truly use every summer, the things you genuinely need later and simply cannot use now.
- The danger is using Store as a hiding place for things you have not got the nerve to route, which is not deciding at all. So the guardrail is a label and a date: anything you store gets a clear tag saying what is inside and when it went in.
- And the harder rule, worth reading twice: never pay to store what you do not need. A rented locker full of things you already decided you do not use is the clutter you cleared, moved offsite and charged to you monthly.

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the line set large in Fraunces medium #2B2622, verbatim: "Storing is not deciding. Store only what you will truly use later, label it and date it, and never rent space to keep what you do not need." A narrow slate STORE door beside it with a labelled, dated box tag. Small Inter caption: "label it, date it, and route everything else."

### Slide 10 · Recycle and Throw

- The two doors for the things nobody can use, the broken and the finished. Recycle is the door for the worn and the electronic: paper and cardboard to paper recycling; old cables, dead chargers, a battery, a small broken gadget to an e-waste drop-off, which keeps the metals and the toxins out of the landfill. It costs almost nothing, so do it where it is easy. Recycle is the second fast lane precisely because so much of a worn-out box qualifies for it.
- Throw is the last door, the smallest, and the one people flinch at. It is for the things that are truly finished. The only real obstacle is not logistics but a feeling, the guilt that says throwing something away is wasteful.
- The money was spent long ago, at the store, and keeping the ruined object does not bring one cent of it back. There is no virtue in living among broken objects.

[VISUAL] Pull-quote card on panel #FBF7EF, slate #3C5A6B rules above and below, the reframe set in Fraunces medium #2B2622, verbatim: "Some things are simply worn out, and throwing them away is not the waste. The waste, if there ever was any, happened at the store. Recycle what you can, bin the rest without guilt, and be done." A honey #DDA63A RECYCLE stream and a small soft #6A625A THROW bin beside it. No sparks; the frame is plain, not scolding.

### Slide 11 · A routing pass on the out box

- Route the entryway box, because the method is easier to trust once you have watched it run. Tip it out and take the items one at a time, down the cascade, and notice how few need the slow doors.
- The two extra umbrellas: usable, not worth the errand of selling, not needed later, still useful to someone. Donate. The wad of surplus tote bags: same answer, Donate, though the torn or stained ones step over to Recycle. The spare key to the car you sold two summers ago opens nothing and is usable by no one: Recycle as scrap metal, or Throw. The fan of old takeout menus is paper: Recycle. The dead charging cable is e-waste: Recycle at the electronics drop-off.
- Tally the pass. The whole box split cleanly between the two quick doors, and it left the house in one trip. Nothing was valuable enough to sell, nothing was needed later enough to store. That is the normal shape of an out box.

### Slide 13 · Sort is finished (close + soft CTA)

- Look at the entryway one last time. The console holds its five working things, the overflow basket is empty, the out box has left in a single trip. On a shelf out of the way, a tag or two still runs out its clock, and when a date passes those maybes will route themselves the same easy way. What is left in the room is only what you chose to keep.
- But notice what the empty corner also reveals. The keepers are still exactly where the pass left them, sitting where they happened to land, without proper places of their own. Sorting decided what stays; it did not decide where any of it lives. So the work turns now: we stop removing and we start arranging.
- One Idea to Keep, verbatim: "The best destination is the one that actually empties the box. A thing given away today beats a thing you mean to sell someday. Choose the door that gets it out of the house, and Sort is finished."
