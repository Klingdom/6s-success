# Podcast Script: What to Donate, Sell, Store, Recycle, or Throw Away

**Chapter 11, 6S Success: Home Edition (Part 3, Sort)**
**Slug:** what-to-donate-sell-store-recycle-or-throw-away
**Estimated runtime:** 8 to 12 minutes (roughly 1,550 spoken words)
**Format:** Conversational, audio-first, single host. No reliance on visuals.

---

## INTRO

Hey, welcome back. Come stand with me at the entryway one last time, because today is the day the corner finally clears.

Look at what waits there. The out box is full, it is settled, and every single thing in it has already been decided. Over the last three chapters you emptied the entryway of what did not belong, you tested what was left against the plain question of whether you actually use it, and you gave the honest maybes a tag and a deadline so they could answer in their own time. What settled to the bottom of all that is the out box: a collection of things you have already judged unnecessary, sitting together, ready to go somewhere. Not one item in it is still in question.

So this episode is not another round of hard calls. Today it leaves, and when it does, Sort is finished. This is the fourth and last chapter of Sort, and it is a routing chapter. By the end the corner is empty, the first of the six S's is genuinely done, and the only things left in the room are the ones you chose to keep.

Let me say plainly what today is and is not, because it changes how heavy the work feels. You are not here to decide anything today. You are here to route.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: DECIDING VERSUS ROUTING

Here is the line I want to draw before we open a single door. Deciding a thing is unnecessary and deciding where it should go are two separate acts, and you already finished the first. That first act, the hard one, weighing whether you actually use a thing, was Chapter 9's work, and it is done. What is left is the second act, and it is smaller and calmer than it looks.

Because the hard part is behind you, routing is quick. You are not weighing attachment or use anymore. You are asking a much simpler question of each thing, which is only this: what is the cleanest way for you to leave the house? Answer that five times, or ten, or however many the box holds, and the corner is clear.

And let me keep the fences honest, the same way we have all through Sort. We are not reopening whether anything belongs in the box; that was Chapter 9. We are not re-running the red tags; the tagged maybes stay on their timers in the holding area, and if one expires untouched, it drops into an out box and routes exactly the way today's items route. And we are not giving the keepers homes; where your working things live is the whole of the next part, and we will not touch it here. Today has one narrow task: take the things already bound for out, and send each one through the right door.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: THE FIVE DOORS

Every unnecessary thing leaves through one of five doors, and it helps to hear them named together before we use them. Donate, Sell, Store, Recycle, Throw. That is the whole set. Nothing bound for out needs a sixth option, and most things do not even need you to think hard about which of the five they take, because a short cascade of questions sorts them almost automatically.

Run each item down this cascade and stop at the first door that fits. Broken or truly unusable? Then Recycle it if you can, and Throw it if you cannot. Genuinely valuable, and worth your time to sell? Then Sell it, with a deadline. Genuinely needed later, just not now? Then Store it, labeled and dated, somewhere out of the way. Still useful to someone? Then Donate it. This is where most of the box goes.

Now hear the order again, because the order is doing quiet work. The two doors that clear a box quickest, Donate and Recycle, sit at the top and the bottom, and between them they will take the great majority of anything you route. Donate handles the usable things, and Recycle handles the worn and the electronic. The two doors in the middle, Sell and Store, are narrow on purpose. They are not bad doors, but they are slow ones, and a thing that goes through either does not actually leave your life today. It takes a to-do with it. So the bias of the whole system is deliberate and strong: when a thing is still usable, the default answer is Donate, and you only step off that default when an item makes a genuine case for one of the others.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: DONATE IS THE DEFAULT

Start with the door most of your box will take. A thing that still works, that someone could use tomorrow, has an easy and honorable exit: you give it to someone who will use it.

Here is the line to keep. Donate is the default. Most usable things you do not need should go to someone who does, today, not to a someday sale that keeps them in your house for months.

Look at what that protects you from. The alternative to donating a usable thing is almost never keeping it, because you already decided you do not use it. The alternative is holding onto it to sell it later, or to give it to the perfect person eventually, or to find just the right charity that deserves it. Each of those feels more careful than a plain donation, and each is really just a way of keeping the thing in your house while you feel good about your intentions. The usable object sits in a bag by the stairs for a season, waiting for a plan that never quite arrives, and the clutter you meant to clear is still clutter.

Donating cuts through all of that. It asks nothing of the future. The bag goes to a place that will pass the things on, and the transaction is over the same day. No listing to write, no buyer to meet, no perfect recipient to wait for. That is why it is the door most of the box should take: not because giving is noble, though it is pleasant that it is, but because it is the exit that actually completes.

And let me head off the small guilt that sometimes shows up here, the sense that giving something away is a kind of waste. It is not. The thing is not being destroyed; it is being handed to someone who wants it, which is the opposite of waste. You are not throwing value away. You are moving it to where it will finally get used, and taking your space back in the bargain.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: THE TWO SLOW DOORS

Now the two middle doors, the ones to use sparingly. Sell and Store are both legitimate, and each is exactly right for a certain rare item. But both are slow, both keep the thing in your house while you tell yourself you have dealt with it, and both are easy to over-use, because both feel responsible. So each one gets a guardrail.

Take selling first. There is a real place for it: a genuinely valuable item, the good bicycle nobody rides, the appliance you replaced that still works. For those, selling is sensible, and the money is real. The trouble is the temptation to sell far more than that, to look at a box of ordinary usable things and see a yard sale.

So here is the discipline. Selling turns clearing into a part-time job. Sell only what is genuinely worth the work, put a deadline on it, and donate whatever has not sold by the date. Selling is work: photographs, a listing, a price, messages, a stranger at your door, a no-show, a relisting. That work is worth it for a hundred-dollar item and a waste of a week for a five-dollar one. So every one of them gets a sell-by date written down the day you list it, and if it has not sold by that date, it does not get relisted forever. It goes straight into the donate bag. The deadline is what keeps a sale from quietly becoming permanent storage with a price tag on it.

The Store door has the same shape and needs the same kind of fence. There is a real place for it too: the winter coats in July, the camping gear you truly use every summer, the things you genuinely need later and simply cannot use now. Those belong in storage. The danger is using Store as a hiding place for things you have not got the nerve to route, which is not deciding at all.

So here is the rule. Storing is not deciding. Store only what you will truly use later, label it and date it, and never rent space to keep what you do not need. Anything you store gets a clear tag saying what is inside and when it went in, so that a year from now you can open the space and see the truth at a glance rather than a mystery box. And the harder rule, worth reading twice: never pay to store what you do not need. A rented locker full of things you have already decided you do not use is the clutter you cleared, moved offsite and charged to you monthly. Store the genuine later-use items, labeled and dated, and route everything else.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FIVE: RECYCLE AND THROW

That leaves the two doors for the things nobody can use, the broken and the finished. These are the easy ones, and they matter mostly for two small habits: do the responsible thing when it is easy, and let go of the guilt that clings to the bin.

Recycle is the door for the worn and the electronic. Paper and cardboard go to paper recycling. Old cables, dead chargers, a battery, a small broken gadget, all of that is e-waste, and most towns have a drop-off or a shop that takes it, which keeps the metals and the toxins out of the landfill. It costs you almost nothing to point these things at the right stream instead of the trash, so do it where it is easy. Recycle is the second fast lane precisely because so much of a worn-out box qualifies for it.

Throw is the last door, the smallest, and the one people flinch at. It is for the things that are truly finished: broken past repair, unusable by anyone, not worth the trouble of recycling. And here the only real obstacle is not logistics but a feeling, the guilt that says throwing something away is wasteful, that you failed by not using it up. So let me take that apart, because it is the same knot we untied back in Chapter 9.

Some things are simply worn out, and throwing them away is not the waste. The waste, if there ever was any, happened at the store. Recycle what you can, bin the rest without guilt, and be done. The money for a worn-out thing was spent long ago, at the moment of purchase, and keeping the ruined object in a drawer does not bring one cent of it back. Holding onto a dead cable to avoid admitting it is finished does not honor the money you spent. It just adds a small daily weight to a cost that was already paid. So recycle what the recycler will take, put the genuinely finished things in the bin, and let the guilt stay in the bin with them. There is no virtue in living among broken objects.

---

## A ROUTING PASS ON THE OUT BOX

Let us route the entryway box, because the method is easier to trust once you have watched it run. Tip the box out and take the items one at a time, and notice how few of them need the slow doors.

The two extra umbrellas come first. Perfectly good umbrellas; you simply do not need three. Down the cascade: not broken, not worth the errand of selling, not something you need later, still useful to someone. Clearly yes. So they go through the Donate door, into the bag. Next, the wad of surplus tote bags, the ones that breed in a drawer. Same answer: usable, not worth selling, not needed later, useful to plenty of people. Donate. The few that are torn or stained are not useful to anyone, so those step over to Recycle, but the good ones join the umbrellas.

The spare key to the car you sold two summers ago opens nothing, is worth nothing, and cannot be used by a single person on earth. It fails every question until the last, so it takes the bottom of the cascade. It is scrap metal, which means Recycle if your program takes small metal, or Throw if not. Either way it is a two-second call. The fan of old takeout menus is paper, plain and simple, so Recycle. And the dead charging cable is e-waste, so it goes to the electronics drop-off rather than the landfill, which is Recycle as well.

Tally the pass. Two umbrellas and a handful of tote bags through Donate. Menus, a dead cable, some torn bags, and a useless key through Recycle, with the key allowed to Throw if that is simpler. Nothing was valuable enough to sell. Nothing was needed later enough to store. The whole box split cleanly between the two quick doors, and it left the house in one trip. That is the normal shape of an out box, and it is exactly why Donate and Recycle are the lanes the cascade leans on.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART SIX: THE OUT BOX LEAVES THE BUILDING

Now carry the box to the car, drop the donation bag where it goes, put the recycling in its bins, and come back to the empty corner. Then go and look at the friction meter, because this time it moves, and it has earned the move.

For three chapters the needle held. It held through the deciding, through the tagging, through the quieting of second thoughts, because the honest rule of this meter is that it only moves when the room does, and understanding is not the same as an emptier room. Last chapter it held one more time, but it drew a faint dashed arrow just ahead of itself, a promise of the step it would take when things actually left. This chapter is that step. The out box did not get tidied or relabeled or moved to a better corner. It left the building. The last of the Sort clutter is out of the house, and so the needle steps forward, off its held position and onto a clearly marked tick on the dial: Sort complete.

Sit with that for a second, because it is a real milestone and you should feel it. The first of the six S's is done. What did not belong in the entryway has left, and what you do not need has left with it, and both are truly out of the room rather than shuffled somewhere else in it. The room is genuinely lighter now, not merely arranged to look that way.

And then be honest about what the meter still shows, because it is telling the truth in both directions. Chapter 10's held position is still there, drawn now as a faint dashed ghost, so you can see how far the needle came. But the goal, the crosshair out on the green calm side, is still ahead of where the needle now sits. Sort is finished; the journey is not. Five S's remain after this one, Straighten, Shine, Safety, Standardize, and Sustain, and each will move the needle again in its turn. The meter marks the milestone plainly and refuses to oversell it, which is exactly what makes it worth trusting.

---

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So if you take one thing from this episode, take this. The best destination is the one that actually empties the box. A thing given away today beats a thing you mean to sell someday. Choose the door that gets it out of the house, and Sort is finished.

And here is your assignment, and it is small on purpose. Grab three things from the out box and get them out of the house today. The usable ones into a donation bag in your car; the broken one to recycling or the bin. Three things gone for good in five minutes. And one small trick that makes it stick: put the donation bag in your car now, not by the door. A bag by the door gets unpacked; a bag in the car gets dropped off. If you want to make it a family thing, everyone picks one item they have been meaning to sell someday and says honestly whether they will do the work this week. If not, it goes in the donate bag. Nobody has to sell anything; everybody has to be honest about it.

---

## OUTRO AND SOFT CTA

Look at the entryway one last time. The console holds its five working things. The overflow basket is empty. The out box has left, carried out in a single trip, its contents split between the people who will use them and the bins that will handle the rest. What is left in the room is only what you chose to keep. Nothing else. That is the end of Sort, and you did it without force and without shame. You did not attack anything. You asked plain questions and sent each unnecessary thing through the door that cleared it.

But notice what the empty corner also reveals. The keepers are still exactly where the pass left them, sitting where they happened to land, without proper places of their own. Sorting decided what stays. It did not decide where any of it lives. And a room full of the right things with no homes for them will drift back toward mess as surely as one full of the wrong things. So the work turns now. We stop removing and we start arranging.
