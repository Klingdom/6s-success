# Chapter 50 Podcast Script: The Patio or Deck

*Eight to twelve minutes. Conversational, unhurried, and this one is the last.*

---

There is one room left, and it has no roof.

Now, that sounds like a small difference, and it is not. I want to spend a minute on why, because it changes everything about how you approach this space.

Every other room in this book, all nineteen of them, is somewhere you keep the weather out of. You have spent this whole book working on the inside of a boundary. And the entire time, that boundary was doing quiet work on your behalf that you never had to think about. It held the temperature roughly steady. It kept the rain off. Above all, it meant that a thing you put down and forgot about was, six months later, still more or less the thing you put down.

Out here, none of that is true.

The patio is the only room in this book where the weather is not something you keep out. It is the reason you are there. Nobody sits outside in spite of the conditions. The whole point of a patio is that it puts you in them.

And that means the strategy that has worked in every other room, which is control the environment and then arrange things inside it, does not work here at all. You cannot make it stop raining on the deck box.

What you can do, and this is the whole chapter, is decide with your eyes open what is allowed to live in those conditions, and put everything else behind a door.

Right. The clock.

Indoors, think about the cable in the office drawer you never made a decision about. It sat there. It annoyed you. And it will be exactly as good on the day you finally decide about it as it was on the day you did not. The cost of postponing was irritation. That is all.

Out here, the cushion you meant to bring in is not the same cushion in April. Something has been happening to it every night since September. In the dark. At no charge to your attention, and at considerable charge to your wallet.

The resin chair that has stood through six summers of sun is not the chair you bought. It looks like the chair you bought, which is the dangerous part, and it will fail at a leg joint under whoever sits in it least carefully.

That is what I mean by the clock. Sun makes plastic brittle, damp gets into fabric and end grain, and a season of putting things off arrives in spring as a bill.

So there is one question that settles this entire space, and it comes straight out of the Micro Zone Manual, unchanged, because it cannot be improved.

Can it take a night of rain?

Not is this useful. Not do I love it. Not have I used it this year. Can it take a night of rain.

A cushion cannot. A cardboard box inside the deck box cannot, and it is genuinely remarkable how many deck boxes contain one. A bag of charcoal resting on the ground cannot; it draws moisture up through the paper and never lights properly again.

And here is why that question is so useful with other people, which most decluttering questions are not: the answers are not arguable. Nobody can defend a wet cushion.

Now, where to start, and this one surprises people. Not the cushions. Not the table. The boards and the railing, on a dry morning, before you touch anything soft.

Because if the structure is not sound, everything you arrange on top of it is decoration. A wobbling rail post does not get safer because the cushions are clean.

Two quick things there, and then the one I want you to take away.

Rake the gaps between the boards clear until you can see daylight through them. It is the least visible job on the whole list and it buys the most time, because a deck almost never rots from the top down. It rots from the end grain outward, in a gap you cannot see into.

And scrub the green algae film off the shaded treads. That film is the slipperiest surface anywhere on your property after rain.

But here is the thing I actually want you to keep, and it is why this room is last in the book.

Chapter 16, a long way back now, said that Shine is not tidying. It is cleaning that inspects. It was true in every room since: dust in a vent, lint in a duct.

Out here, the thing you are cleaning is the thing holding you up.

You will never find a popped fastener, or a soft board end, or a spider web in a burner tube, while sitting in a chair. You find them with a brush in your hand and your face close to the thing.

You cannot see a soft board from a chair. You can feel it through a deck brush.

And one last idea, which only this room could have taught you.

This is the only room in the book whose standard visibly ages. By August the algae is back on the shaded tread and the gaps have packed again. If you understood done as a destination, that feels like failure, and you stop.

It is not failure. It is the clock, which was always running in every other room too, just quietly enough for you to ignore.

Chapter 30 said you do not finish a 6S home, you renew it. Out here that stops being a good idea and becomes simply what is true. The patio is the one room where the book's last lesson is not something you have to take on trust, because you can watch it happen.

So do one zone this week, and make it the boards and the rail.

And then take the after photograph, and go and find the very first photograph you took, back in Chapter 6, of a room you had not yet learned how to read. Put them next to each other.

The difference between them is not tidiness.

Fifty chapters. Twenty rooms. Six S's, and one photograph you now know how to take.

Go and push the rail.
