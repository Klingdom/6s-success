# Chapter 50 Key Takeaways: The Patio or Deck

1. **The patio is the only room where the weather is not something you keep out.** It is the reason you are there, so you do not win it by excluding conditions but by deciding what can survive them.
2. **The clock runs out here and does not run indoors.** A postponed decision indoors costs convenience; outdoors it is billed, and the invoice arrives in spring as a replacement cost.
3. **If it cannot take a night of rain, it belongs behind a door.** That is the entire sorting pass, and it is not arguable, which is what makes it work with other people.
4. **Start with the boards and the rail**, on a dry morning, before you touch a cushion. If the structure is not sound, everything you arrange on top of it is decoration.
5. **Cleaning out here is how you read the structure.** You will never find a popped fastener or a soft board end from a chair. This is the room where cleaning as inspection finally carries your whole weight.
6. **Rinsing comes first outdoors**, which inverts the indoor dry-before-wet rule. Grit does not lift under a cloth, it grinds, so float it off before you scrub or you are sanding the thing rather than cleaning it.
7. **Rake the gaps between the boards until you see daylight.** A deck rots from the end grain outward, in a gap you cannot see into, not from the top down.
8. **The deck box has one job.** It stores cushions, because cushions are the only thing out here that must be under a lid within a minute of the first drop. A box that cannot close protects nothing inside it.
9. **Soft in one spot, replace the board. Soft in two adjacent spots or soft at the wall, stop putting people on it** and get someone qualified underneath it. Recognising that a structure needs looking at is a 6S skill; deciding what to do underneath it is not.
10. **This is the only standard in the book that visibly ages, and that is the last lesson.** You never finished any of the other rooms either. You renewed them, and the patio is simply honest enough to show you the clock.
