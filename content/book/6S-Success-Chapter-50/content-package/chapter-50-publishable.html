Part Nine · Room-by-Room Playbooks

Chapter Fifty

The Patio or Deck

The patio is the last room in this book and the only one with no roof, which makes it the only space you are not defending against the weather but visiting it in, and the only one with a clock running on everything you postpone. This playbook gives you, for each of the six zones, the exact inputs to gather and the precise, step-by-step way to clean and shine it, starting with the boards and the railing on a dry morning, because if the structure is not sound then everything you arrange on top of it is decoration.

There is one room left, and it has no roof.

That sounds like a small difference. It is not. Every other space in this book, all nineteen of them, is somewhere you keep the weather out of. You have spent forty-nine chapters working on the inside of a boundary, and the whole time that boundary was doing quiet work on your behalf. It meant that a thing you put down and forgot about was, six months later, still more or less the thing you put down.

Out here none of that is true.

The patio is the only room in this book where the weather is not something you keep out. It is the reason you are there. So you do not win this room by excluding conditions, you win it by deciding what can survive them, and everything that cannot take a night of rain belongs behind a door.

Nobody sits outside in spite of the conditions; the whole point of a patio is that it puts you in them. So the strategy that worked in every other room, control the environment and then arrange things inside it, does not work here at all. You cannot make it stop raining on the deck box. What you can do is decide with your eyes open what is allowed to live in those conditions, and put everything else behind a door.

The principleSix Zones, Not One Room

Here is what a boundary was doing for you, stated plainly.

The clock

The clock: the rate at which outdoor conditions turn a postponed decision into a replacement cost. Indoors a delayed decision costs you convenience, and the thing waits for you. Out here it does not wait. Sun makes plastic brittle, damp gets into fabric and end grain, and a season of putting things off arrives in spring as a bill.

Think about what that changes. In the home office, the cable you never ruled on is still a cable: it annoyed you, and it will be exactly as good on the day you finally decide about it as it was on the day you did not. The cost of postponing was irritation.

Out here, the cushion you meant to bring in is not the same cushion in April. Something has been happening to it every night since September, in the dark, at no charge to your attention and at considerable charge to your wallet. The resin chair that has stood through six summers of sun is not the chair you bought. It looks like the chair you bought, which is the dangerous part, and it will fail at a leg joint under whoever sits in it least carefully.

Naming the clock converts a vague guilt into a specific question, and specific questions can be answered. Not I really should sort out the patio, but: what out here is on the clock, and what have I already been charged for without noticing?

The rule that settles this room

Every room in Part Nine has had one rule that does most of the work, and this one comes straight out of the Manual, unchanged, because it cannot be improved.

If it cannot take a night of rain, it belongs behind a door.

That is the whole sorting pass for a patio. Not is this useful, not do I love it, not have I used it this year. Can it take a night of rain. A cushion cannot. A cardboard box inside the deck box cannot, and it is remarkable how many deck boxes contain one. A ceramic pot can, right up until the first hard frost finds the hairline crack at its drain hole.

Each of those is fine for a week. That is precisely the trap, and it is why the problem survives so many inspections by a reasonable person who walks past it every evening.

The patio is six zones, not one weekend: the surface, rail, and safety zone, the outdoor seating zone, the outdoor dining zone, the grill and outdoor cooking zone, the garden and plant care zone, and the outdoor storage zone. Start with the boards and the railing on a dry morning, before you touch a single cushion, because if the structure is not sound then everything you arrange on top of it is decoration.

A note on the order, the third time in Part Nine it has come up. The Manual lists the surface, rail, and safety zone last, and its own where-to-start tip says to do it first. When a listing and a tip disagree, this book follows the tip and says so out loud rather than quietly reordering. The tip is right here for a reason you can feel: a wobbling rail post does not get safer because the cushions are clean.

The quick passesThe Quick Passes: Sort, Straighten, Safety, Standardize, Sustain

Sort out here asks one question and only one, which is whether the thing can take a night of rain. Straighten protects the route rather than the look: walk from the door to the steps to the gate carrying a full laundry basket in both arms so you cannot see your feet, and whatever you have to step around gets moved off the deck entirely. Safety is not a pass in this chapter either, because three of the six zones are mostly safety and the first one is nothing else. Standardize is the resting state anyone can check without asking you: bare table but for the umbrella and one tray, cushions in the box, lid closed flat. And Sustain is attached to the last thing that happens on any evening out here, which is somebody carrying the final glass indoors, taking the cushions in the same trip and latching the lid rather than lowering it.

The methodHow to Shine a Patio: The Method

The nine rules of Shine from Chapter 31 still govern. Four rules go on top of them out here, and one of the nine gets applied in a way that looks, at first glance, like breaking it.

One. The weather is not the enemy here, it is the point. You are not defending this space against conditions. You are deciding what can live in them. Every judgment call in this chapter reduces to that.

Two. Postponement is billed. See the clock, above. There is no free version of later out here.

Three. If it cannot take a night of rain, it belongs behind a door. The rule that settles the room.

Four. Out here, cleaning is how you read the structure.

That last one deserves the space, because it is the oldest idea in this book arriving at its most serious use.

You will never find a popped fastener, a soft board end, or a spider web in a burner tube while sitting in a chair. You find them with a brush in your hand and your face close to the thing. This is the last chapter of the book, and it is the one where the oldest idea in it finally carries your whole weight: cleaning is not tidying. It is how you read what you are standing on.

Chapter 16 introduced Shine as cleaning-that-inspects, and it has been true in every room since. But a patio is the only space in this book where the thing you are cleaning is also the thing holding you up.

You cannot see a soft board from a chair. You can feel it through a deck brush.

The inversion

Indoors the rule is dry before wet. Out here you rinse first, and that is the nine rules being applied intelligently rather than broken. Grit left on an outdoor surface does not lift under a cloth, it grinds. Float it off with the hose, then scrub, and you are cleaning the thing instead of sanding it.

Dry-before-wet exists indoors because wetting dry debris turns it into a paste you then remove twice; flour in a pantry is the clean example. Outdoors the debris is sand and grit, and a damp cloth dragged across it makes an abrasive pad. You are not cleaning the chair frame at that point, you are polishing it with sandpaper.

So: hose first, then scrub, then dry the things that must not stay wet, which are bolt heads, hollow frame tubing, the umbrella collar, and the inside of the deck box. Same nine rules, read properly rather than recited.

The kit for outdoors is short and it is mostly brushes, because almost everything out here is cleaned by agitation and rinsing rather than by chemistry. One long-handled deck brush, one stiff scrubbing brush, one detail brush, a bristle-free grate scraper, a hose, gloves, and a cushion cleaner that will not strip the fabric's water resistance. Everything else on this patio is furniture.

One word about the grate scraper, because it is the only input in this book that exists to replace something rather than to add to it. A wire grill brush sheds bristles; a shed bristle sticks to a grate, then to food, and a swallowed one is a genuine emergency. If you can pull a bristle free from your grill brush with your fingers, that brush is finished today, and the replacement is not another wire brush.

Quick Win

Go outside now, before you read another page, and push the top rail hard at every post, outward and then down. It takes ninety seconds. Any movement at all, any softness under your thumb, any screw head standing proud is a stop and fix today, not a note for the weekend.

The centrepieceZone by Zone: Exactly How to Clean It

Six zones. The first one on a dry morning, before anything else.

Zone 1 · The Surface, Rail, and Safety Zone

Purpose: the structure you stand on and lean against, kept sound and kept clear.

Inputs

  • Long-handled deck scrub brush
  • Stiff scrubbing brush
  • Garden hose
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Floor-material-compatible cleaner
  • Colour-coded microfiber cloth set
  • Push broom
  • A putty knife or a paint stirrer
  • A screwdriver
  • Portable cleaning caddy

Steps

  1. Clear the zone completely first. The planter that has drifted into the walking route, the hose coiled across the top step, the bag of paving sand leaning on a rail post: all of it comes off the deck, not to one side of it. Each was holding damp against the surface underneath as well as standing in your way.
  2. Sweep the whole deck with the push broom, working toward one edge, before any water touches it.
  3. Rake every gap between the boards clear with the putty knife or the paint stirrer, running the blade the full length until you can see daylight through the gap. A packed gap holds water against the board ends, and board ends are where rot begins.
  4. Brush the debris out of the line where the boards meet the house wall or the ledger. That junction traps the most leaf litter and stays wettest for longest, and almost nobody cleans it.
  5. Hose the deck to float the loose grit off, then scrub the boards with the deck brush and the neutral pH cleaner, working with the grain, and rinse the dirt off toward the edge.
  6. Scrub the green algae film off the stair treads with the stiff brush, then wipe down the risers and the stringers beside them. That shaded film is the slipperiest surface anywhere on the property after rain.
  7. Wash the top rail where hands leave grime, then work down the balusters and into the gaps between the spindles where dirt and cobwebs gather out of sight.
  8. Brush the grit out of the joint where each rail post meets the deck and wipe the base plate, clearing the debris that keeps the fixing wet, then wipe each step-light lens with a barely damp cloth and dry it immediately. Finally, sweep out whatever has drifted or been stored beneath the deck so the underside of the boards can breathe.

Inspect and flag as you go: a board end or a blocked gap that stays dark and spongy under the brush is rot setting in; press it and flag any board that gives. Rust bleeding out of a fastener and staining the board around it means the screw is corroding below the surface. A proud screw head or a lifted splinter that catches the brush is a bare foot waiting to happen, so note exactly where it sits. A tread that flexes underfoot as you scrub, or a stringer gone soft at its base, is a stair losing its footing. A step light dead or fogged behind its lens, and algae that regrows fast in one particular spot, both point to water pooling where it should be draining.

6S Tip

Rake the gaps between the boards until you can see daylight through them. It is the least visible job on this list and the one that buys the most time, because a deck almost never rots from the top down. It rots from the end grain outward, in a gap you cannot see into.

Zone 2 · The Outdoor Seating Zone

Purpose: seats people can drop into without checking them first, with the cushions kept dry.

Inputs

  • Garden hose
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Stiff scrubbing brush
  • Fabric-safe outdoor cushion cleaner
  • Colour-coded microfiber cloth set
  • Floor-material-compatible cleaner
  • Push broom
  • Portable cleaning caddy

Steps

  1. Sit in every chair before you clean anything. The one that wobbles and the one with the cracked resin arm are decisions, not furniture, and they are easier to make while you are sitting in them.
  2. Hose each frame down completely first to float the loose grit off, so nothing is dragged across the finish when you start wiping.
  3. Go over the arms and the top of the seat pan with a cloth and the neutral pH cleaner, then rinse and let the run-off carry the dirt down and away from the chair rather than around it.
  4. Scrub along the woven webbing with the stiff brush and a little soapy water, working across the weave rather than along it, and rinse until the water runs clear. Grit packed into a weave grinds the straps thin from the inside.
  5. Brush into each joint where arm meets leg, where dirt cakes and holds moisture against the metal, then wipe the bolt heads dry with a microfiber cloth so nothing sits wet in the seam.
  6. Stand each chair on its side, or tip it, so the water trapped inside the hollow frame tubing runs out of the open leg ends instead of sitting in there all summer.
  7. Brush the loose debris off the cushions, then sponge the fabric with the cushion cleaner and a soft cloth, blotting rather than soaking.
  8. Stand each cushion on its edge with both faces open to the air and let it dry all the way through before it goes anywhere near the deck box.
  9. Shake the covers out, wipe them with the neutral pH cleaner, and hang them open to dry rather than refolding them damp.
  10. Sweep and mop the strip of floor behind the planters where a crumpled cover has been shedding leaf litter and holding damp against the boards.

Inspect and flag as you go: black speckling on the underside of a cushion is mildew starting in the foam, so note which cushion before it spreads to the set. Rust weeping from a bolt head or a weld, or an orange stain running down a leg, tells you the frame is corroding from the fixing outward. Frayed webbing, or a strap pulling loose from its rivet, is the seat that will let someone through, so flag the exact chair. Water still running from a frame long after everything else has drained points to a split in the tubing holding a reservoir inside. A cover that went away damp comes out musty with a bloom in the folds; flag it to be washed, not refolded.

Zone 3 · The Outdoor Dining Zone

Purpose: feed people outdoors without carrying the kitchen out in four trips.

Inputs

  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Small detail brush set
  • Soft clothing brush
  • Fabric-safe outdoor cushion cleaner
  • Colour-coded microfiber cloth set
  • Nitrile gloves
  • Compact vacuum with attachments
  • Floor-material-compatible cleaner
  • Portable cleaning caddy

Steps

  1. Clear the table down to bare surface and face what has been living on it. Nothing goes back until the zone is finished.
  2. Wash the tabletop along the grain with the neutral pH cleaner, following the boards or slats rather than cutting across them, then rinse and let it sheet off the far edge.
  3. Work a detail brush all the way around the collar where the umbrella pole passes through the table, flush it clean, and dry it. That ring collects grit and holds a puddle directly against the wood.
  4. Wearing gloves, run a cloth along the underside lip of the table where hands grip to pull chairs in. Clear any early mud tube or papery nest start you meet, and wipe the rail clean.
  5. Wipe the chair seats and backs, then take the detail brush to the rungs and the joints where grime rings the wood, and reach the undersides of the seats where nobody looks.
  6. Open the umbrella canopy fully and brush the bird mess and cobwebs off with the soft brush, then sponge the fabric with the cushion cleaner.
  7. Leave the canopy open until it is completely dry before you furl it, so it never closes on damp, and wipe the grit out of the crank and off the ribs while you wait.
  8. Hose and wipe the umbrella base, and run a cloth down the pole to lift the film that builds where hands and rain both reach it.
  9. Wipe each string-light bulb and the cord with a barely damp cloth, never a wet one, then clean the rail behind the clips where the cord traps dirt.
  10. Vacuum the crumbs and leaf litter from under the table and between the chair feet, then mop with the floor-material-compatible cleaner.

Inspect and flag as you go: a papery nest start or a mud tube under the table edge is a wasp or mud-dauber setting up, so flag it before it is occupied. Canopy fabric gone thin and chalky, or stitching pulling away at the ribs, is ultraviolet rot; note where light shows through the weave. Rust weeping at a rib joint, or a rib that will not sit straight, tells you the frame is seizing. Cracked or brittle insulation on a string-light cord, or a connector that is warm or discoloured, is worth flagging while the strand is still in your hand. Standing water that will not leave the pole collar, with a dark ring in the wood around it, means that joint is staying wet long enough to rot.

The Night-of-Rain Challenge

Walk the patio with whoever else uses it and stop at every single object on it, including the ones that have been there so long they have stopped registering. For each one, say out loud: could this take a night of rain? Not could it survive one. Could it take one, tonight, and be no worse in the morning. The answers are not arguable, which is what makes this work with other people in a way that most decluttering conversations do not. Anything that gets a no has a home behind a door, or it does not belong on this patio.

Zone 4 · The Grill and Outdoor Cooking Zone

Purpose: cook over fire without leaving the grill to find a tool or wondering if there is gas.

Inputs

  • Grill grate scraper or coiled brush
  • Food-area degreaser
  • Nylon-bristled brush from the detail brush set
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Colour-coded microfiber cloth set
  • Hot soapy water and a bucket
  • Floor-material-compatible cleaner
  • Portable cleaning caddy

Steps

  1. Run the burners hot for ten minutes with the lid down to char the residue on the grates to ash. This is the only place outdoors where heat does the work chemistry would do indoors.
  2. Scrape the bars clean with the grate scraper or a coiled brush, never a wire one, then lift the grates out and finish them with the degreaser and hot soapy water.
  3. Pull the flavorizer bars and scrape the baked-on crust off them into the bin, then scrape the loose carbon flakes off the firebox floor down toward the grease channel while everything is out.
  4. With the bars lifted, look straight up into each burner tube and at the venturi opening by the valve end, and brush any nest debris out of the mouth of the tube so the gas runs clean.
  5. Slide the grease tray out of its rails and scrape the rendered layer into the bin rather than leaving it to feed the next flare-up.
  6. Wash the tray and the drip pan with the food-area degreaser and hot soapy water, and dry both before they go back in the rails.
  7. Brush the flaking down off the underside of the lid into the firebox with the nylon brush. That flaking is carbonised grease, not paint, and it comes off in sheets once you commit to it.
  8. Degrease the lid and body, then go over the side shelves, the tool hooks, and the control knobs with the neutral pH cleaner so no grease film is left to gather dust.
  9. Wipe the propane tank and its collar down, then clean the grease and grit off the cart legs and wheels so nothing tracks grime as the grill is rolled.
  10. Clean the decking or pad directly beneath the grill with the floor-material-compatible cleaner, before the spattered grease sets and turns that patch slick.

Inspect and flag as you go: a spider web or debris blocking a venturi tube is what makes a flame run yellow and lazy along one side, so if a burner cooks unevenly, flag that tube. Rust flaking through the firebox floor, or a hole opening in it, means the base is failing where the heat and grease sit; note where the light comes through. Grease that has pooled past the tray and soaked into the pad or the boards is a drip path the tray is no longer catching. A cracked or perished hose, or green corrosion crusting the regulator fitting, is worth flagging the moment you see it. Burner ports rusted or clogged on one side and clear on the other explain a flame strong at one end and absent at the other.

And one check that is not cleaning. Before the first cook of the season, brush soapy water onto the propane hose and the regulator fitting, then open the valve. Bubbles mean the hose gets replaced. Not tightened, not watched, not used carefully this once. Replaced. That test happens before you smell gas, which is the only time it is any use.

Zone 5 · The Garden and Plant Care Zone

Purpose: keep the containers alive and keep everything that feeds them off the ground and out of reach.

Inputs

  • Stiff scrubbing brush
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Garden hose
  • Small detail brush set
  • Light machine oil
  • Nitrile gloves
  • Push broom
  • Floor-material-compatible cleaner
  • Portable cleaning caddy

Steps

  1. Tip the standing water out of every saucer before you clean anything else in this zone. Do this first, every time, for a reason that has nothing to do with tidiness: standing water breeds mosquitoes.
  2. Scrub the white crust and algae from each saucer with the brush and the neutral pH cleaner, rinse, and dry it before it goes back under the pot.
  3. Scrub the chalky mineral crust off the pot rims and down the outsides with the stiff brush and water, working the ring where salts have wept through the clay, then rinse.
  4. Tip each pot and check the drain holes are open, poking a clogged one clear with a stick or a spare stake, and rinse the base where roots and grit have matted over the holes.
  5. Wipe the strip of wall and the shelf the pots stand against, where damp soil has printed rings and moss has taken hold in the shade.
  6. Scrape the caked soil off the trowel and off the pruner blades, wipe them down, and dry them fully rather than putting them back damp.
  7. Open the pruners and put a single drop of light machine oil on the pivot before they go back in the caddy.
  8. Tip the grit out of the bottom of the tool caddy and wipe it out, then rinse the watering can and its rose so nothing sits stagnant in it.
  9. Wipe the outside of the latched chemical box and its latch. The contents stay sealed in their own containers; this is a wipe of the box only, and nothing gets opened, decanted, or combined.
  10. Sweep up the spilled compost and dried soil crust, then mop the floor of the zone so no fertiliser residue is left underfoot.

Inspect and flag as you go: a dark damp ring on the decking under a saucer that never dries out points to a saucer overflowing or a pot draining straight onto the boards. Rust and pitting on a blade, or a pruner spring that has lost its snap, is a tool on its way out; note it as you dry it. A hairline crack starting at a pot's drain hole, or a glazed pot that rings dull instead of clear, is the pot that will split with the next frost. Wrigglers in the standing water of a saucer or a watering can are mosquito larvae, so flag any water that has been sitting long enough to breed. A chewed corner, a leak, or a swollen container spotted inside the chemical box is a spill risk to flag, not to sort out here.

Zone 6 · The Outdoor Storage Zone

Purpose: get the cushions under a lid within a minute of the first drop of rain.

Inputs

  • Stiff scrubbing brush
  • Warm soapy water and a bucket
  • Neutral pH multi-surface cleaner
  • Colour-coded microfiber cloth set
  • Nitrile gloves
  • Push broom
  • Floor-material-compatible cleaner
  • Portable cleaning caddy

Steps

  1. Empty the deck box onto the boards. All of it, in one go, exactly as Chapter 48 asked of the hall closet, and for the same reason: you cannot sort a deep container in place.
  2. Before you handle anything, look for mouse droppings or shredded nesting material. If you find either, put the gloves on, and wash the fabric before folding rather than shaking it out.
  3. Wipe the top of the lid with the neutral pH cleaner, clearing the leaf litter and pollen that cakes on and washes into the seam every time it rains.
  4. Wipe the inside face of the lid, then brush the grit and cobweb out of the hinge line so nothing gums up the pivot.
  5. Wipe the rubber sealing edge along its fold, where the closure actually happens and where nobody ever cleans.
  6. Scrub the interior walls with warm soapy water and rinse them, then scrub the floor of the box.
  7. Check the drain holes in the base are open, poking any that are blocked clear so water has a way out of the box rather than a way to sit in it.
  8. Prop the lid open on a dry, breezy day and leave the box until the corners are genuinely dry to the touch, not merely cool. This is the one step in this chapter that is mostly waiting, and it is the one that decides whether the cushions survive.
  9. Wipe the outside of the labelled totes, confirm the labels still read, and brush and air the cushions alongside the open box.
  10. Tip the box forward and sweep out the packed layer of leaf mulch built up underneath it, mop the footprint, then wipe the latch and carry handles so the closure still seats properly.

Inspect and flag as you go: mouse droppings or shredded nesting material tells you the box has a resident, so flag it before you handle the contents. A gnaw hole or a gap at a corner joint is how they got in; note where light shows through the seam. A musty smell, or a corner that stays damp after airing, means the box is not sealing or not draining as it should. Black mildew spotting spreading up the interior walls is moisture that has been trapped long enough to bloom. A pool that will not leave the base points to drain holes blocked rather than doing their job.

The hard callsThe Calls That Matter Here

Three, and they are the three every patio produces.

The board that gives

There is a board you step over without thinking, or one that flexes slightly under your weight, and you have quietly filed it as fine.

Test it properly instead. Push a screwdriver into the board where it crosses a joist, and again into the board end nearest the house. If the tip sinks in under light pressure, that board is finished.

The harder judgment is what a soft board is telling you. One soft spot on top is a board. Two soft boards side by side, or softness at the ledger where the deck meets the wall of the house, is not a board problem at all, and the surface is only the symptom.

So the rule that settles it: soft in one spot on top, replace the board. Soft in two adjacent places, or soft at the wall, stop putting people on it and get someone underneath it with a torch before the next time you have guests standing out here together.

That is where this book stops, deliberately. Recognising that a structure needs looking at is a 6S skill. Deciding what to do underneath it is not.

The half bottles

There is a shelf of part-used concentrates: a weed killer bought for one path, a rooting hormone, a bug spray whose label has washed away to nothing. You avoid that shelf, because there is no easy way to be rid of them, and pouring them down a drain is not one of them.

Two rules settle it. First, if you cannot read the label, that container is finished, no exceptions, because an unlabelled garden chemical cannot be used safely by you or by anyone who finds it later. It goes to the household hazardous waste collection. Second, if you can read it, ask whether you have used it in the last two growing seasons. If not, it goes the same way.

What survives is a short row you can name from the doorway. Then change what you buy, or you will rebuild the shelf: from here on, buy the smallest container the shop sells rather than the best value size. The value size is what created it.

Common Mistake

Buying replacement cushions before fixing the reason the last set died. The reason is almost never the cushions. It is that they had nowhere dry to go in a hurry, so a new set will follow the old one into the same damp box by the same route. Fix the box first. Then buy the cushions, if you still need to.

What the box is actually for

You bought the deck box for cushions. It now holds everything on this patio with nowhere else to be, so the cushions ride on a layer of pool toys and dead covers, never go all the way down, and the lid stands half open in the rain.

Decide the deck box's single job and hold it against pressure, because a box that cannot close protects nothing inside it. It stores cushions, since cushions are the only thing out here that has to be under a lid within a minute of the first drop. Everything else that has crept in gets a real home indoors or leaves this weekend. Carrying it out will look like waste while you are doing it. It is not. The arrangement you have now is already costing you the cushions, one damp night at a time.

The lid is the measure, and it cannot be talked round. If somebody has to sit on the box to close it, something leaves the box that day. Not eventually. That day.

The hazardsWatch for on the Patio

This is the last room in the book and the only one where all five safety questions answer at full strength. The mudroom had no meaningful fire load; the stair landing had nothing to say about poison. Out here every question lands.

Fall. A railing post that moves will not hold an adult who stumbles into it, and the drop off the side of a deck is onto whatever is stored below. Algae film on shaded boards and stair treads turns to grease after rain, and the treads are where a slip costs the most.

Burn or fire. Accumulated grease in the tray and drip pan is what turns a normal flare-up into a fire, and a grill within reach of siding, decking rail, or a canopy gives that fire somewhere to go. Keep a clear arm's length on every side of the grill body.

Poison, choke, or strangle. Slug pellets and fertiliser granules look like food to a dog. A concentrate decanted into a drink bottle is the classic route to a poisoning in a house with children, which is why nothing out here is ever moved out of the container it came in. And a bristle shed from a worn wire brush ends up on a grate, then on food.

Fall, cut, or crush. A deck box lid is heavy and hinged at exactly a child's hand height. Lift it and let go halfway; if it drops on its own, fit a lid stay before anything else in this zone, because crushed fingers in that hinge is the injury this zone reliably produces. Sun-aged resin chairs fail suddenly at the leg joint, and they fail under the person least able to catch themselves. A large glazed pot full of wet soil is far heavier than it looks and it lands on feet, so slide it on a wheeled saucer rather than lifting it.

Water and electricity. An indoor extension lead run out to string lights or a speaker, lying on boards that get wet, is the one electrical shortcut outdoors that genuinely kills. Outdoor-rated cord, clipped up off the floor, on a socket with a residual current device.

Five for five, and not a coincidence: a space with no roof has no boundary doing quiet work on its behalf, so every hazard a wall would have moderated arrives at full strength.

The ritualBefore and After

This is the patio the whole loop was for: daylight visible through the gaps between the boards, every rail post immovable when you push it hard, no green film on the shaded treads, a bare table but for the umbrella and one tray, the grates scraped bare, and a deck box whose lid closes flat without anyone sitting on it. Take the before photo before you touch a thing, and take the after as the standard. Then put it beside the very first photograph you took, fifty chapters ago, of a room you had not yet learned how to read.

Do that last part properly, because it is the only instruction in this book that requires the whole book to have happened.

Back in Chapter 6 you were asked to photograph a room before you touched it, and at the time it probably felt like an odd request. You did not know what you were looking at yet. You could see that the room was cluttered; you could not see that the coats on the chair were a hook problem, that the drawer was full of postponed judgments rather than objects, or that the thing on the stairs was waiting for a trip that was never going to happen with it in hand.

Put that photograph next to this one. The difference between them is not tidiness. It is that you can now read a room.

One Idea to Keep

This is the only room whose standard visibly ages, and that is the last thing this book has to teach you. Weather will undo part of it, and that is not a failure of the standard, it is the reason there is a standard at all. You never finished any of the other rooms either. You renewed them, and the patio is simply honest enough to show you the clock. Do one zone this week, and make it the boards and the rail, because everything else out here is arranged on top of them.

There is one more thing this room does that no other room in the book could have done, and it is why it is last.

Every indoor room let you believe, a little, that done was a state you could arrive at; they drift slowly enough that the illusion survives. The patio will not let you believe it. By August the algae is back on the shaded tread and the gaps have packed again, and if you understood done as a destination then that feels like failure and you stop.

It is not failure. It is the clock, which was always running in every other room too, just quietly enough to ignore.

Chapter 30 said you do not finish a 6S home, you renew it, and put the next event on the calendar before this one ends. Out here that stops being a good idea and becomes simply what is true. This is the room where the book's last lesson is not something you take on trust, because you can watch it happen.

And the method ends where it started. Chapter 46 noticed it first, in the workshop: all this came out of a factory floor where the stakes were fingers, and where the whole point was that a space tells you the truth about itself if you clean it carefully enough. Nineteen rooms later, on a deck, with a brush in your hand and your face close to a board, the point is exactly the same one.

Fifty chapters. Twenty rooms. Six S's, and one photograph you now know how to take.

Go and push the rail.
