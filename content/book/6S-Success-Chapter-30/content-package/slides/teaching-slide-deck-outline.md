### Slide 4 · A line has an end, a loop does not

- Almost everyone, when they first meet 6S, pictures it as a line, because a book has chapters that run in order and a project has a beginning and an end, so the mind quietly assumes that keeping a home works the same way: you do the six steps, reach the last one, and are finished, the way you finish painting a room or assembling a shelf. That picture is comfortable, and it is also the single most expensive mistake you can make from here, because a home is not a shelf.
- Life keeps arriving. This is not a failing of your systems; it is simply what a lived-in home is. New things come through the door, seasons change what you use and where, children grow into different needs, jobs and habits and hobbies shift, and the standard you set last spring is not quite the best way for this autumn. None of that is a crisis, and none of it means you did anything wrong; it is the tide of a life being lived, and it comes in whether or not your project was declared finished. A line has no answer for any of that, because a line has already ended. A loop does, because a loop expects it: it comes back around, takes a fresh look, clears what has crept in, updates what has changed, and sets off again, a little wiser.
- The loop-not-line reframe, frozen: "You have been picturing 6S as a line with an end: you sort, you straighten, you shine, and one day you are done. But a home is never done, because life keeps arriving. 6S is not a line but a loop, and the last step of every turn is simply deciding when the next turn begins."

### Slide 5 · The 6S event

- So what actually turns the loop? A daily rhythm holds the home steady day to day, but it is deliberately tiny, and tiny upkeep, however faithful, cannot catch everything. Over months, things still accumulate below the level a two-minute reset would notice: a drawer slowly fills with the not-quite-right, a shelf collects a category it was never meant to hold, a standard drifts a little out of date. What clears all of that is not a bigger daily time, which would just become a chore, but an occasional, deliberate return to the whole area, and that return is the 6S event.
- It is worth being clear about what an event is not, because the word can sound daunting: it is not the long, dreaded, whole-weekend overhaul that people put off and then resent. Because the area has already been done, and because your daily rhythm and regular check-ins have been holding it steady, an event on a well-kept space is light: an afternoon, or even an hour, spent carrying that one area briskly back around the six S's you already know by heart. You sort what has drifted in, straighten what has wandered, give it a proper shine, cast the safety eye over it again, refresh any standard that has gone stale, and confirm the daily rhythm is still doing its job. Then, the step that matters most, you decide when you will do it again, and you write that date down. Picture it on the entryway on a Saturday morning in early autumn, the date circled last spring, carried briskly around the loop in under an hour, so that at the end it is not merely tidy, it is renewed, a little better than it was in spring, and it cost an hour.
- The definition box, frozen: "A 6S event is a scheduled return to an area you have already done: a short, whole-area re-run of the six S's that clears what has crept back in, refreshes the standards, and raises the bar a little. It is the occasional renewal that keeps a good home from slowly becoming an old one, and its most important step is choosing the date of the next one." The book line, verbatim: "That is a 6S event: not a reckoning, just a good turn around a loop you already know."

### Slide 6 · Three clocks, one calm home

- Step back and see the whole of Sustain at once, because this event is the last of three practices that have been quietly assembling into a single, complete way of keeping a home. You met them one at a time, but they were always meant to run together, each on its own clock. The daily times run fastest and lightest, a couple of anchored minutes every day, holding the ordinary drift at bay so gently you barely notice. The regular check-in, the friendly audit, runs slower, a quick walk every week or month that catches the small slips the daily rhythm missed, while they are still small. And the 6S event runs slowest of all, an afternoon every season or so, that renews the whole area from the top and lifts it a little higher than before.
- You need all three, rather than just the one that appeals to you, because each catches a kind of drift the others cannot. The daily times will keep a surface clear, but they will never notice that a whole category of thing has slowly outgrown its shelf. The regular check-in will spot that the shelf is straining, and flag it, but it is too quick and too light to actually re-home the category and rebuild the standard around it; only the event has the time and the intent to do that deeper work. And equally, the event alone would be hopeless, because a space left entirely to a seasonal overhaul would drift into chaos long before the season turned. The fast clock keeps the slow clock's job small; the slow clock does the work the fast clock cannot reach.
- The three-cadences line, frozen: "Keeping a home runs on three clocks: the daily times that take a few minutes, the regular check-in that catches drift in one, and the occasional 6S event that renews the whole area from the top. The small ones hold the line day to day; the event is what lifts the home back to its best and sets the bar a little higher." The book line, verbatim: "They are not three competing chores you must choose between. They are one rhythm, layered, and a home held on all three timescales at once is a home with nowhere left to slide."

### Slide 7 · Schedule the next one before this one ends

- Everything in this chapter comes down to one small habit, and it is so small it is easy to skip, which is exactly why so many homes that were beautifully sorted once are cluttered again a year later: you schedule the next event before you finish the current one. Not afterward, when the memory has faded and the calendar is closed and the good intentions have quietly evaporated, but right then, in the last few minutes of the event itself, while everyone is standing in the renewed space and it is obvious how good it feels. That is the only moment the next date reliably gets set, and it is the difference between a loop and a line wearing a loop's clothes: a person who says "we should do this again sometime" has drawn a line and is merely hoping it bends back; a person who writes "spring: the garage again" on an actual calendar has closed the loop.
- There is a neat trick that makes the date even harder to lose, the same one you used to anchor the daily times, scaled up to the year: hang each event on a marker the calendar already has. Tie the garage to the first warm weekend of spring, the children's rooms to the week before the school year starts, the whole-home refresh to the quiet days after the winter holidays. Anchored to a moment the year was always going to bring, the event needs no more remembering than sunrise does; the season itself becomes the reminder. And there is a quiet gift in doing it this way, the spirit the whole method finally rests on: each turn you see a little more, and the home rises with you, not toward some fixed and final perfect state, which does not exist, but simply a little better each time, forever.
- The pursue-perfection reframe, frozen: "Perfection in a home is not a place you arrive at and stay. It is a direction you keep facing. Each 6S event does not only restore the home to where it was, it lifts it a little higher, because you always notice something this time that you were not ready to notice last time. You do not chase a finish line, you keep raising the one you have." Quick Win, verbatim: "Before you close this book, open your calendar and put one date on it: your next 6S event. Pick an area you have already done, name the day, and the loop has begun." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Schedule the next event at the end of this one, while the area is fresh in everyone's mind and the calendar is open. Tie it to a season or a repeating date so it never has to be remembered, only kept."
