## Chapter Opening

You have come a long way into the fifth S. You have the idea of a standard, the best way you know, written down where anyone can follow it. You have the three tools that make a standard actually run: a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it has a time, and a name so someone owns it. For most of the recurring work of a home, that is enough, and it is a great deal.

But you have almost certainly run into the edge of it by now, the place where words start to strain. Try to write down, in sentences, exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, and you will find yourself piling up clauses that still do not quite capture it: mugs here, but not like that, spaced but not too spaced, and the whole thing somehow still comes out looking fussier and less clear than the shelf itself. Some things simply resist description. How the cables should be coiled and where they tuck. What the entryway looks like when it is genuinely reset. Where each thing sits when the room is right. For all of these, a paragraph is the wrong tool, and there is a far better one sitting in your pocket.

This chapter is that better tool: the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell. It is the last kind of standard the fifth S needs, and adding it completes Standardize. When you have it, the parts of your home that were hardest to put into words become the easiest of all to keep, because "right" stops being a description someone has to interpret and becomes a picture they can simply match.

## When Words Are Not Enough

There is a particular kind of standard that fails not because it is wrong but because it is written. Everyone reads the same careful sentences and pictures something slightly different, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is exactly how a shelf that was meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.

> A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

> A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.

This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments: it is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable. My tidy and your tidy are genuinely different pictures, and no amount of careful writing fully closes the gap, because we are trying to transmit an image through the narrow pipe of language and each person rebuilds it a little differently at the other end. The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret. You are not comparing your idea of tidy to mine; we are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it. The disagreement was never really about tidiness. It was about the fact that we were each holding a different picture, and a shared picture is the thing that fixes it.

## The Picture of the Ideal State

So here is the flagship tool of this chapter, and it is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works.

> Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.

That is the whole move. You get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts, you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, taped inside the cupboard door or stuck to the wall beside the shelf, and from that moment the standard for that space is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door.

> The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.

You have actually done a version of this before, at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That before photo was the visual baseline, the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image: not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." The same simple act of pointing a camera, aimed now at the target instead of the starting line. A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo, which is a fair sign of how low this tool sets the bar for being useful.

## The Storyboard

A photo of the ideal state is perfect for a space that should look a certain way. But some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest; they are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those, one photo is not enough. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one.

> For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.

This is a storyboard, borrowed, without any ceremony, from the way films are planned: a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order. How the folding table gets set up and stowed. How the cables coil and where they clip. How the recycling gets sorted on collection night. For a task like these, three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is not converting words into actions in their head; they are just copying what they see, frame by frame. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

## Make It Match-able

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that, which is worth a paragraph because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used.

Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light that the person will actually have when they go to set the space right. A photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Get down or up to the real eye line, use the ordinary light of the room, and capture the space the way it is actually seen. Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible; a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. And keep it honest rather than impressive. A few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder of visual standards that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

> **Quick Win**
> Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match.

> **6S Tip**
> Shoot the standard photo from the exact angle and in the same light someone will see the space from when they go to set it right. A picture shot from an odd angle or in different light is hard to match, and a standard that is hard to match is one people quietly stop using.

## Standardize, Complete

That is Part Seven, and it is the fifth S, done. It is worth pausing on, because it changed the nature of what you have built. For the first six parts of this book you made your home good: cleared, arranged, clean, and safe. Standardize did something different. It made that goodness durable, took it out of your head and your hands and your good days, and put it somewhere it can survive a tired week and a house full of other people.

You did it in three moves. In Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote the first one down: the best way you know today, captured on a card. In Chapter 24 you gave standards the three tools that make them run: a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, a name for the who. And in this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words: the ideal-state photo, the storyboard, the visual standard.

> With this, the fifth S is complete. The best way you know is written down, carried by a checklist and a schedule and a name, and, where words fall short, shown in a picture. Your home no longer runs on your memory. It runs on what you have made visible.

There is one S left, and it is the one that turns all of this from an achievement into a way of life. You have built a home that is good and, now, held. What you have not yet done is keep it, not for a proud month but for years, through moves and new babies and busy seasons and every ordinary force that pulls a home back toward chaos. That work, the sixth S, is Sustain, and it is the whole of the last part. For now, stand back and look at what a standardized home actually is: not a home that depends on you remembering, but one that shows everyone, plainly, what right looks like.

> **Common Mistake**
> Building an elaborate laminated binder of visual standards that looks impressive and helps no one, instead of a few honest photos stuck where the work happens. The whole value of a visual standard is being seen at the point of use; a beautiful one filed away is worth nothing.

> **One Idea to Keep**
> When words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.
