## What the chapter says (in one paragraph)
You already have standards in words (Chapter 23) and the three tools that make them run (Chapter 24), and for most recurring work that is enough, but you have met the edge where words strain: try to write down exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, how the cables should coil, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you pile up clauses that still miss it, because some things simply resist description. A written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, so the problem is not the people and not the standard, it is the medium. The fix is the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell (the definition box and the show-do-not-tell reframe): a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like, captured as a picture rather than described in words. The chapter's flagship is the photo of the ideal state: photograph a space when it is exactly right, put the picture where the space is, and from then on tidy is not an opinion and not an argument but a picture on the cupboard door the room either matches or does not, the optimistic mirror image of the before photo you took back in Prepare, so low a bar that a child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo. For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph: borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard shows the few frames that matter in order, so the next person copies what they see instead of converting words into actions, and four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty, because the value is in the sequence being visible, not the pictures being beautiful. The craft is to make it match-able: shoot from the angle and in the light of use, post it at the point of use, and keep it honest rather than laminated, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms and a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more than a beautiful binder in a drawer. The friction meter reaches the STANDARDIZE COMPLETE milestone, the fifth mark on the dial after Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety: the needle takes a small, honest tick forward, and that smallness is the point, because Standardize was never about clearing big new friction, its gift is quieter and you see it in the catch, the anti-backslide catch that first appeared when you began to standardize and grew when standards got their tools is now fully engaged and locked, its firmest state, so the needle barely moved and the grip behind it is complete. Five of the six S's are done and the goal is a step away on the calm side, because one S remains: your home no longer runs on your memory, it runs on what you have made visible. Hands off to Chapter 26 · Sustain: Keeping the Gains, which opens Part Eight, the sixth and final S, the work of keeping a held home held not just today but over all the years to come.

## Frozen strings (copy verbatim; do not paraphrase)
- The rule (hero): **Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.**
- The pictures line (hero): **Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.**
- The storyboard line (hero): **For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.**
- The definition box (frozen): **A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.**
- The show-do-not-tell reframe (frozen): **A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.**
- The photo-of-the-ideal-state reframe (frozen): **The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.**
- The completion reframe (frozen, the Part 7 close): **With this, the fifth S is complete. The best way you know is written down, carried by a checklist and a schedule and a name, and, where words fall short, shown in a picture. Your home no longer runs on your memory. It runs on what you have made visible.**
- One Idea to Keep: **When words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.**
