# Chapter 25 Key Takeaways: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

The core ideas a reader should carry out of this chapter.

1. **This chapter completes Standardize.** It is the third and final chapter of Part Seven and completes the fifth S. Chapter 23 gave the concept of a standard, Chapter 24 gave standards their three tools, and this chapter adds the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list. With it, the fifth S is complete.

2. **Some things are far easier to show than to say.** Written standards run out at a particular edge: try to put into sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, how the cables should be coiled, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you pile up clauses that still miss it. Some things simply resist description, and for those a paragraph is the wrong tool.

3. **The problem is the medium, not the people.** A written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.

4. **The show-do-not-tell reframe, frozen.** A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

5. **The definition, frozen.** A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.

6. **The flagship: photograph the ideal state.** Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance. Get the space right on one of those rare days, take the photo before it drifts, and put it where the space is.

7. **The photo-of-the-ideal-state reframe, frozen.** The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.

8. **The ideal-state photo is the optimistic mirror of the before photo.** You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was the visual baseline, the honest record of the mess; the ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

9. **The storyboard, frozen.** For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.

10. **A storyboard carries a sequence a paragraph cannot.** Borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order. For setting up a folding table, coiling the cables, or sorting the recycling, three or four little pictures carry more and faster than a numbered list, because the reader copies what they see instead of converting words into actions. Four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty; the value is in the sequence being visible, not the pictures being beautiful.

11. **Make it match-able: shoot from the angle and light of use.** A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they set the space right; a photo taken from a stepladder when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned.

12. **Put it where the work happens, and keep it honest.** A visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. A few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

13. **The rule, frozen (hero).** Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

14. **The needle reaches Standardize complete, and the tick is honestly small.** The needle sits now at the Standardize-complete tick, the fifth on the dial, beside Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety complete. The tick forward was small, and that is honest: Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way Sort, Straighten, and Shine were. Its whole gift was quieter, and you see it in the catch, not the needle.

15. **The catch behind the needle is fully engaged now, locked.** The small catch that first appeared when you began to standardize, and grew stronger when standards got their tools, is fully engaged now. Everything is written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures, which means for the first time it is held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be. The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete.

16. **Five of the six S's are done, and the goal is a step away.** Five of the six S's are complete after this chapter. The goal is a step away on the calm side, because one S still remains, Sustain, which is the work of keeping a held home held not just today but over all the years to come.

17. **A visual standard distinct from a visual control.** A label, an outline, or a boundary (from Straighten's visual controls) makes a storage spot self-explaining. A visual standard is a different device: it shows the ideal state or result of a space or a task, what "done right" looks like, so a person can match reality against a picture. A label is a tiny visual standard; the ideal-state photo is the full one.

18. **Standardize, complete (the Part 7 retrospective).** You did it in three moves: in Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote one down; in Chapter 24 you gave standards the three tools that make them run; and in this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words. That goodness is now durable, out of your head and your good days.

19. **The completion reframe, frozen.** With this, the fifth S is complete. The best way you know is written down, carried by a checklist and a schedule and a name, and, where words fall short, shown in a picture. Your home no longer runs on your memory. It runs on what you have made visible.

20. **One S remains, and the goal is still ahead.** You have built a home that is good and, now, held. What you have not yet done is keep it, not for a proud month but for years. That work, the sixth S, is Sustain, and it is the whole of the last part. One Idea to Keep: when words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.
