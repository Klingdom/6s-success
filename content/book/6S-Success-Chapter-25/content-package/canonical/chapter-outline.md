## 1. Chapter Opening: The edge where words strain
You have come a long way into the fifth S. You have the idea of a standard, the best way you know written down, and the three tools that make a standard actually run, a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it has a time, and a name so someone owns it. For most of the recurring work of a home that is enough, and it is a great deal. But you have almost certainly run into the edge of it by now, the place where words start to strain: try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like and you pile up clauses that still do not quite capture it. Some things simply resist description, how the cables should be coiled, what the entryway looks like when it is genuinely reset, where each thing sits when the room is right, and for all of these a paragraph is the wrong tool, and there is a far better one sitting in your pocket. This chapter is that better tool: the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell. It is the last kind of standard the fifth S needs, and adding it completes Standardize.

## 3. When Words Are Not Enough
There is a particular kind of standard that fails not because it is wrong but because it is written: everyone reads the same careful sentences and pictures something slightly different, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments: it is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable, because we are trying to transmit an image through the narrow pipe of language and each person rebuilds it a little differently at the other end. The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret. You are not comparing your idea of tidy to mine; you are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the show-do-not-tell reframe): A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.
- Definition box (frozen): A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.

## 4. The Picture of the Ideal State
The flagship tool of the chapter, and it is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works. You get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, taped inside the cupboard door or stuck to the wall beside the shelf, and from that moment the standard for that space is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door. You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix: that was the visual baseline, the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the pictures line): Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the photo-of-the-ideal-state reframe): The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.

## 5. The Storyboard
A photo of the ideal state is perfect for a space that should look a certain way, but some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest; they are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those one photo is not enough. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one. A storyboard, borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order: how the folding table gets set up and stowed, how the cables coil and where they clip, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night. Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is not converting words into actions in their head; they are just copying what they see, frame by frame. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty.
- Pull-quote (frozen, the storyboard line): For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.
- Infographic (the storyboard): a short horizontal strip of three simple picture-frames showing one ordinary task's key steps in order, the entryway reset (frame 1: shoes on the rack; frame 2: bags on the hooks; frame 3: surface clear), each frame a small hand-drawn or photographed image with a two or three word caption beneath, an arrow leading from frame to frame. ## 6. Make It Match-able
A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that, because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees it from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Get down or up to the real eye line, use the ordinary light of the room, and capture the space the way it is actually seen. Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible; a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. And keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.
- Callout (Quick Win): Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match.
- Callout (6S Tip): Shoot the standard photo from the exact angle and in the same light someone will see the space from when they go to set it right. A picture shot from an odd angle or in different light is hard to match, and a standard that is hard to match is one people quietly stop using.

## 8. Standardize, Complete (the Part 7 retrospective)
That is Part Seven, and it is the fifth S, done. It is worth pausing on, because it changed the nature of what you have built. For the first six parts you made your home good: cleared, arranged, clean, and safe. Standardize did something different. It made that goodness durable, took it out of your head and your hands and your good days, and put it somewhere it can survive a tired week and a house full of other people. You did it in three moves: in Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote the first one down; in Chapter 24 you gave standards the three tools that make them run; and in this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words, the ideal-state photo, the storyboard, the visual standard. There is one S left, and it is the one that turns all of this from an achievement into a way of life. You have built a home that is good and, now, held. What you have not yet done is keep it, not for a proud month but for years. That work, the sixth S, is Sustain, and it is the whole of the last part.
