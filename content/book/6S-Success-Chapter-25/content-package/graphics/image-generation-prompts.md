# Image Generation Prompts: Chapter 25

**"Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards"**
*6S Success: Home Edition · Part Seven · Standardize*

Five prompts written for the book's warm, editorial, hand-made feel, in reading order. Palette to hold
across all five: warm paper `#F7F2E9`, panel cream `#FBF7EF`, deep ink brown `#2B2622`, terracotta
`#BC4B2A`, honey `#DDA63A`, slate blue `#3C5A6B` (photo frames and data), soft green `#6E8B5B`, a single
warm red `#CB4B36` reserved for the smallest marks only and used barely, if at all, on these calm images.
Natural soft daylight, shallow but not showy depth, real textures over polish.

The named space throughout is the finished home the reader has cleared, arranged, cleaned, made safe, and
standardized in words and tools. This chapter, the THIRD and FINAL of Part Seven, COMPLETES Standardize,
the fifth S, and does one turn: it names the edge where written standards strain (some things, what a tidy
shelf looks like, how the cables coil, where each thing sits when the room is right, are far easier to
show than to say, and the problem is not the people and not the standard, it is the medium) and delivers
the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list. It delivers the show-do-not-tell
reframe and the definition box (a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what
right looks like; a visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather
than described in words), delivers the flagship, the photo of the ideal state (photograph a space when it
is exactly right and that photo becomes the standard anyone can match reality against at a glance, the
optimistic mirror image of the before photo), delivers the storyboard (for a task with steps, a short
strip of pictures beats a paragraph, the few frames that matter in order, so the next person copies what
they see), delivers the craft of making it match-able (shoot from the angle and light of use, put it where
the work happens, keep it honest rather than laminated), and reads the friction meter as the STANDARDIZE
COMPLETE milestone with the catch behind the needle fully engaged and locked. Nothing of the earlier four
S's is re-taught, the concept of a standard from Chapter 23 and the three tools from Chapter 24 are used,
not re-explained, and no long-term Sustain work is built (the sixth S, named only as still to come, one
step away). Keep the tone calm, warm, and freeing: a written standard about how things should look is
almost infinitely arguable not because anyone is careless but because words rebuild a slightly different
picture at every reader, so the fix is simply to show the image instead. The visual standard is meant to
make right easier to reach, a gift and not a rule; the register is relief, the argument evaporating the
moment you show the picture. Crucially, describe only the calm, warm illustrated house style: no corporate
office look, no laminated binder or wall of procedures presented as virtue, no one standing over a
shoulder, no force or control framing (no enforce, comply, inspect), no cages, no photoreal polish; the
visual standard is one honest photo taped to a real door and its frames are light and hand-made.

No typeset text is baked into these images. Any label, name, or word mentioned below is a physical object
in the scene, a hand-lettered card, a handwritten line on a real sheet, a real photograph in a frame, or
the reading on a real gauge, not a typeset overlay. Separator discipline for any such incidental
hand-lettered label: use the middot, for example `CHAPTER 25 · STANDARDIZE`, never a floating hyphen or a
floating dot between words. Compound terms keep their internal hyphen: ideal-state, show-do-not-tell,
tidy-shelf, cupboard-door, name-tag, anti-backslide, before-photo, point-of-use, Sort-complete,
Straighten-complete, Shine-complete, Safety-complete, Standardize-complete, tired-week, match-able,
stick-figure.

## 1. The same shelves, two kinds of standard (the opener)

A warm, wide editorial illustration of one set of open kitchen shelves shown twice in soft daylight, to
make a single point. On the left, the shelves are being standardized the hard way: a wordy handwritten
card is taped beside them, covered in careful sentences, and a person stands frowning up at it with a
question mark overhead, trying to translate the words back into a picture, while the real shelves are close
but not quite right. On the right, the very same shelves, but taped to the cupboard door is a single clear
photograph of the shelves on the day they were exactly right, in a slate frame with a curl of tape, and a
person is simply glancing between the photo and the shelves and moving one mug, at ease, no reading
required. The left side is effortful; the right is calm and quick. Warm paper cream and honey counter
tones, ink-brown line work, slate for the photo frame, one honey accent on the single mug being matched,
soft green for the at-ease note, one terracotta for the question mark, no warm-red anywhere. Editorial
cookbook-style illustration. The same shelves, one you decode and one you match.

- Mood: a written standard about how a thing should look is almost infinitely arguable because words
  rebuild a slightly different picture at every reader; the moment you stop describing the image and simply
  show it, the argument evaporates and there is nothing left to interpret; the register is relief and ease,
  not blame; for anything you can see, the picture is the better standard.
- Avoid: glossy real-estate staging, cold blue tints, a corporate or office look, floating typeset text
  overlays, a laminated binder or wall of procedures presented as the answer, anyone being scolded or
  standing over another, a cage or lock, any warm-red accent, brand names or recognizable products; keep
  the left side genuinely effortful with the shelves close but not right, and the right side calm and
  quick, the very same shelves, the same goal, only now carried by one photo anyone can look at and match.

## 2. The photo of the ideal state (the hero infographic)

A warm editorial illustration of a cupboard door on the left carrying a small framed photograph of a shelf
at its best, in a slate frame with a curl of tape at the corner, and the hand-written words the photo of
the ideal state beneath it, in soft daylight. On the right, the real shelf, the two clearly matching, mug
for mug. A light slate note between them reads match this, with a gentle arrow from the photo to the real
shelf. Along the foot, a small camera icon and a hand-lettered line read photograph it on the day it is
right, and post it where the space is. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, slate photo
frame, note, and camera, one honey accent held to the single matched mug that also appears in the photo so
the eye reads the match at a glance, soft green only for a small match-tick if wanted, no warm-red
anywhere. A picture of one plain idea: photograph a space when it is exactly right, tape the photo where
the space is, and tidy stops being an opinion and becomes a picture the room either matches or does not.

- Mood: the most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph, the shelf, the counter, the
  entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right; nobody has to remember or interpret what tidy means,
  they look at the picture, look at the shelf, and match the two; the optimistic mirror image of the before
  photo, this is what it looks like at its best and this is what we are keeping it like; warm, honest, and
  low-effort.
- Avoid: a polished catalogue image instead of an honest snapshot, a laminated binder, a wall of framed
  procedures, a corporate slide, cold clinical light, floating typeset text, any clipboard or inspector,
  any warm-red, brand names or recognizable products; keep the photo small and honestly a real snapshot of
  this very shelf, the real shelf beside it matching mug for mug, the note a small hand-lettered match
  this, and the foot line photograph it on the day it is right, and post it where the space is.

## 3. The storyboard (a task shown, not told)

A warm editorial illustration of a short horizontal strip of three simple picture-frames showing one
task's key steps in order, the entryway reset, in soft daylight. Frame one shows two shoes on a rack,
captioned in a couple of hand-lettered words shoes on the rack. Frame two shows a bag hanging on a wall
hook, captioned bags on the hooks. Frame three shows a clear flat surface with a small soft green tick,
captioned surface clear. Small honey arrows lead from frame to frame, left to right. A thin hand-written
line under the strip reads a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown
line work, slate for the three frames and the small numerals, one honey accent held to the arrows between
frames, soft green only for the final surface-clear tick, no warm-red anywhere. A picture of one plain
idea: for a task with steps, a few pictures in order carry more, and carry it faster, than a paragraph.

- Mood: a storyboard borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a short strip of simple
  frames each showing one key step in order; the next person is not converting words into actions, they
  are just copying what they see, frame by frame; four phone snaps or quick stick-figure sketches are
  plenty; the value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.
- Avoid: an elaborate diagram, a glossy how-to spread, a clinical flowchart, gridlines everywhere, a
  corporate slide, cold clinical light, more than a few frames, floating typeset text, any warm-red, brand
  names or recognizable products; keep the three frames simple, even, and plainly hand-made, phone-snap or
  stick-figure plain, read clearly left to right in order, the captions a couple of words each, the foot
  line a small hand-lettered a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph.

## 4. Show, do not tell (the reframe)

A calm, warm illustration of the chapter's central turn, drawn as two gentle panels read left to right,
each the same shelf standardized a different way, in soft daylight. The left panel, tell: a written
standard, a card dense with careful sentences about how a shelf should look, and above it two people
rebuilding slightly different pictures from the same words, a gentle disagreement, drawn a little
effortful and dim. The right panel, show: the same shelf as a single photograph taped where the space is,
in a slate frame, and the same two people simply looking and matching, warm and lit, the argument gone. A
gentle arrow crosses from tell to show. Between or beneath the panels, a quiet hand-written line carries
the definition: where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right
looks like. To one side, a quieter, dimmer, unlit panel stands for the next chapter, Sustain, kept
low-contrast and clearly one step ahead. Warm paper cream background, ink-brown line work, the tell panel
dim and a little effortful, the show panel warm and lit, slate for the photo frame and the arrow, the
Sustain panel dim and unlit, no warm-red anywhere. The image reads as one plain idea: the problem was
never the people or the standard, it was the medium, so show the result instead of describing it.

- Mood: a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like, and for
  anything you would struggle to put into words that is the difference between a standard people argue
  about and one they can simply see and match; the problem is not the people and not the standard, it is
  the medium; the register is warmth and relief, not correction, nobody was careless, the words were simply
  the wrong tool.
- Avoid: drawing the written standard as villainous or the photo as flimsy, any scolding or shaming tone, a
  cage or lock or someone standing over a shoulder, a corporate office look, teaching any next-chapter
  Sustain content in the dim side panel, any warm-red, floating typeset text, hype; keep the tell panel dim
  and a little effortful and the show panel visibly warm and lit and easy, the register an argument that
  evaporates the moment you show the image.

## 5. The needle reaches Standardize complete, and the catch locks (the friction meter)

A hand-made editorial illustration of the book's recurring friction meter, a half-circle gauge dialing
from calm green on the left, through honey in the middle, to friction red on the right. This chapter the
dark live needle takes a small, honest step forward and reaches a clearly marked slate milestone tick on
the dial, the fifth, after Sort complete, Straighten complete, Shine complete, and Safety complete, so
five of the six S's are now done. The tick forward is deliberately small: just behind the live needle sits
the position it held last chapter, which is also the Safety-complete mark, now a faint passed marker, and
the Shine-complete, Straighten-complete, and Sort-complete milestones sit further back as small passed
markers. What has fully arrived this chapter is the catch behind the needle: the three-tooth ratchet whose
teeth stand for the checklist, the schedule, and the name, a list, a time, and a name, is now drawn fully
engaged and locked, its firmest state, with a small hand-drawn padlock on it, and the faint dashed
backslide arrow toward the friction side is barred solidly, hand-marked no backslide, so the home's gains
are held about as firmly as they can be. A slate crosshair labeled the goal, carrying its purpose flag,
still sits further out on the green calm side, clearly ahead of the live needle but now only a step away,
because one S remains, Sustain. Warm paper cream background, ink line work, consistent stroke weight,
friendly but not childish.

- Mood: the needle does move this chapter, but only a small honest step, because Standardize was never
  about clearing big new friction the way Sort, Straighten, and Shine were, so a big leap would be
  dishonest; the real gain is behind the needle, in the catch, now fully engaged and locked at its firmest
  state, everything written down, carried by tools, and shown in pictures, held against the slide back as
  firmly as a home can be; five of the six S's are done and the goal is a step away, because one S remains,
  Sustain, the work of keeping a held home held over the years.
- Avoid: the live needle leaping forward or reaching the goal crosshair, drawing this as a big forward
  step or the whole journey finished, a completion glow or celebratory confetti of the whole home, the
  passed markers or the catch drawn faint, no room left between the needle and the goal, hype, neon color.
  The needle takes only a small honest tick to the Standardize-complete milestone, the catch behind it is
  fully engaged and locked with a small padlock, the backslide is barred solidly, and a clear step remains
  between the needle and the goal.
