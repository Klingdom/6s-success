strip of three slate-framed frames of the entryway reset in order, shoes on the rack, bags on the hooks,

Scope fence: this chapter is the THIRD and FINAL chapter of Part Seven, and it COMPLETES Standardize, the
fifth of the six S's. It does one turn: it names the edge where written standards strain (some things,
what a tidy shelf looks like, how the cables coil, where each thing sits when the room is right, are far
easier to show than to say, and the problem is not the people and not the standard, it is the medium), and
it delivers the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list. It delivers the
show-do-not-tell reframe and the definition box (a written standard tells you what to do, a visual
standard shows you what right looks like; a visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task,
captured as a picture rather than described in words), delivers the flagship, the photo of the ideal state
(photograph a space when it is exactly right and that photo becomes the standard anyone can match reality
against at a glance, the optimistic mirror image of the before photo from Prepare), delivers the
storyboard (for a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph, the few frames that matter
in order, so the next person copies what they see rather than converting words into actions), delivers the
craft of making it match-able (shoot from the angle and in the light of use, put it where the work
happens, keep it honest rather than laminated, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in
terms), and reads the friction meter as a MILESTONE, the STANDARDIZE COMPLETE tick, with the anti-backslide
catch fully engaged and locked. It USES the written standard from Chapter 23 and the three tools from
Chapter 24 and does NOT re-teach them. CRITICAL fence: this is the visual standard, a picture of the ideal
state and a storyboard of the steps, NOT the concept of a standard (Chapter 23, already given), NOT the
three tools that make a standard run (Chapter 24, already given), and NOT the long-term adherence and
Sustain work (the sixth S, named only as still to come, one step away). A visual standard is also distinct
from a visual control from Straighten: a label or an outline makes a storage spot self-explaining, while a
visual standard shows the ideal state or result, what done right looks like, so a person can match reality
against a picture. Because this chapter completes the fifth S by adding the last kind of standard, the
friction needle takes a small, honest tick forward to the STANDARDIZE COMPLETE milestone, the fifth mark
on the dial after Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety; the tick is deliberately small, because Standardize
was never about clearing big new friction, and the real gain is behind the needle, in the catch, which is
now fully engaged and locked at its firmest state. Separator discipline: labels use the middot, for
example `CHAPTER 25 · STANDARDIZE`, never a floating hyphen or a floating dot between words. Compound
terms keep their internal hyphen: ideal-state, show-do-not-tell, tidy-shelf, cupboard-door, name-tag,
anti-backslide, before-photo, point-of-use, Sort-complete, Straighten-complete, Shine-complete,
Safety-complete, Standardize-complete, tired-week, match-able, stick-figure. Anti-shame throughout: a
written standard that people argue over is the normal case, not a failing; a standard about how things
should look is almost infinitely arguable not because anyone is careless but because the medium is words,
and words rebuild a slightly different picture at every reader. The visual standard is meant to make right
easier to reach, not to police anyone; a photo taped to a door is a gift, not a rule. No force or control
framing anywhere (no enforce, comply, inspect, no laminated binder presented as virtue, no one standing
over a shoulder); no corporate or office register; no hype; no brand names. The register is calm, warm,
human, and illustrated, the relief of turning the best way you know into something anyone can simply see
and match.

## 1. The Same Shelves, Two Kinds of Standard (the opener)

**Purpose:** Show the chapter's whole idea in one image. The same set of open shelves, standardized two
ways: on the left in careful written sentences that someone still has to decode, on the right in a single
photo of the shelves on the day they were right, with nothing to decode at all. Nothing about the goal
changes; what changes is the medium, words you translate versus a picture you match. Mirrors Figure 1 in
the HTML.

**What it shows:** One set of open kitchen shelves drawn twice in soft daylight. On the LEFT, the shelves
are being standardized the hard way: a wordy handwritten card is taped beside them, covered in sentences,
and a person stands frowning up at it with a question mark overhead, trying to translate the words back
into a picture, while the real shelves are close but not quite right. On the RIGHT, the very same shelves,
but taped to the cupboard door is a single clear photograph of the shelves on the day they were exactly
right, in a slate frame, and a person is simply glancing between the photo and the shelves and moving one
mug, at ease, no reading required. The left side is effortful; the right is calm and quick.

**Rough layout:**
- A warm counter-top wash on cream `#FBF7EF`. A faint Newsreader italic line, muted: `the same shelves,
  two kinds of standard: one you decode, one you match`.
- LEFT, `WRITTEN · DECODE IT` in soft sans caps at the foot: the shelves close but not quite right, a wordy
  handwritten card taped beside them in greyed paper tones `#ECE6DA` and `#9C9284` reading `the tidy-shelf
  standard`, a person frowning up at it with a terracotta `?` overhead, a small muted note `effortful`.
- RIGHT, `SHOWN · MATCH IT` in slate sans caps at the foot: the same shelves, a single clear ideal-state
  photograph taped to the cupboard-door in a slate frame with a cream face `#FCF8F0` and a curl of tape,
  the words `the photo of the ideal state` beneath it, a person calmly matching one honey `#DDA63A` accent
  mug to the picture, at ease, a small green note `at ease`.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter): "The same shelves, two kinds of standard. On the left, the best way
  written out in careful sentences that someone still has to decode. On the right, a single photo of the
  shelves on the day they were right, and nothing to decode at all: you look, you match, you are done. For
  anything you can see, the picture is the better standard."

**Note for the illustrator:** the point is the shift from a standard you have to translate to a standard
you simply match, so draw the left side genuinely effortful, the person frowning and the shelves close but
not right, and the right side calm and quick, the same shelves, the same goal, only now carried by one
photo anyone can look at and match. Hold the color discipline: slate for the photo frame and the SHOWN
label, terracotta for the question mark and the WRITTEN eyebrow, one honey accent on the single mug being
matched, soft green for the at-ease note; spark-red does not appear. The wordy card is the normal case,
never a scolding; a written standard about how things look is arguable not because anyone is careless but
because words rebuild a different picture at every reader. Warm line-and-flat-color style, slate photo
frame, the register of an argument that simply evaporates the moment you show the image instead of
describing it.

## 2. The Photo of the Ideal State (the hero infographic)

**Purpose:** Give the reader the flagship tool on one page: a photograph of a space on the day it was
exactly right, taped where the space is, beside the real space, so tidy stops being an opinion and becomes
a picture the room either matches or does not. This is the single strongest image of the chapter and the
subject of the infographic spec. Mirrors Figure 2 in the HTML.

**What it shows:** A cupboard door on the left carrying a small framed photograph of a shelf at its best,
in a slate frame, with a curl of tape at the corner and the words the photo of the ideal state beneath.
On the right, the real shelf, the two clearly matching, mug for mug. A light slate note between them reads
match this, with an arrow from the photo to the real shelf. Along the foot, a small camera icon and a line
read photograph it on the day it is right, and post it where the space is.

**Rough layout:**
- A calm panel background `#FBF7EF`. Top, a small sans caps label, letter-spaced, soft: `THE PICTURE OF
  THE IDEAL STATE`.
- LEFT, `the cupboard door`: a cupboard-door panel with a slate-framed photograph, cream face `#FCF8F0`,
  a curl of tape `#E8DFCB` with a `#C9BEA8` edge at the corner, a Newsreader line beneath `the photo of
  the ideal state`.
- CENTRE, a light slate note `match this` on a small card-slate `#EEF3F6` tag with a gentle slate arrow
  pointing from the photo to the real shelf.
- RIGHT, `the real shelf`: the real shelf drawn matching the photo mug for mug, one honey `#DDA63A` accent
  mug echoing the accent mug in the photo so the eye reads the match at a glance.
- Foot, a small slate camera icon and a two-line Newsreader line: `photograph it on the day it is right,`
  then `and post it where the space is`.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter): "The whole tool, in one image. A photograph of a shelf on the day it
  was exactly right, taped where the shelf is, and the real shelf beside it. Nobody has to remember or
  interpret what "tidy" means here. They look at the picture, look at the shelf, and match the two."

**Note for the illustrator:** the message is that the most powerful standard in a home is often a single
photograph, so keep the photo small, honest, and clearly a real snapshot of this very shelf, not a
polished catalogue image, and draw the real shelf beside it matching it plainly, mug for mug. Set the note
`match this`, the label `the photo of the ideal state`, and the foot line `photograph it on the day it is
right, and post it where the space is` exactly as they read; they are frozen wording and must not be
trimmed or reworded. Hold the color discipline: slate for the photo frame, the note, the arrow, and the
camera; one honey accent held to the single matched mug; soft green only if a small match-tick is wanted;
no spark-red anywhere. Draw no laminated binder, no wall of procedures; this is one honest photo taped
where the shelf is. Warm line-and-flat-color style, the register of tidy turned from an argument into a
picture on the cupboard-door.

## 3. The Storyboard (a task shown, not told)

**Purpose:** Show the second kind of visual standard: for a task with steps, a short strip of pictures in
order, so the next person copies what they see rather than converting a paragraph into actions. The value
is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful. Mirrors Figure 3 in the
HTML.

**What it shows:** A short horizontal strip of three simple slate-framed frames showing one task's key
steps in order, the entryway reset. Frame one shows two shoes on a rack, captioned shoes on the rack.
Frame two shows a bag hanging on a wall hook, captioned bags on the hooks. Frame three shows a clear flat
surface with a small green tick, captioned surface clear. Small honey arrows lead from frame to frame. A
thin line under the strip reads a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph.

**Rough layout:**
- A calm panel background `#FBF7EF`. Top, a small sans caps label, letter-spaced, slate: `THE ENTRYWAY
  RESET, IN THREE FRAMES`.
- Three even slate-framed frames with cream `#FCF8F0` faces, each with a small slate numeral and a two or
  three word Newsreader caption:
  - Frame `1`, `Shoes on the rack`: two simple shoes on a rack.
  - Frame `2`, `Bags on the hooks`: a bag hanging on a wall hook.
  - Frame `3`, `Surface clear`: a clear flat surface with a small green `#6E8B5B` tick.
- Small honey `#DDA63A` arrows leading frame to frame, left to right.
- A thin Newsreader italic foot line, slate: `a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph`.
- Caption (frozen, from the chapter): "A task shown, not told. Three frames of the entryway reset, in
  order, each a small picture with two or three words. The next person does not read a list and turn it
  into actions; they copy what they see, one frame at a time."

**Note for the illustrator:** the point is that a sequence is carried by a few frames a paragraph cannot
match, so keep the three frames simple, even, and plainly hand-made, phone-snap or stick-figure plain, and
read them clearly left to right in order. Set the three captions `Shoes on the rack`, `Bags on the hooks`,
`Surface clear`, and the foot line `a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph` exactly as they read; they
are frozen wording and must not be trimmed or reworded. Hold the color discipline: slate for the frames
and the numerals, one honey accent held to the arrows between frames, green only for the final
surface-clear tick; no spark-red anywhere. Draw no elaborate diagram, no glossy how-to; four rough
pictures of real steps are the whole tool, because the value is in the sequence being visible, not the
pictures being beautiful. Warm line-and-flat-color style, slate frames, honey arrows, the register of a
task the next person can follow without reading a word.

## 4. Show, Do Not Tell (the reframe, no direct HTML twin)

**Purpose:** Render the chapter's central turn as a picture: a written standard tells you what to do, a
visual standard shows you what right looks like, and for anything you would struggle to put into words
that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match. This
diagram has no direct HTML figure twin; it renders the show-do-not-tell reframe as a calm side-by-side,
so the reader feels that the problem was never the people or the standard, only the medium.

**What it shows:** Two quiet panels, read left to right. The LEFT panel, tell: a written standard, a card
dense with careful sentences about how a shelf should look, and above it two people rebuilding slightly
different pictures from the same words, a gentle disagreement, drawn a little effortful. The RIGHT panel,
show: the same shelf as a single photograph taped where the space is, and the same two people simply
looking and matching, the argument gone. A gentle arrow crosses from tell to show. Beneath, a small strip
carries the definition, and to one side a dim, unlit panel names Sustain as next, one step away.

**Rough layout:**
- Top: sans caps label, soft, `A WRITTEN STANDARD TELLS · A VISUAL STANDARD SHOWS`, with a Fraunces ink
  line beneath carrying the section's spine: "The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is
  the medium."
- Two cream `#FCF8F0` panels with rule `#E2D8C4` borders, joined by a gentle slate arrow:
  - `TELL` (left, a little dim): a card dense with sentences, two figures above it rebuilding slightly
    different pictures, a muted note `a standard people argue about`.
  - `SHOW` (right, warm and lit): a single slate-framed photo taped where the shelf is, the same two
    figures simply matching, a note `a standard they can see and match`.
- Beneath, a small slate-bordered strip carrying the frozen definition in ink: "A visual standard is the
  ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what
  "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a
  visual standard shows you what right looks like."
- To the right or below, a dimmer honey-outlined panel, unlit, tagged `SUSTAIN · KEEPING THE GAINS · NEXT`
  in muted honey sans caps, a muted italic note beneath, `one S remains, the work of keeping a held home
  held over the years`, held quiet and clearly one step ahead, not yet reached.
- Footer, Newsreader italic, slate: "For anything you can see, show the result instead of describing it,
  and right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can match."

**Note for the illustrator:** this is the chapter's central turn, so keep it calm and plain, never
scolding; the point is not that the written standard was wrong but that words are the wrong medium for how
a thing should look, and the moment you show the image the argument evaporates. Draw the tell panel a
little effortful and the show panel warm and lit and easy. Hold the color discipline: slate for the photo
frame, the arrow, and the sub-strip, one muted honey for the dim Sustain panel; no spark-red, no cage, no
one standing over a shoulder. Keep the Sustain panel dim, unlit, and clearly one step ahead; you may name
Sustain as next and as the sixth and final S, but teach nothing of it. The register is warmth and relief,
not correction: nobody was careless, the words were simply the wrong tool for the job. Warm
line-and-flat-color style.
