# Infographic Spec: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

**Chapter 25, "Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards"**
**6S Success: Home Edition · Part Seven · Standardize**

This is the single strongest image in the chapter. It carries the whole idea in one glance: some things
are easier to show than to say, so where words strain you photograph the space when it is exactly right,
tape the picture where the space is, and from then on tidy is not an opinion but a picture the room either
matches or does not. The concept pairs the hero rule, **Some things are easier to show than to say. When a
picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.**, with the picture at its
heart: **the photo of the ideal state**, a cupboard door carrying a small framed photograph of a shelf at
its best, the real shelf beside it matching mug for mug, a light note match this between them, and the
posting line along the foot, photograph it on the day it is right, and post it where the space is. So the
viewer sees the whole move at once, that a visual standard is something you look at and match rather than
read and decode.

It stays consistent with the four inline SVG figures in `chapter_25_final.html`. Slate carries the photo
frame, the note, the arrow, the camera, and the labels; one warm honey accent is held to the single mug
that appears in both the photo and the real shelf, so the eye reads the match at a glance; the single small
warm red is held back for marks only and is barely used, if at all, on this calm image, because a visual
standard is a gift that makes right easier to reach, not a live hazard. "Inspection" is not the point, and
neither is "policing"; a home given a photo of its ideal state is warmer and simpler, an argument turned
into a picture on the cupboard-door. Anti-shame throughout: a written standard about how things should look
is almost infinitely arguable not because anyone is careless but because words rebuild a slightly different
picture at every reader, so the problem is the medium, not the people. The register is warm and freeing,
never corporate, never a laminated binder; the visual standard is a gift to the household.

## Layout notes

- The rule is the loudest words on the page. Draw it as the whole chapter compressed: when a picture
  carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard. The eye should read the rule,
  then see the photo of the ideal state make it real, the cupboard-door photo the real shelf matches.
- The photo of the ideal state is the hero, so make it clear and honest: a small snapshot of this very
  shelf taped where the shelf is, the real shelf beside it matching mug for mug, a light note match this
  between them, never a laminated binder or a wall of procedures. Set the note, the label, and the foot
  line byte-for-byte as they read in the chapter; they are the frozen strings.
- Hold the color discipline: slate carries the photo frame, the note, the arrow, the camera, and the
  labels; one warm honey accent is held to the single mug that appears in both the photo and the real shelf
  so the match reads at a glance; green only for a small match-tick; the single small warm red is reserved
  for marks and is barely used, if at all, because this image is a gift and not a live hazard. This image
  is calm, so no fill-line red anywhere.
- This image makes the concept, the photo of the ideal state that completes Standardize. Do NOT re-teach
  the earlier four S's, and do NOT re-explain the concept of a standard (Chapter 23, already given) or the
  three tools that make a standard run (Chapter 24, already given), nor build the long-term Sustain work
  (the sixth S, named only as still to come, one step away). A visual standard is also distinct from a
  visual control from Straighten: a label or an outline makes a storage spot self-explaining, while a
  visual standard shows the ideal state or result, what done right looks like. And name no brand and draw
  no specific product; the shelf and the mugs are plain and generic, teaching the idea, not a shopping
  list.
- Anti-shame: a written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable not because
  anyone is careless but because words rebuild a slightly different picture at every reader, so the problem
  is the medium, not the people. What you are building is not inspection, it is a gift to the household.
  Keep the register warm and freeing, the visual standard makes right easier to reach, never a rule to
  police; no force or control language (no enforce, comply, inspect), no laminated binder or wall of
  procedures presented as virtue, no cage, no hype.
- Hand-made feel: a faint paper grain on the background, slightly soft corners, the photo an honest
  snapshot with a real curl of tape rather than a polished catalogue image, the cupboard-door and the
  shelf drawn by hand rather than as a clinical chart, the whole image the calm, warm register of an
  argument turned into a picture on the cupboard door rather than a procedures binder in a drawer.
