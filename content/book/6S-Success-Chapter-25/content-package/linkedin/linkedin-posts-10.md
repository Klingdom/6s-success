## 1. Some things resist description

You wrote the standard. You gave it a checklist, a schedule, and a name. And still, everyone argues about what "tidy" means.

Try to put into words exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, or how the cables should be coiled, and you pile up careful clauses that still miss it. Everyone reads the same words and pictures something slightly different.

Some things simply resist description. And for those, the fault is not the family and not the standard you wrote.

The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.

Words are the wrong tool for some standards. This chapter adds the fix: the one you show instead of tell.

## 2. Some things are easier to show than to say

When a standard about how things should look keeps getting argued over, the answer is not better words. It is to stop using words at all.

Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

The moment you stop describing the image in your head and simply show the image, there is nothing left to interpret and nothing left to disagree about. The argument evaporates.

A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps.

Two everyday forms, and neither needs anything you do not already own.

## 3. Show, do not tell

Here is the whole reframe of the chapter, and it is worth holding still for a second.

A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like.

For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

A written note about a tidy shelf is almost endlessly arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end. A photo of the shelf is not arguable at all. The room either matches it or it does not.

So for the things words strain to hold, stop describing and start showing.

## 4. The most powerful standard is often a single photo

Get a space exactly right on one of those rare good days, and before it drifts, take a picture of it. That picture is now the standard.

Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.

The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.

## 5. The optimistic mirror of the before photo

You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix.

That before photo was the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image: not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like."

And it sets the bar for being useful very low. A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

So the next day a space is exactly the way you want it, take the picture before it drifts. That is the whole move.

## 6. For a task with steps, storyboard it

Some standards are not a space but a task, a thing with steps that has to happen in order. A numbered paragraph never quite carries it.

For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.

Borrowed from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step. For setting up the folding table, coiling the cables, or sorting the recycling, three or four little pictures carry more, and faster, than a list.

And it does not have to be pretty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

## 7. Make it easy to match

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and that comes down to a little craft.

Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right. A photo taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match.

And subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. A standard nobody can easily copy is a standard people stop using, without ever quite deciding to.

So kneel where they kneel, stand where they stand, and shoot it the way they will see it. Match-able is the whole job.

## 8. Put it where the work happens

The urge, once you make a visual standard, is to make it beautiful: laminate it, bind it, file it neatly. Resist it.

A visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use.

A few slightly imperfect photos, taped honestly where they are needed, will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

So tape the pantry photo inside the pantry, the shelf photo on the cupboard door, the storyboard where the task happens. Keep it honest, not polished.

The point is not a lovely album. The point is a picture the next person actually sees, exactly where they need it.

## 9. It ends the argument, gently

A visual standard is not a rule imposed on the family. It is a kindness that ends an argument.

Think of how much of the friction in a home is really just words being the wrong tool: one person means one thing by "put it away," another means something else, and nobody is wrong, they are just picturing different rooms.

Show the room instead. A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like.

And it works best when it is shared. Let the children take the ideal-state photos and put them up at their own height. A child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to keep it that way, because the standard is theirs.
