# You Wrote the Standard, Gave It Its Tools, and Still Everyone Argued About What "Tidy" Meant, Because Some Things Are Easier to Show Than to Say.

Over the last two chapters you did the real work of the fifth S. You took the best way you knew to do a recurring task and wrote it down, and then you gave that written standard the three things that make it run: a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it happens, and a name so it is owned. For most of the recurring work in a home, that is genuinely enough.

But you have probably met the edge where it stops being enough. Try to write down, in sentences, exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, or how the cables should be coiled, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you find yourself piling up careful clauses that still miss it. Everyone reads the same words and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, and then, gently and endlessly, they argue about it.

> Some things simply resist description.

Here is the quiet insight that fixes it. When a standard about how things should look keeps getting argued over, the fault is not with the family and not with the standard you wrote. It is with the tool you chose to carry it.

> The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.

Words are the wrong medium for some standards. And the fix is not better words. It is to stop using words at all and show the thing instead.

> Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

## Show, do not tell

This is the whole reframe of the chapter, and it is worth holding still for a second.

> A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

The moment you stop describing the image in your head and simply show the image, there is nothing left to interpret and nothing left to disagree about.

> The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret.

> A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.

There are two everyday forms of it, and neither needs anything you do not already own.

## The photo of the ideal state

The first, and the more powerful, is a photograph. Get a space exactly right on one of those rare good days, and before it drifts, take a picture of it. That picture is now the standard.

> Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.

Put it where the space is, taped to the cupboard door or the inside of the pantry, and something quietly changes. "Tidy" stops being a matter of opinion that everyone weighs differently and becomes a fact anyone can check in a second.

> The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.

You have done a version of this before, at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image: not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." And it sets the bar for being useful very low indeed.

> A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo, which is a fair sign of how low this tool sets the bar for being useful.

## The storyboard

The second form is for a task rather than a space, a thing with steps that has to happen in order.

> For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.

Borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order. For setting up the folding table, coiling the cables, or sorting the recycling, three or four little pictures carry more, and faster, than a numbered list, because the reader copies what they see instead of converting words back into actions. And it does not have to be pretty.

> The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

Four phone snaps or four stick-figure sketches are plenty.

## Make it easy to match

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and that comes down to a little craft. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right. A photo taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned.

And then, the one rule that matters most: put it where the work happens. A beautiful laminated binder of visual standards that lives in a drawer helps no one.

> a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use.

A few slightly imperfect photos, taped honestly where they are needed, will do more for your home than any polished album that nobody ever opens.

## The needle barely moves, and the grip becomes complete

Go and look at the friction meter and you will find the needle has taken only a small, honest tick forward, to the Standardize-complete mark, the fifth on the dial after Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety. That small tick is honest, because Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way the earlier S's were. Its whole gift was quieter, and you see it not in the needle but in the catch behind it.

That catch, the small tooth that first appeared when you began to standardize and grew stronger when standards got their tools, is fully engaged now. Everything is written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures. For the first time your home is held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be.

> The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete.

## Standardize is complete

You did it in three moves. In Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote one down. In Chapter 24 you gave standards the three tools that make them run. In this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words could. Between them, that goodness is no longer fragile.

> It made that goodness durable, took it out of your head and your hands and your good days, and put it somewhere it can survive a tired week and a house full of other people.

> With this, the fifth S is complete. The best way you know is written down, carried by a checklist and a schedule and a name, and, where words fall short, shown in a picture. Your home no longer runs on your memory. It runs on what you have made visible.

Five of the six S's are done now, and the goal is a step away on the calm side, because one S remains. What you have built is a home that is good and, now, held. What you have not yet done is keep it, not for a proud month but for years. That is the sixth S, Sustain, and it is what comes next.

> When words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.
