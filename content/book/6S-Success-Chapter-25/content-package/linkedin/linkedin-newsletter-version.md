# You Wrote the Standard, Gave It Its Tools, and Still Everyone Argued About What "Tidy" Meant, Because Some Things Are Easier to Show Than to Say.

*A 6S Success: Home Edition newsletter*

Over the last two chapters you did the real work of the fifth S. You took the best way you knew to do a recurring task and wrote it down, and then you gave that written standard the three things that make it run: a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it happens, and a name so it is owned. For most of the recurring work in a home, that is genuinely enough.

But you have probably met the edge where it stops being enough. Try to write down, in sentences, exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, or how the cables should be coiled, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you find yourself piling up careful clauses that still miss it. Everyone reads the same words and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, and then, gently and endlessly, they argue about it.

**Some things simply resist description.**

Here is the quiet insight that fixes it: when a standard about how things should look keeps getting argued over, the fault is not with the family and not with the standard you wrote. It is with the tool you chose to carry it.

**The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.**

Words are the wrong medium for some standards, and the fix is not better words. It is to stop using words at all and show the thing instead. Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

## Show, do not tell

This is the whole reframe of the chapter, and it is worth holding still for a second.

**A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.**

The moment you stop describing the image in your head and simply show the image, there is nothing left to interpret and nothing left to disagree about. A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. There are two everyday forms of it, and neither needs anything you do not already own.

## The photo of the ideal state

The first, and the more powerful, is a photograph. Get a space exactly right on one of those rare good days, and before it drifts, take a picture of it. That picture is now the standard.

**Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.**

Put it where the space is, taped to the cupboard door or the inside of the pantry, and something quietly changes. Tidy stops being a matter of opinion that everyone weighs differently and becomes a fact anyone can check in a second. The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was the honest record of the mess; the ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

## The storyboard

The second form is for a task rather than a space, a thing with steps that has to happen in order.

**For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.**

Borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order. For setting up the folding table, coiling the cables, or sorting the recycling, three or four little pictures carry more, and faster, than a numbered list, because the reader copies what they see instead of converting words back into actions. Four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty; the value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

## Make it easy to match

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and that comes down to a little craft. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. And put it where the work happens, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, its entire value being seen at the point of use. A few slightly imperfect photos, taped honestly where they are needed, will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

## The needle barely moves, and the grip becomes complete

Go and look at the friction meter and you will find the needle has taken only a small, honest tick forward, to the Standardize-complete mark, the fifth on the dial after Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety. That small tick is honest, because Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way the earlier S's were; its whole gift was quieter, and you see it not in the needle but in the catch behind it. That catch, the small tooth that first appeared when you began to standardize and grew stronger when standards got their tools, is fully engaged now. Everything is written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures, which means for the first time your home is held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be.

**The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete.**

Five of the six S's are done now, and the goal is a step away on the calm side, because one S remains.

## The assignment

Right now, before you do anything else, give one space a picture-standard, which costs about a minute. Wait for a space to be exactly the way you want it, or take one minute to make it so, then photograph it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. From that moment, "put it back the way it should be" stops being an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret and becomes a picture to match. If you have children, let them take the photos and put them up at their own height, because a child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to keep it that way; the standard is theirs. And if there is a task with steps that you keep re-explaining, take four quick snaps of the key frames in order and stick the strip where the task happens.

**Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.**
