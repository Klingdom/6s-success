## Beat 3 · The problem is the medium, not the people

Be precise about why a written standard about how things should look fails, because it fails in a way that points straight at the fix: the problem is not the people and not the standard, it is the medium.

- Composition: two small calm panels on panel #FBF7EF. On the LEFT, headed "Told in words," one wordy card and three faint figures each imagining a slightly different shelf, a soft spark red #CB4B36 "argument" flicker between them. On the RIGHT, headed "Shown in a picture," one slate #3C5A6B framed photo and the same three figures all looking at the one image, calm and agreeing, a green #6E8B5B "matched" mark.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the panel frames and the photo frame, a sparing spark red #CB4B36 on the argument flicker, green #6E8B5B on the matched mark. Calm and legible; no verdict scolding, because the fault is the medium, not the reader.
- Caption, from the book: "The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret."
- Point: a written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments: it is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable, because we are trying to transmit an image through the narrow pipe of language. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates, because you are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it.
- The frozen show-do-not-tell reframe sits as a Fraunces pull-quote on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match." The frozen definition box sits as a Fraunces box on panel #FBF7EF with slate #3C5A6B rules: "A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. ## Beat 4 · The picture of the ideal state

- Composition: the ideal-state-photo infographic, a cupboard door with a small slate #3C5A6B framed photograph of a shelf at its best taped to it, and beside it the real shelf, the two clearly matching, with a light slate #3C5A6B note reading "match this". - Palette: warm paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the photo frame and the "match this" note, one honey #DDA63A accent, green #6E8B5B on the matched real shelf. Calm; the whole tool in one clean figure, nothing corporate.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "photograph it on the day it is right, and post it where the space is."
- Point: you get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, taped inside the cupboard door or stuck to the wall beside the shelf, and from that moment the standard for that space is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door. A label, an outline, or a boundary from Straighten made a storage spot self-explaining; this is a different device, because it shows the ideal state or result of a space, what "done right" looks like, so a person can match reality against a picture. A label is a tiny visual standard; the ideal-state photo is the full one.
- Frozen figure caption, verbatim: "The whole tool, in one image. A photograph of a shelf on the day it was exactly right, taped where the shelf is, and the real shelf beside it. Nobody has to remember or interpret what "tidy" means here. They look at the picture, look at the shelf, and match the two." ## Beat 6 · The storyboard

The second form made visual: for a task that is a sequence rather than a look, one photo is not enough, so use a few pictures instead of one, a short strip of simple frames in order, so the next person copies what they see.

- Composition: the storyboard infographic, a short horizontal strip of three simple slate #3C5A6B picture-frames showing one ordinary task's key steps in order, the entryway reset (frame 1: shoes on the rack; frame 2: bags on the hooks; frame 3: surface clear), each frame a small hand-drawn or photographed image with a two or three word caption beneath, a slate #3C5A6B arrow leading from frame to frame. A thin slate #3C5A6B line under the strip reads "a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph".
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the frames and the arrows, one honey #DDA63A accent, plain and legible. Warm; four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty, and the value is in the sequence being visible, not the pictures being beautiful.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "a few frames, in order, beat a paragraph."
- Point: a photo of the ideal state is perfect for a space that should look a certain way, but some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest; they are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those one photo is not enough. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one. A storyboard, borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order: how the folding table gets set up and stowed, how the cables coil and where they clip, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night. Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is just copying what they see, frame by frame. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty.
- Frozen figure caption, verbatim: "A task shown, not told. Three frames of the entryway reset, in order, each a small picture with two or three words. The next person does not read a list and turn it into actions; they copy what they see, one frame at a time." The storyboard line may run in Fraunces: "For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word."

## Beat 7 · Make it match-able

The craft of the chapter made visual: a visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, so shoot from the angle and in the light of use, post it at the point of use, and keep it honest rather than laminated.

- Composition: a warm two-part scene on panel #FBF7EF. On the LEFT, "match-able," a slate #3C5A6B framed photo shot at standing eye line in ordinary room light, taped right beside the shelf it describes, a green #6E8B5B "easy to match" tick. On the RIGHT, faded soft #6A625A, "hard to match," the same photo shot from an odd high angle and filed in a drawer, quietly unused.
- Palette: paper ground, slate #3C5A6B on the framed photo and the point-of-use tape, green #6E8B5B on the "easy to match" tick, soft #6A625A on the faded drawer version. Warm; honest not impressive.
- Slate #3C5A6B label: "the angle and light of use, posted at the point of use, honest not laminated."
- Point: a visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that, because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees it from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Get down or up to the real eye line, use the ordinary light of the room. Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible; a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. And keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.
- The Quick Win sits in a green #6E8B5B bordered callout, verbatim: "Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match." The 6S Tip sits in a slate #3C5A6B bordered callout, verbatim: "Shoot the standard photo from the exact angle and in the same light someone will see the space from when they go to set it right. A picture shot from an odd angle or in different light is hard to match, and a standard that is hard to match is one people quietly stop using."
