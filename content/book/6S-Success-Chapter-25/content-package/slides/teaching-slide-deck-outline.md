### Slide 4 · The problem is the medium, not the people

- Be precise about how a written standard about how things should look fails, because it fails in a way that points straight at the fix. Everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments: it is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable, because we are trying to transmit an image through the narrow pipe of language.
- The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. Verbatim: "The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret." You are not comparing your idea of tidy to mine; you are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it.
- The show-do-not-tell reframe, frozen: "A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match." The definition box, frozen: "A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like."

### Slide 5 · The picture of the ideal state

- The flagship tool of the chapter, and it is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works. You get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, taped inside the cupboard door or stuck to the wall beside the shelf, and from that moment the standard for that space is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door.
- A label, an outline, or a boundary from Straighten made a storage spot self-explaining. This is a different device: it shows the ideal state or result of a space, what "done right" looks like, so a person can match reality against a picture. A label is a tiny visual standard; the ideal-state photo is the full one.
- The pictures line, frozen: "Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance."

### Slide 6 · Tidy is not an opinion, it is a picture

- The ideal-state photo has a quiet power a written standard never had: it ends the argument before it starts. Once the picture is on the door, tidy is not your idea against mine. It is one image, and the room either matches it or it does not.
- You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix: that was the visual baseline, the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." It sets the bar for being useful astonishingly low. Verbatim: "A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo, which is a fair sign of how low this tool sets the bar for being useful."
- The photo-of-the-ideal-state reframe, frozen: "The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. ### Slide 7 · The storyboard

- A photo of the ideal state is perfect for a space that should look a certain way, but some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest; they are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those one photo is not enough. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one.
- A storyboard, borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order: how the folding table gets set up and stowed, how the cables coil and where they clip, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night. Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is not converting words into actions in their head; they are just copying what they see, frame by frame. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty. Verbatim: "The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful."
- The storyboard line, frozen: "For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word."

### Slide 8 · Make it match-able

- A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that, because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees it from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Get down or up to the real eye line, use the ordinary light of the room, and capture the space the way it is actually seen.
- Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible; a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. And keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.
- Quick Win, verbatim: "Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match." 6S Tip, verbatim: "Shoot the standard photo from the exact angle and in the same light someone will see the space from when they go to set it right. A picture shot from an odd angle or in different light is hard to match, and a standard that is hard to match is one people quietly stop using."

### Slide 9 · The rule

- That is the whole chapter, and it fits in a single line. A written standard tells you what to do; where words fall short, a picture shows you what right looks like, and then the room either matches it or it does not. The written standard and the visual standard are not rivals: the written one carries what you can say, and the visual one carries what you can only show. For anything you can see, the picture is the better standard.
- Held rightly, a visual standard is a kindness, not a control. It shares clarity and ends arguments; it is not factory signage or a management dashboard imposed on the household. The best way to raise one is to let the people who use a space make its picture, because a standard that is theirs is one they keep.
- The rule, frozen, the hero of the chapter: "Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard."

### Slide 12 · Five of six done, one S remains
