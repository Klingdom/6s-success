### Slide 1 (hook)

**You wrote down exactly what "tidy" means. Then you watched four people read it and picture four different shelves.**

You have come a long way into the fifth S. You have the idea of a standard, the best way you know written down, and the three tools that make it run: a checklist, a schedule, and a name. For most of the recurring work of a home that is enough, and it is a great deal.

But you have almost certainly run into the edge of it: the place where words start to strain. Try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like and you pile up clauses that still do not quite catch it. Some things simply resist description. For those, a paragraph is the wrong tool, and there is a far better one sitting in your pocket. This chapter is that better tool, and adding it completes Standardize. Swipe.

### Slide 2

**Some things simply resist description.**

The line the whole chapter turns on:

"Some things simply resist description."

How the cables should be coiled. What the entryway looks like when it is genuinely reset. Where each thing sits when the room is right. You can write clause after careful clause and still miss it, because you are trying to push a picture through the narrow pipe of language, and each person rebuilds it a little differently at the far end. That is not a failure of effort. It is a failure of the medium.

### Slide 3

**The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.**

A written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable. Everyone reads the same careful sentences and pictures something slightly different, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments: it is a word, and words about how things should look are always open to interpretation.

"The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret." You are no longer comparing your idea of tidy to mine. You are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it.

### Slide 4

**A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like.**

The frozen reframe, and the definition it earns:

"A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match."

That is the whole move: stop describing the ideal state and show it instead. A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words. It comes in two forms, and the rest of the chapter is those two.

### Slide 5

**Photograph a space on the day it is exactly right. That photo is now the standard.**

The frozen pictures line, the flagship tool:

"Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance."

It is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works. Get the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk exactly the way it should be on one of those rare good days, and before it drifts, take the photo. Then tape it inside the cupboard door or beside the shelf. From that moment the standard is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door.

### Slide 6

**Tidy stops being an opinion. It becomes a picture on the cupboard door.**

The frozen photo-of-the-ideal-state reframe:

"The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not."

You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image: not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

### Slide 7

**For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph.**

The frozen storyboard line, the second form:

"For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word."

A photo is perfect for a space that should look a certain way. But some standards are a sequence, the steps of a task in the right order, and for those the answer is not a paragraph, it is a few pictures. A storyboard, borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, in order. Four phone snaps of your own hands, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty. "The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful."

### Slide 8

**A picture only works if it is genuinely easy to match. There is a little craft to that.**

Getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they set the space right: a photo taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Get to the real eye line, use the ordinary light of the room.

Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible. "a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use." And keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed beat a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

Quick Win: "Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match."

6S Tip: "Shoot the standard photo from the exact angle and in the same light someone will see the space from when they go to set it right. A picture shot from an odd angle or in different light is hard to match, and a standard that is hard to match is one people quietly stop using."

### Slide 9

**When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.**

The rule, frozen, the whole chapter in a single line:

"Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard."

A written standard tells you what to do. Where words fall short, a picture shows you what right looks like, and then the room either matches it or it does not. That is not a lesser standard. For anything you can see, it is the better one.

### Slide 10

**Let the children take the photos. A shelf whose picture they took is a shelf they put back.**

There is a right way to raise a visual standard, and it is to let the household make it. A picture is a kindness that ends arguments, not a control imposed on the family. When the people who use a space take its ideal-state photo and hang it at their own height, the standard becomes theirs, and what is theirs gets kept.

Family Challenge: "Let the children take the ideal-state photos and put them up at their own height. A child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to put that shelf back the way the picture shows, because the standard is theirs."
