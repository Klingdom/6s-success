# Newsletter Edition: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

**Subject line (placeholder):** Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

**Preview text (placeholder):** Show it, don't say it: when a picture carries the standard, it is the standard.

---

Hi friend,

Over the last two issues you built Standardize almost all the way. In Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote one down, the best way you knew put on a card. In Chapter 24 you gave that standard its three tools, a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it happens on a rhythm, and a name so someone owns it. For most recurring work, that is enough. But you have almost certainly hit the edge where words run out: you sit down to write exactly what a "tidy" shelf looks like, or how the cables should be coiled, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you pile up careful clauses that still miss it. That is not a failing on your part, and it is not a bad standard.

That is the edge this last chapter is for, and here is the honest picture it starts from.

**Some things simply resist description.** A written standard about how a thing should look is almost infinitely arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. And once you see it as a medium problem, the fix is almost silly in how simple it is: the moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret. That is the whole move, and here is the definition to keep.

**A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.**

Start with the flagship, because it is the simplest and does the most work. Get a space exactly right on one of those rare days when it is, and take a picture of it before it drifts.

**Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.**

You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was the honest record of the mess; this is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." And the bar it sets for being useful is astonishingly low: a child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo. Put the picture where the space is, and tidy stops being something anyone has to remember, interpret, or argue about.

**The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.**

A photo carries a finished state. For work that has an order to it, there is a second form that carries a sequence instead. Borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order.

**For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.**

For setting up the folding table, coiling the cables, or sorting the recycling, three or four little pictures carry more, and faster, than a numbered list, because the reader copies what they see instead of converting words into actions. Four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful. Which points at the one thing that actually matters with any of this: a visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will really have when they set the space right, because a photo taken from a stepladder when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Then put it where the work happens, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. A few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

**To try this week:** Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match. If you have a task with an order to it, sketch or snap the three or four frames that matter and post them at the point of use. Let the children take some of the photos and put them up at their own height, because a child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to put that shelf back the way the picture shows, since the standard is theirs. Keep it honest, not laminated: the value is being seen where the work happens, not being beautiful.

That is the thing to notice. You built Standardize in three moves: in Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote one down; in Chapter 24 you gave standards the three tools that make them run; and in this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words. That is the whole of it, held in one line.

**Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.**

When words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.
