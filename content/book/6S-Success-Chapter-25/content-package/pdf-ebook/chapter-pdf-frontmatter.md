# Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

**Part Seven, Standardize**
Chapter 25 of *6S Success: Home Edition*

Subtitle · Some things are far easier to show than to say. A written standard tells you what to do; a visual standard shows you what right looks like. This chapter adds the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list: a photo of the ideal state, and a short storyboard of the steps. With it, the fifth S is complete.

---

## Chapter descriptor

The third and final chapter of Part Seven, and Standardize completes here, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept, the best way you know written on a card, and Chapter 24 gave every standard its three tools, a checklist for the what, a schedule for the when, and a name for the who; this chapter names the edge those reach and cannot cross. Try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, how the cables should be coiled, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you pile up clauses that still miss it, because some things simply resist description. A written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable: everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, which is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. This chapter delivers the fix, the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell, and it comes in two forms. The flagship is the photo of the ideal state: get a space exactly right on one of those rare days, photograph it before it drifts, and put the picture where the space is, and from then on tidy is not an opinion but a picture the room either matches or does not, the optimistic mirror image of the before photo you took at the very start. The second is the storyboard, for a task that is a sequence rather than a look: a short strip of a few simple frames, each one key step, in order, so the next person copies what they see instead of converting a paragraph into actions, and four phone snaps or stick figures are plenty. The craft is to make it match-able, shot from the angle and light of use, posted at the point of use, kept honest rather than laminated, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms. Then the chapter reads the friction meter as a Standardize-complete milestone, the fifth mark on the dial after Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety, where the needle ticks only a small honest step forward and the anti-backslide catch behind it is fully engaged and locked, its firmest state, and it carries a short Part 7 retrospective, standards written in Chapter 23, carried by tools in Chapter 24, shown in pictures here, before the hand-off to Chapter 26 and the sixth and final S. Five of the six S's are done, and the goal is a step away.

## How to read this chapter

This is the third and final chapter of Part Seven, Standardize, and it completes the fifth S. It picks up exactly where Chapters 23 and 24 left off: you have the concept of a standard, and the three text-based tools, a checklist, a schedule, and a name, that make a written standard actually run. For most of the recurring work of a home that is enough. This chapter is for the edge where words strain, the place you have almost certainly reached by now, where you try to describe in sentences exactly what a space should look like and the sentences never quite land. Read it through once for the idea, that some things are far easier to show than to say, then do the one small, concrete thing it asks: photograph a single space on a day it is exactly right, and put the picture where the space is.

The chapter rests on a plain observation. There is a kind of standard that fails not because it is wrong but because it is written, and the moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates, because there is nothing left to interpret. So a visual standard shows what right looks like rather than telling you what to do, and it takes two forms, one for a space and one for a task. For a space, the photo of the ideal state: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right and taped where the space is, so a child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo. For a task with steps, the storyboard: a short strip of a few frames in order, carrying a sequence a paragraph cannot, because the reader copies what they see. The register throughout is warm, human, and homely, quietly proud and never corporate: a visual standard is a kindness that ends arguments, not factory signage or a control imposed on the household, and a home whose standards live only in words or in one person's head is the normal case, not a failing.

A word on the scope fence, so the chapter stays the thing that completes Standardize rather than drifting into the earlier chapters or the S that follows. It is deliberately distinct from Chapter 14's visual controls: a label, an outline, or a boundary makes a storage spot self-explaining and passes the Glance Test, whereas a visual standard shows the ideal state or result of a whole space or task, what "done right" looks like, so a person can match reality against a picture. A label is a tiny visual standard; the ideal-state photo is the full one, and this chapter does not re-teach labels, outlines, boundaries, or the Glance Test. It uses Chapter 23's concept and Chapter 24's three tools without re-teaching either, and it makes a one-line callback to the before photo you took back in Prepare, of which the ideal-state photo is the positive cousin, without re-teaching that matched-pair technique. It does not build the long-term adherence, audit, and habit-as-a-system work that keeps everyone doing all of it over the years, which is Sustain, Chapters 26 to 30. And it does not re-open Sort, Straighten, Shine, or Safety; a one-line callback is fine, but the earlier S's are not re-taught here. Because this chapter secures what was already built rather than clearing big new friction from the rooms, the friction meter ticks only a small, honest step forward to its fifth marked milestone, Standardize complete, and it would be dishonest to pretend the leap was large. The real change is behind the needle: the small catch that appeared when you began to standardize, and grew stronger when standards got their tools, is fully engaged now, locked, because everything you have built is written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures, held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be. The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete. The goal is a step away on the calm side, because one S still remains, Sustain.

If you only do one thing: photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match. That is the smallest possible proof of the whole chapter, and it completes the fifth S. Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.
