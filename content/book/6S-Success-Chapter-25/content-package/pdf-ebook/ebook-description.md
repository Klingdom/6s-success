# 6S Success: Home Edition (Ebook Description)

You have come a long way into the fifth S. You have the idea of a standard, the best way you know written down, and the three tools that make a standard actually run: a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it has a time, and a name so someone owns it. For most of the recurring work of a home that is enough, and it is a great deal. But you have almost certainly hit the edge of it, the place where words start to strain: try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like and you pile up clauses that still do not quite capture it. Chapter 25 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is where you cross that edge, and the fix is not more careful writing; it is a far better tool sitting in your pocket. As the chapter puts it, "Some things simply resist description," and for those the problem is not the people and not the standard, "It is the medium."

"Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards" is the third and final chapter of Part Seven, and it completes Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept and Chapter 24 gave standards their three tools; this chapter adds the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list. It uses the earlier chapters without re-teaching them, and it does one thing: it gives you the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell, in its two forms, the photo of a space's ideal state and the storyboard of a task's steps.

The chapter starts at the edge where written standards fail. There is a particular kind of standard that fails not because it is wrong but because it is written: everyone reads the same careful sentences and pictures something slightly different, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments; it is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable, because we are trying to transmit an image through the narrow pipe of language and each person rebuilds it a little differently at the other end. "The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret." You are not comparing your idea of tidy to mine; you are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it.

That is the reframe, and it fits in a box: "A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like." A written standard tells; a visual standard shows. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

Then the chapter delivers the flagship, and it is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works. You get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, and from that moment the standard for that space is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door. "Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance." You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix; that was the honest record of the mess, and the ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." As the reframe has it, "The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo, which is a fair sign of how low this tool sets the bar for being useful.

A photo is perfect for a space that should look a certain way, but some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest; they are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one. "For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word." A storyboard, borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order: how the folding table gets set up and stowed, how the cables coil and where they clip, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night. Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is just copying what they see, frame by frame. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist; four phone snaps of your own hands, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty, because "The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful."

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that, because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees it from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible, because "a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use." And keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

Then go and look at the friction meter, because it has reached a mark, the fifth on the dial. The needle sits now at the Standardize-complete tick, beside Sort complete, Straighten complete, Shine complete, and Safety complete. Five of the six S's are done. The tick forward was small, and that is honest: Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way Sort, Straighten, and Shine were. Its whole gift was quieter, and you see it behind the needle, in the small catch that first appeared when you began to standardize and grew stronger when you gave standards their tools. It is fully engaged now, locked. Everything you have built across twenty-five chapters is written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures, which means for the first time it is held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be. As the rule of the chapter has it, "Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard." And on the meter: "The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete."

That is Standardize, done. For the first six parts you made your home good: cleared, arranged, clean, and safe. Standardize did something different. "It made that goodness durable, took it out of your head and your hands and your good days, and put it somewhere it can survive a tired week and a house full of other people." You did it in three moves: in Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote one down; in Chapter 24 you gave standards the three tools that make them run; and in this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words. "With this, the fifth S is complete. The best way you know is written down, carried by a checklist and a schedule and a name, and, where words fall short, shown in a picture. Your home no longer runs on your memory. It runs on what you have made visible." One Idea to Keep: "When words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match."
