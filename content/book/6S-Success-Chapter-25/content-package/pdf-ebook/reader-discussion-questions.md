# Reader and Book-Club Discussion Questions: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

*Chapter 25, 6S Success: Home Edition. Use these for personal reflection or a group discussion. They are open-ended, with no single right answer.*

1. The chapter opens at the edge where words strain: "Some things simply resist description." Where in your home have you tried to write down or explain exactly what a space should look like, only to find the words never quite land?

2. The chapter locates the real cause of the trouble: "The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium." Think of a household argument about how something should look or be left. How much of it, honestly, was about the medium, that you were all rebuilding the same words into slightly different pictures?

3. The chapter says the trouble ends the moment you show instead of tell: "The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret." What is one thing you argue about at home that a single shared photograph might simply settle?

4. The reframe at the heart of the chapter is short: "A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like." For which of your standards would a picture do more than any careful sentence, and why?

5. The definition box says a visual standard is "the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words." Which matters more to you right now, a picture of a space at its best or a storyboard of a task's steps, and what would you point it at first?

6. The flagship move is almost comically simple: "Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard... that anyone can match their reality against at a glance." Which space in your home is worth catching on one of its rare exactly-right days, before it drifts?

7. The chapter promises that a photo settles the argument for good: "From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not." How would it change things in your home if "tidy" stopped being an opinion and became a picture on a door?

8. The ideal-state photo is framed as the positive cousin of an earlier one: it is the optimistic mirror image of the before photo you took at the very start, "not 'this is what it looked like at its worst' but 'this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like.'" Does thinking of it as the hopeful twin of the before photo change how you feel about taking it?

9. The chapter notes how low this tool sets the bar: "A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo." Who in your household could keep a space right with a picture that they cannot yet keep right with words?

10. The storyboard reframe is for sequences, not looks: "For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word." What task in your home is really a sequence, and which few frames would carry it?

11. The chapter is relaxed about craft: "The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful." Does knowing that four phone snaps or stick figures are plenty make you more likely to actually make one? What has stopped you before?

12. The chapter gives the craft of making a standard match-able: shoot from the angle and in the light someone will actually have, because a picture that is subtly hard to match is one people quietly stop using. Where might a photo you would take be subtly hard to match, and how would you shoot it instead?

13. The Common Mistake is a warning against polish: "a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use." Where might you be tempted to make a beautiful binder instead of taping a few honest photos where the work happens?

14. The chapter distinguishes a visual standard from Chapter 14's visual controls: a label or an outline makes a storage spot self-explaining, while a visual standard shows the ideal state or result of a whole space or task. Where in your home would a label do the job, and where do you actually need a picture of what "done right" looks like?

15. The Family Challenge points at ownership: "A child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to put that shelf back the way the picture shows, because the standard is theirs." Which ideal-state photos could you let the children take and hang at their own height, so the standard becomes theirs?

16. The chapter reads the friction meter at a milestone: "The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete." With five of the six S's now done and the fifth S complete, what does it mean to you that your home "no longer runs on your memory" but "on what you have made visible"? As the book turns to Chapter 26 and Sustain, what would it take to keep it that way not for a proud month but over the years?
