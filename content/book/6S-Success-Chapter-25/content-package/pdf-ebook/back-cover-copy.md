# Back Cover Copy: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

## Back-cover text

By now you have come a long way into the fifth S. You have the idea of a standard, the best way you know written down, and the three tools that make a standard actually run: a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it has a time, and a name so someone owns it. For most of the recurring work of a home that is enough, and it is a great deal. But you have almost certainly hit the edge of it: try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like and you pile up clauses that still do not quite capture it. Some things simply resist description. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. And there is a far better tool sitting in your pocket. "Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard."

Chapter 25 of *6S Success: Home Edition* is the third and final chapter of Part Seven, and it completes Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept and Chapter 24 gave standards their three tools; this chapter adds the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list: the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell. "A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like." It comes in two forms. The first is the photo of the ideal state: "Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance." From then on tidy is not an opinion and not an argument; it is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not, the optimistic mirror image of the before photo you took at the very start. The second is the storyboard, for a task that is a sequence rather than a look: "For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word." Four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty, because the value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

The craft is to make it genuinely easy to match: shoot from the angle and in the light someone will actually have when they set the space right, put it where the work happens, and keep it honest rather than laminated, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms. Then go and look at the friction meter, which reaches a mark, the fifth on the dial: the Standardize-complete milestone, beside Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety complete. The tick forward is small, and that is honest, because Standardize was never about clearing big new friction; its gift is in the catch, not the needle. The small catch that appeared when you began to standardize is fully engaged now, locked. "The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete." Five of the six S's are done. With this, the fifth S is complete: your home no longer runs on your memory, it runs on what you have made visible. Give any space a picture-standard with **The Ideal State Photo card**, and show a task's steps at a glance with **The Storyboard template**. Next, Chapter 25 hands off to Chapter 26 · Sustain: Keeping the Gains, which opens Part Eight and the sixth and final S, the work of keeping a held home held over the years.
