# 6S Success: Home Edition (Sales Page Copy)

## You wrote it all down. Then you tried to describe what "tidy" looks like.

You have come a long way into the fifth S. You have the idea of a standard, the best way you know written down, and the three tools that make a standard actually run: a checklist, a schedule, and a name. For most of the recurring work of a home that is enough, and it is a great deal. And then you hit the edge of it, the place where words start to strain: you try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, and you pile up clauses that still do not quite capture it.

Some things simply resist description. How the cables should be coiled, what the entryway looks like when it is genuinely reset, where each thing sits when the room is right, for all of these a paragraph is the wrong tool. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. And there is a far better tool sitting in your pocket.

> Some things simply resist description.

## Welcome to the end of Standardize, the fifth S.

Chapter 25 is the third and final chapter of Part Seven, and it completes Standardize, the fifth of the six S's. Chapter 23 gave you the concept and Chapter 24 gave standards their three tools; this chapter adds the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list. It uses the earlier chapters without re-teaching them, and it does one thing.

> This chapter gives you the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell: a photo of a space's ideal state, and a storyboard of a task's steps.

## Why a written standard about how things look never quite works.

There is a particular kind of standard that fails not because it is wrong but because it is written. Everyone reads the same careful sentences and pictures something slightly different, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. This is why "tidy" causes so many household arguments: it is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable, because we are trying to transmit an image through the narrow pipe of language and each person rebuilds it a little differently at the other end.

The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret. You are not comparing your idea of tidy to mine; you are both looking at the same photograph and comparing the shelf to it.

> The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.

## Show, do not tell.

The whole chapter turns on one reframe, and it fits in a box.

> A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.

A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

## The photo of the ideal state.

The flagship tool of the chapter is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works. You get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, taped inside the cupboard door or stuck to the wall beside the shelf, and from that moment the standard is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door.

> Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.

You did a version of this at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix; that was the honest record of the mess. The ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

> The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not.

## The storyboard: a sequence a paragraph cannot carry.

A photo is perfect for a space that should look a certain way, but some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest; they are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one. Borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order: how the folding table gets set up and stowed, how the cables coil and where they clip, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night.

> For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word.

Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is not converting words into actions in their head; they are just copying what they see, frame by frame. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty. "The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful."

## Make it match-able.

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that, because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees it from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Then put the standard where the work happens.

> A visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use.

Keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.

## Give one space a picture-standard, in about a minute.

None of this is heavy, and the fastest way to feel that is to give a single space a picture-standard right now.

> Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it, print it small, and tape it up where the space is. Now "put it back the way it should be" is not an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match.

That is the difference between a standard that lives in words, or in one person's head, and one anyone can see and match. And you can hand it to the household: let the children take the ideal-state photos and put them up at their own height, because a child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to put that shelf back the way the picture shows, since the standard is theirs.

## Not corporate. A kindness that ends arguments.

One word about how this is meant to feel, because the subject usually sounds like factory signage and it does not have to.

A visual standard is a kindness that ends arguments, not a control imposed on the family. A home whose standards live only in words, or only in one person's head, is the normal case, not a failing, and a picture simply shares the clarity around so nobody has to hold it alone. There is no force in it and nothing to enforce; the room either matches the picture or it does not, and anyone, even a child too young to read, can see which.

## Keep it honest, not impressive.

One caution, so the visual standard stays a relief and not one more project.

> Building an elaborate laminated binder of visual standards that looks impressive and helps no one, instead of a few honest photos stuck where the work happens. The whole value of a visual standard is being seen at the point of use; a beautiful one filed away is worth nothing.

The whole point is to be seen at the point of use. A few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed beat any binder in a drawer, so keep the pictures honest, keep them where the work happens, and do not turn a one-minute kindness into a laminating project.
