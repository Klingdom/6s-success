# Podcast Script: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

## INTRO

Hey, welcome back. This is the one that finishes the fifth S. Over the last two chapters you built standards two different ways. First you learned what a standard even is, the best way you know to do some recurring job, written down on a card anyone can follow. Then you gave that standard its three tools, a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so it has a time, and a name so someone owns it. And for most of the recurring work of a home, honestly, that is plenty. That is a great deal.

But I would bet you have already run into the edge of it. There is a particular place where words just start to strain. Try to write down, in sentences, exactly what a tidy shelf looks like. You end up piling clause on clause, mugs on the left, handles facing out, tallest at the back, bowls stacked no more than four high, and it still does not quite capture the thing you can see perfectly clearly in your head. Some things simply resist description. How the cables should be coiled. What the entryway looks like when it is genuinely reset. Where each thing sits when the room is right. For all of these, a paragraph is the wrong tool. And there is a far better one, and it is sitting in your pocket.

So this chapter is about the last kind of standard, the one you show instead of tell. The visual standard. And adding it completes Standardize. Here is the whole idea up front, and it is the rule I want you to hold onto: some things are easier to show than to say, and when a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART ONE: WHEN WORDS ARE NOT ENOUGH

Let me be precise about why a written standard fails at this particular edge, because it is not the failure you would expect. It fails not because it is wrong, but because it is written.

Here is the mechanism. Everyone reads the same careful sentences, and everyone rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end. And slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf that was meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. Nobody did anything wrong. Four people read the same words and honestly pictured four different shelves.

So I want to be really clear about where the fault actually lies, because it is the hinge of the whole chapter. The problem is not the people, and the problem is not the standard. It is the medium. This is why the word "tidy" causes so many household arguments. It is a word, and words about how things should look are almost infinitely arguable, because we are trying to push an image down the narrow pipe of language, and each person rebuilds it a little differently at the far end.

And here is the lovely part. The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret. You are no longer comparing your idea of tidy to my idea of tidy. You are both just looking at the same photograph, and comparing the shelf to it. So here is the reframe to hold: a written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match.

And to put a clean definition on it, because it is worth being exact: a visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words. A photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART TWO: THE PICTURE OF THE IDEAL STATE

So let me give you the flagship tool, and it is almost comically simple, which is honestly part of why it works. You get a space exactly the way it should be. The pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk. On one of those rare days when it is genuinely right. And before it drifts, which it will, you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is. Taped inside the cupboard door, stuck to the wall beside the shelf. And from that moment, the standard for that space is not in anyone's head, and it is not in any document. It is on the door.

Now, you have actually done a version of this before, right at the very start. When you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was your visual baseline, the honest record of the mess. Well, the ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image. Not "this is what it looked like at its worst," but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." Same tool, pointed the other way.

And here is the reframe, because it is worth saying plainly: the most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph. The shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not. And notice how low this sets the bar for being useful. A child too young to read the word "tidy" can still match a shelf to a photo. That is the whole move: photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard, not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART THREE: THE STORYBOARD

Now, a photo of the ideal state is perfect for a space that should look a certain way. But some standards are not about how a thing looks at rest. They are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those, one photo is not enough. And the answer, when one photo is not enough, is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead of one.

That is a storyboard. Borrowed, without any ceremony, from the way films are planned. It is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order. How the folding table gets set up and stowed. How the cables coil and where they clip. How the recycling gets sorted on collection night. Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, and here is why: the reader is not converting words into actions in their head. They are just copying what they see, frame by frame.

So here is the reframe: for a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word. And please do not overthink this. You do not need to be a photographer or an artist. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

---

## THE TEACHING, PART FOUR: MAKE IT MATCH-ABLE

Now, there is a little craft to this, because a visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and getting it slightly wrong is exactly how good visual standards quietly stop being used.

The first thing is the angle and the light. Shoot the picture from the angle, and in the light, that the person will actually have when they go to set the space right. A photo of the pantry taken from up a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match. And subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. So get down, or up, to the real eye line. Use the ordinary light of the room. Capture the space the way it is actually seen.

The second thing is where you put it. Put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible. Because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms. Its entire value is being seen at the point of use.

And the third thing is honesty over polish. Keep it honest rather than impressive. A few slightly imperfect photos, taped up where they are actually needed, will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one. Which, by the way, is the classic mistake here, building the impressive binder instead of taping up the honest photo. The impressive one helps nobody. The honest one, at the point of use, does all the work.

So go and look at the friction meter, because this time it has reached a mark. The fifth one on the dial. The needle sits now at the Standardize-complete tick, right beside Sort complete, Straighten complete, Shine complete, and Safety complete. Five of the six S's, done.

But I want to be honest with you about that needle, because the tick forward was small. And that is exactly right, it is not a disappointment. Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way Sort and Straighten and Shine were. Its whole gift was different, and quieter, and you do not see it in the needle. You see it in the catch behind the needle. That small catch that first appeared when you began to standardize, and then grew stronger when you gave standards their tools, it is fully engaged now. Locked. Because everything you have built across twenty-five chapters is now written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures. Which means for the first time it is held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be. The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete. And the goal is a step away now, on the calm side, because there is one S still to come.

## LISTENER TAKEAWAY

So if you take one thing from this episode, take the rule, because it holds the whole chapter. Some things are easier to show than to say. When a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard. That is it. Where a written standard tells you what to do, a visual standard shows you what right looks like.

And here is your assignment, and it is genuinely a one-minute job, which is sort of the point. Photograph one space when it is exactly the way you want it. Print it small. And tape it up where the space is. That is it. And now "put it back the way it should be" stops being an instruction anyone has to remember or interpret. It is a picture to match. If you want the warm version, hand the camera to the kids. Let them take the ideal-state photos and put them up at their own height, because a child who took the picture of the tidy shelf is far more likely to put that shelf back the way the picture shows. The standard is theirs.

So that is the fifth S, complete. And it is worth pausing on for a second, because it changed the nature of what you have built. For the first six parts, you made your home good. Cleared, arranged, clean, and safe. Standardize did something different. It made that goodness durable. It took the goodness out of your head and your hands and your good days, and put it somewhere it can survive a tired week and a house full of other people. You did it in three moves: in Chapter 23 you learned what a standard is and wrote one down, in Chapter 24 you gave standards their three tools, and in this chapter you added the last kind, the standard a picture carries better than any words. With this, the fifth S is complete. Your home no longer runs on your memory. It runs on what you have made visible.

There is one S left. And it is the one that turns all of this from an achievement into a way of life. Because you have built a home that is good, and now, held. What you have not yet done is keep it, not for a proud month, but for years. That work, the sixth and final S, is Sustain, and it is the whole of the last part. That is the next chapter, Chapter 26, Sustain: Keeping the Gains, and it opens Part Eight. The full chapter is free in the online book. For now, though, hold on to the one idea to keep: when words fall short, show the result instead. Photograph a space at its best, or storyboard a task's steps, and put the picture where the work happens. Right stops being an argument and becomes something anyone can see and match.
