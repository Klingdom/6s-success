# Short-Form Scripts: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

**Five scripts for Reels, Shorts, and TikTok, each 30 to 90 seconds.**

## SHORT 1: Some things are easier to show than to say
**Topic: the edge where words strain, the problem is the medium, not the people. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Try to write down, in sentences, exactly what a tidy shelf looks like. You will pile up clauses and still miss it. That is not your fault. That is the medium.

**Script:**
You have standards written down now, and for most jobs that is plenty. But you have hit the edge: try to put into words exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, or how the cables coil, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you pile up clauses that still do not capture it. Some things simply resist description. And a written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end. Four people, four guesses. So here is the thing: the problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates. There is nothing left to interpret. You are both just looking at the same photo, and comparing the shelf to it.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "Write down what "tidy" looks like. Go on."
- 14s: "Some things simply resist description."
- 30s: "The problem is not the people. It is the medium."
- 44s: "Stop describing it. Just show it."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A careful "tidy" card read by four people, each picturing a subtly different shelf, then the four guesses collapsing into one shared photograph taped to the cupboard door. Warm daylight, no blame.

## SHORT 2: Show what right looks like
**Topic: the show-do-not-tell reframe and the definition of a visual standard. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything hard to put into words, that is everything.

**Script:**
There is a kind of standard you do not write, you show. It is called a visual standard, and here is the reframe. A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match. So what is it exactly? A visual standard is the ideal state of a space or a task, captured as a picture rather than described in words: a photo of what "done" looks like, or a short storyboard of the steps. You are not comparing your idea of tidy to mine anymore. We are both looking at the same picture. And the rule is simple: some things are easier to show than to say, and when a picture carries the standard better than words can, the picture is the standard.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "A written standard tells you what to do."
- 12s: "A visual standard shows you what right looks like."
- 28s: "A photo of what "done" looks like."
- 40s: "The picture is the standard."

**[VISUAL] direction:** Careful "tidy" sentences dissolving as a single photograph lands on the cupboard door, two people looking at the same picture together rather than at each other. Warm, human, slate photo frame.

## SHORT 3: Photograph the ideal state
**Topic: the flagship, photograph a space at its best and the photo becomes the standard. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The most powerful standard in your home is probably a single photo you have not taken yet.

**Script:**
Here is the simplest tool in the whole book, and it works because it is so simple. Get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right. And before it drifts, take a photograph. Then tape that photo where the space is, inside the cupboard door, beside the shelf. From that moment the standard is not in anyone's head. It is on the door. Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not. A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo. That is how low this sets the bar.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The most powerful standard in your home?"
- 14s: "A photo of the space on the day it was right."
- 30s: "Tape it where the space is."
- 44s: "The room either matches it or it does not."

**[VISUAL] direction:** A space caught exactly right, a phone taking the photo before it drifts, then the small slate-framed print taped to the door beside the matching real shelf, a "match this" note, a child matching the two. Warm, calm, honey accent on the note.

## SHORT 4: A short strip of pictures beats a paragraph
**Topic: the storyboard, show a task's steps as a few frames in order. Runtime ~45 seconds.**

**Hook line:** Some standards are not a look. They are a sequence. And nobody follows a paragraph.

**Script:**
A photo of the ideal state is perfect for a space that should look a certain way. But some standards are about a sequence, the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those, one photo is not enough, and a paragraph is the wrong tool. So use a few pictures instead of one. That is a storyboard, borrowed from the way films are planned: a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, in order. The entryway reset, three frames: shoes on the rack, bags on the hooks, surface clear. For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word, because they are not converting words into actions, they are just copying what they see. Four phone snaps of your own hands are plenty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "Some standards are a sequence, not a look."
- 12s: "A short strip of pictures beats a paragraph."
- 28s: "Show the few frames that matter, in order."
- 40s: "Four phone snaps are plenty."

**[VISUAL] direction:** The three-frame entryway-reset strip, shoes then bags then clear surface, arrows frame to frame, the next person copying what they see one frame at a time. Warm, human, slate frames, honey accent.

## SHORT 5: The needle reaches Standardize complete
**Topic: the milestone, the needle ticks a small honest step, the catch is fully engaged, five of six S's done. Runtime ~50 seconds.**

**Hook line:** The needle moved this time. Just a little. And that tiny tick is the whole point.

**Script:**
Go and look at the friction meter, because this time it has reached a mark, the fifth on the dial: Standardize complete, right beside Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety. Five of the six S's, done. But the tick forward was small, and that is honest. Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way Sort and Straighten and Shine were. Its whole gift was quieter, and you see it behind the needle, in the catch. That small catch that first appeared when you began to standardize, and grew stronger when standards got their tools, is fully engaged now, locked. Everything you have built is written down, carried by checklists and schedules and names, and, where words fell short, shown in pictures, which means for the first time it is held against the slide back about as firmly as a home can be. The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete. The goal is a step away, because one S remains: Sustain.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT] beats:**
- 0s: "The needle ticks forward. Just a little."
- 14s: "Standardize complete. Five of six S's done."
- 30s: "The catch behind it is fully engaged now."
- 44s: "The needle barely moved. The grip is complete."

**[VISUAL] direction:** The friction meter ticking a small step to the STANDARDIZE COMPLETE tick, the fifth milestone after Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety. The catch behind the needle drawn fully engaged and locked, the faint dashed backslide arrow toward FRICTION barred solidly. Passed markers behind, the GOAL crosshair, tagged "purpose", a step ahead on the green calm side.
