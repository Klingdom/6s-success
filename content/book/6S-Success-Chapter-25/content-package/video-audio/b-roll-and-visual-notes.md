- The starting point is the home from the end of Chapter 24: cleared, arranged, kept clean, made safe, and now with written standards that actually run, each carried by a checklist, a schedule, and a name. Those standards are real, and for most of the recurring work of a home they are plenty. But you have almost certainly hit the edge where words strain: try to write in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, and you pile up clauses that still do not quite capture it. Some things simply resist description. This chapter does NOT re-clean, re-tidy, or re-run any earlier S; it does NOT re-teach the concept of a standard (Chapter 23) or the three tools (Chapter 24); it does NOT re-teach the visual controls that make a storage spot self-explaining (Chapter 14, labels, outlines, boundaries, the Glance Test) or the matched-pair before-photo technique (Chapter 6); and it does NOT build the long-term adherence and audit system (Sustain, Chapters 26 to 30). It adds the last kind of standard, the one a picture carries better than any list.
- The honest problem, drawn plainly, not as blame. A written standard about how things should look is almost infinitely arguable, because everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, and slightly different, multiplied across a household, is how a shelf meant to be uniform ends up looking like four people's separate guesses. The point to draw: the problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium. Show the same tidy standard read by four people, each imagining a subtly different shelf, then the moment the words are replaced by one shared photograph and every guess collapses into a single picture everyone is looking at.
- The flagship is the photo of the ideal state, and it is almost comically simple, which is part of why it works. You get a space exactly the way it should be, the pantry, the coat closet, the play shelf, the desk, on one of those rare days when it is genuinely right, and before it drifts you take a photograph. Then you put that photograph where the space is, taped inside the cupboard door or stuck to the wall beside the shelf, and from that moment the standard for that space is not in anyone's head or in any document. It is on the door. This is the optimistic mirror image of the before photo you took at the very start: not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.
- The storyboard is for standards that are about a sequence, not a look. Some standards are not about how a thing sits at rest; they are about the steps of a task done in the right order, and for those one photo is not enough and a paragraph is the wrong tool. A storyboard, borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order: how the folding table gets set up and stowed, how the cables coil and where they clip, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night, the entryway reset. Three or four little pictures carry more, and carry it faster, than a whole numbered list, because the reader is not converting words into actions in their head; they are just copying what they see, frame by frame. Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.
- The craft is making it match-able, because getting it slightly wrong is how good visual standards quietly stop being used. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right; a photo of the pantry taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees it from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Get down or up to the real eye line, use the ordinary light of the room. Then put the standard where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible, because a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, its entire value being seen at the point of use. And keep it honest rather than impressive: a few slightly imperfect photos taped where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one.
- The friction meter reaches a MILESTONE, the fifth on the dial, STANDARDIZE COMPLETE. The live needle takes a small, honest step forward from where it held in Chapters 23 and 24, to a clearly marked slate tick labeled "STANDARDIZE COMPLETE", the fifth milestone after Sort complete, Straighten complete, Shine complete, and Safety complete. The forward tick is deliberately tiny, and that is honest: Standardize was never about clearing big new friction the way Sort, Straighten, and Shine were; its gift was quieter, and you see it behind the needle, in the catch. The anti-backslide catch that first appeared when you began to standardize and grew stronger when standards got their tools is now drawn fully engaged and locked, its firmest state, with the faint dashed backslide arrow toward the FRICTION side barred solidly. Chapter 24's held position is a small passed marker just behind the live needle; the Safety-complete, Shine-complete, Straighten-complete, and Sort-complete milestones sit further back as passed markers. The GOAL crosshair, tagged "purpose", sits just ahead on the green calm side, a step away, because one S remains, Sustain.
- Warm daylight, lived-in homes, real households. Not a styled catalog look. The ideal-state photo drawn as a small slate-framed print taped where the work happens, a honey accent on the "match this" note and the card titles, slate for the photo frames, the headings, and the data labels, green for the calm side of the meter. The tone is warm, human, and quietly proud, a Part close: five of six S's are done, and the home no longer runs on your memory, it runs on what you have made visible.
- Keep the whole thing warm, human, and anti-shame. A home whose standards live only in words, or only in one person's head, is the normal case, not a failing. No corporate or compliance framing, no force or control words (no enforce, comply, audit in the punitive sense, lock down), no factory-signage or management-dashboard feel, no hype, no brand names (say "the fridge", "a phone", not a brand, and no named cameras, apps, or label products). A visual standard is a kindness that ends arguments; it shares clarity, it is not a control imposed on the family. Standardize is complete here, and the goal is only a step away, because one S remains and Sustain follows.
