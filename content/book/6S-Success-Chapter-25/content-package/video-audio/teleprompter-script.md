# Teleprompter Script: Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

**Chapter 25, 6S Success: Home Edition**
**Slug:** pictures-storyboards-and-visual-standards
**Length:** about 760 words. Narration only. Short lines for easy reading.

---

[Read at a calm, even pace. Pause at each blank line.]

This is the third chapter of Standardize.

The one that completes the fifth S.

You already have standards in words.

The best way you know, written down.

And you gave them their three tools.

A checklist, a schedule, and a name.

For most of the work of a home, that is plenty.

But you have hit the edge by now.

The place where words start to strain.

Try to write down what a tidy shelf looks like.

You pile up clauses, and still miss it.

Some things simply resist description.

How the cables coil. What a reset entryway looks like.

For all of these, a paragraph is the wrong tool.

And a far better one is in your pocket.

Here is why a written standard fails here.

Everyone reads the same careful sentences.

And everyone pictures something slightly different.

Four people, four separate guesses.

The problem is not the people and not the standard.

It is the medium.

Words about how things look are almost infinitely arguable.

We push an image down the narrow pipe of language.

And each person rebuilds it a little differently.

But the moment you stop describing it and simply show it.

The argument evaporates.

There is nothing left to interpret.

So here is the reframe.

A written standard tells you what to do.

A visual standard shows you what right looks like.

Now the flagship tool. It is almost comically simple.

Get a space exactly the way it should be.

On one of those rare days when it is genuinely right.

And before it drifts, take a photograph.

Then put that photo where the space is.

Taped inside the cupboard door.

From that moment the standard is not in anyone's head.

It is on the door.

Photograph a space when it is exactly right.

And that photo becomes the standard.

Not a description of the ideal state.

A picture of it, that anyone can match at a glance.

From then on, tidy is not an opinion.

The room either matches the picture or it does not.

A child too young to read "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

Now, some standards are a sequence, not a look.

The steps of a task, in the right order.

For those, one photo is not enough.

But the answer is not a paragraph.

It is a few pictures instead of one.

That is a storyboard.

A short strip of simple frames, each one key step, in order.

The entryway reset. Shoes. Bags. Clear surface.

For a task with steps, a short strip beats a paragraph.

The next person copies what they see, frame by frame.

Four phone snaps of your own hands are plenty.

The value is the sequence being visible.

Not the pictures being beautiful.

And there is a little craft to it.

Shoot from the angle and light of use.

A photo from a stepladder is subtly hard to match.

And subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned.

Put the standard where the work happens.

A visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms.

Keep it honest, not laminated.

A few taped photos beat a beautiful binder in a drawer.

Now go and look at the friction meter.

This time it has reached a mark. The fifth on the dial.

Standardize complete. Beside Sort, Straighten, Shine, and Safety.

The tick forward was small. And that is honest.

Standardize was never about clearing new friction.

Its gift was quieter. You see it in the catch.

The catch behind the needle is fully engaged now. Locked.

Everything is written down, carried by tools, shown in pictures.

Held against the slide back as firmly as a home can be.

The needle barely moved. And the grip behind it is complete.

So here is your job. It takes a minute.

Photograph one space when it is exactly right.

Print it small, and tape it up where the space is.

Now "put it back the way it should be" is a picture to match.

Five of the six S's are done.

The best way you know is written down.

Carried by a checklist and a schedule and a name.

And, where words fall short, shown in a picture.

Your home no longer runs on your memory.

It runs on what you have made visible.
