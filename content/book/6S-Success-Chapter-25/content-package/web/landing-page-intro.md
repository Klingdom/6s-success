# Landing Page Intro: Chapter 25, Pictures, Storyboards, and Visual Standards

For the last two chapters you have been building standards in words. Chapter 23 gave you the concept, the best way you know today, written down where anyone can follow it, a recipe and not a rule. Chapter 24 gave that standard its three tools, a checklist so nothing is forgotten, a schedule so the work has a time, and a name so someone owns it, and turned a hopeful card on the fridge into a small machine that runs on a tired Tuesday. For most of the recurring work of a home, that is enough. But if you have tried it, you may already have found the edge where words run out.

Try to write down exactly what a tidy shelf looks like. You start well, then you are three clauses deep, adding "spines aligned," "tallest on the left," "nothing in front of anything else," and you can feel it slipping, because the sentence still is not the shelf. How the cables should be coiled, where each thing sits when the room is right, what "done" actually looks like: some things simply resist description. And here is the thing worth seeing clearly, because it changes who you blame. When a written standard about how things should look gets ignored or argued over, it is tempting to decide the family is careless or the standard is bad. It is usually neither. Everyone reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end, so the standard is almost infinitely arguable. The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium.

So this chapter adds the last kind of standard, the one a written sentence was never going to carry: the visual standard, the standard you show instead of tell. A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. The moment you stop describing the image and simply show it, the argument evaporates, because there is nothing left to interpret. It comes in two plain forms, and neither needs any skill you do not already have.

The first, and the flagship, is the photo of the ideal state. Get a space right on one of those rare days when it is exactly the way you want it, and take a photograph before it drifts. That photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance. Put it where the space is, taped to the cupboard door, and from then on tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture, and the room either matches it or it does not. You have done a version of this before, at the very start, when you took a before photo of a space you were about to fix. That was the honest record of the mess; the ideal-state photo is its optimistic mirror image, not "this is what it looked like at its worst" but "this is what it looks like at its best, and this is what we are keeping it like." And it sets the bar for being useful about as low as a tool can: a child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo.

The second form is the storyboard, for a task that has steps. Borrowed without ceremony from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, laid out in order. For setting up the folding table, coiling the cables, or sorting the recycling, three or four little pictures carry more, and faster, than a numbered paragraph, because the next person copies what they see instead of converting words into actions. Four phone snaps or stick-figure sketches are plenty. The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful.

Whichever form you use, the craft is the same: make it genuinely easy to match. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they set the space right, because a photo taken from a stepladder when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned. Then put it where the work happens. A visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use. A few slightly imperfect photos taped exactly where they are needed will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one. (And keep it distinct from a visual control: a label, an outline, or a boundary makes a storage spot self-explaining; a visual standard shows what "done right" looks like. A label is a tiny visual standard, and the ideal-state photo is the full one.)
