## Post 1 · Some things are easier to show than to say

You have written the standard down and given it a checklist, a schedule, and a name. For most of a home's work, that is enough. But you have probably hit the edge of it, the place where words start to strain.

"Some things simply resist description."

Try to write down in sentences exactly what a tidy shelf looks like, how the cables coil, or where each thing sits when the room is right, and you pile up clauses that still miss it. And when a "how it should look" standard gets ignored, it is usually nobody's fault.

"The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium."

The fix is to stop describing the picture and simply show it. This is the start of Chapter 25 of 6S Success: Home Edition. Read it free in the online book. 🙂

## Post 2 · Photograph the shelf on the day it was right

Here is the flagship tool, and it is almost comically simple. Get a space exactly right, the pantry, the play shelf, the coat closet, on one of those rare good days, and before it drifts, take a photo. Then tape the photo where the space is.

"Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance."

From then on, "tidy" stops being an opinion anyone can argue with.

"The most powerful standard in a home is often a single photograph: the shelf, the counter, the entryway, captured on the day it was exactly right. From then on, tidy is not an opinion and not an argument. It is a picture on the cupboard door, and the room either matches it or it does not."

A child too young to read the word "tidy" can match a shelf to a photo. More in Chapter 25 of 6S Success: Home Edition, free to read online.

## Post 3 · For a task with steps, show the pictures

A photo is perfect for how a space should look. But some standards are a sequence, the steps of a task in the right order, and for those one photo is not enough. The answer is not to fall back on a paragraph. It is to use a few pictures instead.

"For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word."

Borrowed from the way films are planned, a storyboard is a short strip of simple frames, each showing one key step, in order: how the folding table gets set up, how the cables coil, how the recycling gets sorted on collection night. You do not need to be a photographer. Four phone snaps of your own hands, or four quick stick-figure sketches, are plenty.

"The value is entirely in the sequence being visible, not in the pictures being beautiful."

## Post 4 · Tape it where the work happens, and keep it honest

A visual standard only works if it is genuinely easy to match, and there is a little craft to that. Shoot the picture from the angle and in the light the person will actually have when they go to set the space right. A photo taken from a stepladder, when everyone else sees the pantry from standing height, is subtly hard to match, and subtly hard to match means quietly abandoned.

Then put it where the work happens, not in a folder somewhere sensible.

"a visual standard filed away is a contradiction in terms, because its entire value is being seen at the point of use."

And keep it honest rather than impressive. A few slightly imperfect photos, taped where they are needed, will do more for your home than a beautiful laminated binder that lives in a drawer and helps no one. More in Chapter 25 of 6S Success: Home Edition, free to read online.

"The needle barely moved, and the grip behind it is complete."
