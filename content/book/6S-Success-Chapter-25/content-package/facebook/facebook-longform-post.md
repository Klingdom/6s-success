# Facebook Longform Post: I Wrote Down What "Tidy" Means. We Still Argued About It.

A longer, story-driven post from Chapter 25 of 6S Success: Home Edition, the third and final chapter of Part Seven and the one that completes Standardize, the fifth S. It picks up where Chapters 23 and 24 left off (you wrote a standard and gave it a checklist, a schedule, and a name), names the honest edge (some things are far easier to show than to say, and words about how a thing should look are almost infinitely arguable, so the problem is the medium, not the people), and delivers the hero: the visual standard, the photo of the ideal state and the short storyboard for a task's steps. Warm and conversational, like talking to a friend. The photo is a kindness that ends arguments, never corporate. Ends with the completion note and a soft CTA. No em dashes. Body hard-capped 300 to 450 words.

---

For a while I kept trying to write down what "tidy" meant on our kitchen shelves. I got as far as a card taped inside the cupboard: mugs on the left, handles out, tallest at the back. Everyone read it, and everyone still built a slightly different shelf. For a long time I thought that was people not paying attention.

That is not the honest gap this chapter names.

"The problem is not the people and not the standard. It is the medium."

The words were fine. The trouble is that words about how a thing should look are almost infinitely arguable, because each of us reads the same careful sentences and rebuilds a slightly different picture at the other end. "Tidy" is a word, and we were all quietly translating it into our own image.

So the chapter hands you the tool that ends the translating, and it is already in your pocket.

"A written standard tells you what to do. A visual standard shows you what right looks like. For anything you would struggle to put into words, that is the difference between a standard people argue about and one they can simply see and match."

Here is what I did, and it cost about a minute. On a rare day when the shelf was exactly right, I photographed it and taped the photo inside the cupboard door.

"Photograph a space when it is exactly right, and that photo becomes the standard: not a description of the ideal state, but a picture of it, that anyone can match their reality against at a glance."

The arguing stopped. There is nothing left to interpret. You look at the picture, look at the shelf, and match the two. Even our youngest, too young to read the word, can do it.

For jobs that are a sequence rather than a look, the book gives you a storyboard: a few pictures instead of a paragraph.

"For a task with steps, a short strip of pictures beats a paragraph. Show the few frames that matter, in order, and the next person can follow the pictures without reading a word."

Four phone snaps of your own hands doing the steps are plenty. The value is the sequence being visible, not the pictures being beautiful.

And with that small move, the fifth S is quietly complete. Your home stops running on your memory and starts running on what you have made visible.
