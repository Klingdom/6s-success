Pilot · Production Scripts

The Entryway Pilot

Five shootable episodes, to prove the format before we build 114.

Full production scripts for the five Entryway zones: verbatim voiceover, a synced shot sequence, on-screen text, and the six Short cuts per episode. Everything is headless, no presenter on camera. Shoot these five, watch how they land, and adjust the format here before scaling to the rest of the house.

How to read a script

  • Every episode is the fixed cold open, then six segments in order, then the close.
  • The voiceover boxes are the exact words to record. Timing estimates are targets, not measured, so let the edit breathe.
  • The shot table syncs each setup to the line of VO it plays under.
  • The green box in each segment is its Short cut: the extra intro line that lets it stand alone, and the end card back to the full episode.
What the pilot is testing

Does headless carry a full reset on its own. Is sixty seconds the right opener. Does a single segment really stand alone as a Short. Five episodes answer all three cheaply.

Beat 1 of the cold open is written per zone and lives in each episode below. Beats 2 and 3 are the same on all 114 episodes and are recorded once as the asset below. Only the final line of beat 3 changes, where the zone name and session length are swapped in.

Beat 2 What 6S is~20s
Voiceover

6S is six steps, always in the same order. Sort, keep what earns its place and route out the rest. Straighten, give what stays a home you can reach. Shine, clean closely enough to catch the small problems early. Safety, the fourth step, make the space safe before you make it pretty. Standardize, set the state you want so anyone can find it again. And Sustain, keep it with a habit rather than a burst of willpower.

MZ-001.R01.01

Landing Zone

7–9 min · thumbnail: “Act, file, or recycle”

Title: Entryway Landing Zone Reset: Keys, Mail, and the Paper With No Verdict

Shoot notesShoot on a bright overcast day with the front door open for fill and a single white foam board propped against the wall opposite the door as the only bounce. One-person shoot: tripod, a clamp arm for overheads clamped to the door frame or hall architrave since there is rarely floor space for full tripod legs, and window light only. Anything that moves through the room is a locked-off frame the hands and objects pass through, never a follow. Work at bench height for three-quarter angles, and keep everything above the wrists out of every frame. Grit here is abrasive: lay a moving blanket under the tripod feet and keep the lens capped between takes. Tape a floor mark for where you stand so you stay outside the overhead's edge. Safety staging: place the umbrella drip line with a spray bottle rather than genuinely soaking a surface near a live outlet, and stage the chained power strip unplugged, plugging in only for the single beat where it matters. For the Standardize overhead, check the phone screen and any glossy tray finish for reflections of the ceiling before rolling, and angle the bounce to kill hot spots on the wiped surface in Shine.
Cold open Beat 1 · the problem~20s
Voiceover

Watch this surface for a week and you will see the same few sheets of paper slide from one end to the other and back again. A form to fill in. A phone call to make. A payment to authorise. The keys were never the problem here. The paper with no verdict is.

Sort Sort~85s
Voiceover

Start by tipping the whole thing onto the floor. The tray, the keys, and every sheet of paper stacked on the surface. For the first time the real volume is sitting out where you can see it, and it is more than you thought. Now pull out the things that decide nothing and hold a spot anyway. Keys that open nothing. Loyalty cards. Dead batteries. Last month's flyers. Those leave first, because they were never waiting on a decision, they were just sitting there. Then take the paper one piece at a time. Every sheet gets one of three verdicts, and there are only three: act, file, or recycle. Nothing comes back onto this surface undecided. The sheets that need you to act go upright into a folder, and you write the date in the corner as they go. That date is the clock that will make the decision for you later.

Shine Shine~45s
Voiceover

You clean this surface to read it, not to impress anyone. Lift the tray straight out and set it aside. Underneath it is a ring, a grey film that keys and coins shed over time. Reading it is the whole point. That is what cleaning to inspect means on a landing surface.

Safety Safety~60s
Voiceover

Three hazards come off this one surface, and all three are easy to see once you look. First, water and electricity. Wet umbrellas and rain-soaked sleeves drip onto whatever sits directly below the hooks. Trace that drip line, and if the charging dock or a power strip is under it, move it out. You keep water and electricity apart by distance, not by being careful. Second, the overloaded outlet. Count what else is plugged into the outlet that feeds the dock. A lamp, a seasonal heater, and a four-port charger chained onto one strip is where a fire starts here. Unchain it. One dock, one outlet, nothing else. Third, the floor. That work bag parked beside the tray is the thing you go over in the dark with your arms full of shopping. Lift it off the floor.

Sustain Sustain~55s
Voiceover

The folder empties after dinner. As you sit down for the evening, it comes with you, and you work it one sheet at a time, not in a batch. You will watch it thin out as you go. When a sheet passes fourteen days in that folder, it stops being paperwork and becomes a decision. You do it tonight, or you accept honestly that you never will and you recycle it. Both of those are answers. Leaving it on the surface is not.

MZ-002 . R01.02

Coat and Outerwear Zone

9–12 min · thumbnail: “One coat per weather”

Title: Entryway Coat Rail Reset: Count Coats by Weather, Not by Number

Shoot notesSmall, high-traffic, dark room, so shoot on a bright overcast day with the front door open for fill and a white foam board propped against the opposite wall as the only bounce. One-person shoot: tripod, a clamp arm for overheads, window light, no second operator, so anything that moves through the room is a locked-off frame the hands and coats pass through, never a follow. Work at bench height for three-quarter angles and clamp the overhead arm to the door frame or hall architrave, since there is rarely floor space for a full tripod spread. Grit here is abrasive, so lay a moving blanket under the tripod feet and keep the lens capped between takes. Nothing above the wrists appears in any frame, so tape a floor mark for where the operator stands to stay outside the overhead's edge. Safety segment staging: pre-loosen or pre-score a plasterboard offcut for the hook pull-out so nothing real fails unpredictably on set, and stage the anchored hook alongside as the fix. For the weight test, use a hook you have already proven, and pull toward the wall, not out into the lens. Shine segment: pre-check the umbrella stand actually has water and grit in it before rolling, and shoot the tip-out outside over a tray so no water goes near the rail. Reflection check on the macro passes, keep the operator and the foam board out of the bin face and any glossy hook chrome.
Cold open Beat 1 · the problem~20s
Voiceover

Somewhere on this rail is one good coat. The one that cost real money, and goes outside almost never. Yet it survives every cull, while the cheaper coats you actually wear come down without a thought. This rail is not full of coats you need. It is full of coats you keep for reasons that have nothing to do with the weather, and the expensive one is the hardest to let go.

Then the fixed opener (beats 2 and 3) plays, with this episode’s zone name and session length swapped into its closing line.

Sort Sort~100s
Voiceover

Take every coat down. All of them, onto the floor, so the rail hangs empty and you can see the real number for the first time. Now hold each coat by the shoulders and ask it one question. Did this go outdoors, even once, through the last cold season? If it did not, it leaves now. Here is the rule that makes every other choice easy. You count coats by weather, not by number. Each person gets one coat for each kind of weather your climate genuinely produces. Wet, cold, and something in between. A second coat in the same category has to beat the first one on a specific day you can name. If you cannot name that day, that is the coat that leaves. The expensive one is not exempt. The money is already spent, whether it hangs here or keeps someone else warm. So give it one trial. Front hook, the most reachable spot, two weeks. If you did not reach for it, you have your answer, and you had it before you started. The single gloves you kept in case the other one ever turned up, let them go. And off-season coats go to a bedroom closet, not the far end of this same rail. Moving a problem three feet along the wall is not solving it.

Straighten Straighten~72s
Voiceover

Now split the pile into two. Anything that goes on and off every day belongs on a hook. The wool coat that creases belongs on a hanger, and almost nothing else does. Set your hooks by the shortest person who uses this rail, not by yourself. Hold that person's own coat flat to the wall and mark the wall at its shoulder. That mark is your lowest hook. You measured it off the coat that will actually hang there, instead of guessing. Leave the two hooks nearest the door empty, on purpose, for visitors. Those two empty hooks are going to become the way you read this whole rail later. Hats and gloves go together into one bin on the shelf above, and umbrellas go into the stand, not looped over a hook.

Safety Safety~88s
Voiceover

A loaded coat rail hurts somebody in three ways, so deal with all three now, while it is empty. First, a fall. A soaked winter coat is far heavier than the dry one you casually hang, so hang your own full weight on each hook before you trust it with one. A hook driven into plasterboard without an anchor pulls out later, with the coat still on it and a lump of wall behind it. Fit the anchor now, not after it lets go. Second, a crush. Anything heavy stored above head height on the shelf lands on whoever tugs the coat underneath it. Bring the boots and the holdalls down low, and send the light bulky things up high. Weight low, bulk high. Third, and this is the one that is easy to miss, a strangle. A long scarf or a bag strap left on a low hook hangs as a loop at a small child's neck height. Coats stay on the low hooks. Straps and scarves go up high.

Standardize Standardize~60s
Voiceover

Standardize means making the right state obvious at a glance, so nobody has to hold it in their head. Write the contents and the season on the face of the shelf bin. Hats and gloves. Winter. Write it big enough to read while you are standing on the floor, looking up at it, because that is exactly where you will be. Then test the label from that same spot. Reach up the way you will in October, when the cold finally arrives. You want to know before the lid ever comes off whether this is the right bin. If you can read it from down there, it works. A label like this is doing the same job as a taped outline on the floor. It is a visual control, so the answer lives on the bin instead of in one person's memory.

Sustain Sustain~66s

They are your gauge. If every hook is full, one coat is in the wrong room, and it is time to walk it back. An empty hook is not wasted space. It is the rail quietly telling you it is still working.

Straighten Straighten~32s
Voiceover

You want two mats here, and only two. A coarse one outside the door to scrape, and an absorbent one inside to dry. Those are two different jobs, so give them two different mats. Now test the inside one. Walk across it at your normal pace, the way you really come in, and watch your feet. On most mats the second foot lands past the end, straight onto bare floor. That is the whole problem. A mat too short for one stride is exactly how the grit gets past it, so the length is what matters here, not the pattern printed on it. And if there is a third mat, take it out. A third mat just adds one more edge to catch a toe.

Safety Safety~42s
Voiceover

Two things at this threshold can put someone on the floor, and one more can catch a hand. First, the curled corner. You step in with your arms full and your eyes down, and the toe of your shoe finds the edge that has lifted. So grip the edges flat, with tape or a strip of grip backing, then press the corner down and test it by hand. Second, a mat that slides on hard floor. Push it sideways. If it moves, it is a hazard even lying perfectly flat, and the same backing holds it still. And while you are already down there with the cloth, tighten the screws on the door hinge. A loose hinge or a tired closer swings the door harder than you expect, and that is how it catches a finger against the frame. Find it, and fix it now.

Standardize Standardize~28s
Voiceover

Right now you are the only person who knows where the mat is meant to sit. Fix that with tape. The offset is obvious now, to anyone walking in, not just to you. That is the difference between a rule that lives in your head and a boundary anyone can read at a glance. Sustain Sustain~30s
Voiceover

Here is how it holds without anyone having to remember. The mat sits directly on the route between the last room you vacuum and the cupboard where the vacuum lives.
