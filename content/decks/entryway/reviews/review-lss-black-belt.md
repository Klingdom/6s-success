# Entryway Deck v2 — Lean Six Sigma Review

*Reviewer: Master Black Belt (6S/5S, standard work, poka-yoke, 8 wastes, visual management). Canon respected: Sort · Straighten · Shine · **Safety (4th S)** · Standardize · Sustain.*

This is a genuinely strong deck. The Shine cards are real standard work (ordered steps, matched inputs, inspect-as-you-clean), the sequencing is correct, and Safety-after-Shine is exactly right. Card 6's "the empty hook does the asking" is a textbook poka-yoke and the best single idea in the deck. What follows is the value it is still leaving on the table.

---

## Deck-wide assessment: top 5 systemic improvements

**1. Give the deck one small, countable scoreboard — it is almost entirely qualitative.**
The deck says "clean," "clear," "calm" everywhere but rarely gives a number you can check in a glance. Adopt three house metrics and repeat them on the same cards throughout:
- **Seconds to launch** (already implied on Card 5 as "under a minute" — name it and make it the headline number).
- **Items on the surface, target ≤ 3** (Card 34 already uses this — promote it to the deck-wide surface rule).
- **Empty hooks, target 2** (Card 6 already implies it — make it a counted state, not a feeling).
These three convert the Glance Test from an opinion into a pass/fail. Put them on Card 5, capture them on Cards 7 and 10, and watch the first one daily on Card 11.

**2. Name poka-yoke as a principle and thread it through every zone.** The deck leans hard on habit ("bolt the upkeep to a moment that already happens"). Habit is Sustain's backstop, not its engine. Poka-yoke makes the wrong state physically impossible or instantly visible so no one has to remember. The deck already does this in three places (empty hook asks, lowest hook at shortest height, mat test) but doesn't call it out or apply it consistently. The single biggest miss: the **taped floor/mat outline** — it is in the source manuscript ("an absorbent mat lying dead flat inside a thin taped outline," "tape the outline where the mat sits") but was dropped from the Straighten/Standardize cards for Zone 5. Add it back; it is the cleanest visual poka-yoke in the whole room.

**3. Add the 8 wastes as a diagnostic lens — the entryway is a waste factory and the deck never says so.** Hunting for keys is **motion + waiting**, and it is the number-one morning loss the room exists to kill. The never-worn coat is **inventory**. Re-doing a rushed job is a **defect**. Piling instead of deciding is **over-processing**. Naming these on the purpose and audit cards tells the user *why* each rule pays. DOWNTIME (Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-used talent, Transport, Inventory, Motion, Extra-processing) is worth one line on Card 5 or 8.

**4. Add 5-Why prompts for the three recurring failures — the deck fixes symptoms, never causes.** "Re-hang strays," "empty the folder," "coat off, not over a chair" all treat the recurrence as a discipline problem. Nine times in ten it is a **design** problem: the coat lands on the chair because the hook is two steps past where hands are full. Give the user one 5-Why prompt per chronic failure (pile on the console, coat on the chair, shoes in the swing) so they fix the cause, not the person.

**5. Close the control loop — an audit with no cadence and no trigger is not a control.** Card 8 scores the room but nothing says when to re-score, and Card 10's after-photo is a standard that nothing compares against. Define the control: the **daily leading indicator** (items on the surface) catches drift early; the **glance-test-vs-photo** is the weekly check; the audit is monthly. State the trigger — "when the glance test fails, run the lowest S" — so the system self-corrects instead of waiting for a full reset.

## Card-by-card value-adds

**Card 4 · Room Kit.** This is standard-work inventory, so protect it like one: tape a photo of the fully-packed caddy to the caddy itself (or list its contents on the lid). A missing cloth or empty bottle then shows *before* you start, not mid-clean when walking off to fetch it becomes waiting + motion waste and breaks the flow. Add one line: "Keep a spare of each consumable in the caddy so running out never stops the job."

**Card 5 · Primary Purpose.** Make this the metric card. Rename the test's number: **"Seconds to launch: can anyone leave in under 60 with zero hunting?"** Hunting is the waste the whole room fights (motion + waiting). Add the two countable states it depends on — surface ≤ 3 items, 2 hooks empty — so "land and launch in seconds" becomes three numbers, not a mood.

**Card 6 · Family Values.** Excellent as is — just label the mechanism so it transfers to other rooms: *"the empty hook is a poka-yoke: it makes 'no room for a stray' visible so the room asks, and nobody has to nag."* Naming it teaches the reader to build the same trick elsewhere.

**Card 7 · Before Photos.** Capture the **baseline numbers**, not just the image: count items on the console, time one real launch, count empty hooks, write all three on the photo. Without a before number the after "improvement" is a feeling. A measured before is what makes the after undeniable.

**Card 8 · 6S Audit.** Two additions. (a) Anchor the soft scores to countable checks so the audit is repeatable across people: *Sort = surface ≤ 3? · Straighten = 2 hooks empty? · Shine = no grit on a white-cloth wipe? · Sustain = did last night's reset happen?* (b) Add one waste-lens question: *"Where did someone hunt, wait, or redo this week?"* — that finds the systemic problem the S-scores miss. And state the cadence: **re-audit monthly**; between audits the glance test governs.

**Card 9 · Safety Blitz.** Push each fix from "correct it today" to "make it can't-happen." A drip tray or relocating the power strip permanently out of the drip line is a poka-yoke; wiping the drip up is a chore you repeat. Add: *"Prefer the fix that makes the hazard impossible over the one you have to redo."*

**Card 10 · Signature / After.** Write the **after numbers** beside the after photo (seconds to launch, items on surface, empty hooks). The photo shows *what right looks like*; the numbers prove *how far it moved* and give the weekly glance test something objective to match.

**Card 11 · Keeping Rhythm.** This is takt done well. Close the loop: name the **one leading indicator to watch daily — items on the surface** — because it drifts first and warns you before the weekly and seasonal clocks would. Add the trigger: *"When a glance shows more than three on the top or no empty hook, that's the signal to reset now, not next week."* A leading indicator caught early is worth ten scheduled deep-cleans.

**Card 13 · Landing Spot · Sort.** The 14-day rule is a good quantified standard. Add a physical **WIP cap that self-polices**: one folder, and *it must close*. If it won't close, that is the visible signal to run a verdict pass — the folder poka-yokes its own overflow. Then a 5-Why seed for the chronic pile: *"Why does paper land here? → no verdict at the door → no bin/recycle within reach → put a recycle slot at the drop point."* Fix the path, not the pile.

**Card 14 · Landing Spot · Straighten.** Add point-of-use to kill motion: the key hook and tray belong exactly where your hand already falls as the door closes, so keys land in **one motion** with no reach or turn. A home that is one step out of the natural path is why keys end up loose on the top. "A home chosen by use" should explicitly mean *chosen by the shortest, most natural motion.*

**Card 17 · Landing Spot · Standardize.** Strengthen the visual standard so it's checkable without the photo: **tape a thin outline for the tray and mark the "max 3"** on the surface. A returned item now has an outline to match (poka-yoke), and anyone can see an over-three top at a glance instead of comparing to a photo taped inside a cupboard.

**Card 21 · Coats · Straighten.** Good ergonomic poka-yoke already (lowest hook at shortest height = can't fail to reach). Make the empty-hook rule countable: *"Two hooks stay empty — if fewer than two are free, the room is over capacity and it's a Sort signal."* The empty hooks are a live WIP gauge, not just a courtesy for visitors.

**Card 25 · Coats · Sustain.** Replace the willpower framing with a cause hunt. Add the deck's clearest 5-Why: *"Coat on the chair again? Ask why five times. Usually the answer is 'the hook is past where my hands are full' — so move a hook to the exact spot the coat lands. Behavior problems at the door are almost always layout problems."* This is the single highest-leverage teaching addition in the deck.

**Card 26 · Shoes · Zone.** Name the principle the boot tray embodies: **control at the source.** The entryway is itself a poka-yoke for the whole house — catch grit and salt at the door and every downstream floor stays clean. That reframing tells the user why this zone earns disproportionate effort ("the salt and grit is exactly what scratches every floor in the house" is the *what*; "catch defects at the source" is the *why*).

**Card 28 · Shoes · Straighten.** Add a visible **capacity limit**: the rack holds N pairs, one slot per pair, and a pair with no slot is the Sort trigger. Overflow onto the floor isn't a tidiness lapse, it's the rack telling you it's over WIP. Make the limit visible (named slots already help) so the signal can't be ignored.

**Card 34 · Bench/Console · Sort.** "Top to three items or fewer" is the deck's best countable standard — promote it. Flag in the text that **≤ 3 is the surface rule for the whole room** (landing spot, console, everywhere a flat top invites a pile), so one number governs every horizontal surface and the glance test is identical in every zone.

**Card 39 · Bench/Console · Sustain.** Name the nightly reset for what it is — a **reset to standard** — and give it the number: *"back to three or fewer."* A reset with a count is checkable; "reset the top" is not.

**Card 42 · Door/Mat/Floor · Straighten.** Restore the dropped poka-yoke from the source: **tape a thin outline where the mat sits and mark the clear stride inside the door swing.** A curled or shoved mat, or anything creeping into the swing, then shows instantly against the tape — the floor marking does the noticing for you. This is the most conspicuous omission versus the manuscript and the cleanest 5S floor-marking example in the room.

**Card 44 · Door/Mat/Floor · Safety.** The mat test is a proper wear-out inspection standard — sharpen it into a dated one: **write the install date on the mat's underside.** Then "replace it this month" becomes a fact, not a guess, and you stop relying on a mat that "looks fine" (a classic hidden defect). Good place to note that a mat is a consumable with a life, not furniture.

## Highest-impact recommendations (summary)

1. Adopt three tiny house metrics and repeat them across the deck: **seconds to launch, items on the surface (≤ 3), empty hooks (= 2)** — captured as a before/after pair (Cards 5, 7, 10) and watched daily (Card 11). This turns the Glance Test from opinion into pass/fail.
2. Restore the **taped mat outline and floor-swing marking** on Cards 42/45 — it's in the source manuscript, was dropped, and is the deck's cleanest visual poka-yoke.
3. Add a **5-Why prompt on Card 25** (coat on the chair) and Card 13 (paper pile): fix the layout cause, not the person — the deck's biggest teaching gap.
4. Name **poka-yoke** and **control-at-the-source** explicitly (Cards 6, 26) and prefer can't-happen fixes over redo-it fixes on the Safety cards (9, 16).
5. Close the control loop: give Card 8 countable score anchors, a monthly cadence, and a "when the glance test fails, run the lowest S" trigger, plus one 8-wastes question (where did someone hunt, wait, or redo?).
